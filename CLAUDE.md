# Writing style for Ars Grammatica (Timothy Kearns)

Purpose: every panel, question, option, and explanation in this course should read like a good standard grammar textbook, in plain expository prose that uses the standard terms.

Read this file before you write or revise any student-facing text. Change wording only. Do not change doctrine, citations, Latin or Greek, or which answer is correct.

## The main problem to avoid

AI-written text tends to coin its own phrasing in place of the standard term, for example "a word wrongly sounded" for "a mispronounced word" (in the classical sense, a barbarism), or "the complete saying" for "the sentence." This happens even when a style guide is in place. A coined phrase is wrong even when it is understandable. The student must learn the term that every grammar uses.

## Core rules

1. **Use the standard textbook term every time, even if it repeats.** Never paraphrase a technical term or coin a substitute for it, and never vary it for elegance. If the term is "sentence," write "sentence" five times. If a term needs explaining, gloss it in parentheses the first time it appears: "the base (the main line)."
2. **No calques from Latin or Greek.** Do not translate a Latin or Greek term word for word into English when English has its own established term. Write "sentence," not "complete saying" (*oratio perfecta*); "construction," not "joining" (*constructio*); "art," not "road." If no English term exists, use the Latin term in italics and gloss it.
3. **Plain declarative sentences.** State the claim. Make the logical link explicit with "so," "but," "because," "therefore," "that is." Write full sentences, not fragments or "Label: fragment" answers. Do not write short sentences for effect, aphoristic closers, or metaphors that stand in for a claim (fog, hinge, seed, payoff, detour).
4. **No AI tics.** Do not write "it's worth noting," "it is important to note," "delve," "crucially," "not just X but Y," reflexive lists of three (tricolons), rhetorical questions used for effect, "journey," "rich tapestry," "here is the point," "this is the heart of it," "notice that," or dashes used as flourishes. Use few dashes; use a comma, parentheses, or a semicolon. A question is fine only when it is the question the paragraph answers, stated plainly and then answered.
5. **Address.** Use "we" or let the tradition make the claim. Do not coach the student with "you" and imperatives. Do not comment on the page, the app, or the scoring. In student-facing text, say "course" or "set of exercises," not "app."
6. **American spelling:** center, meter, color, license, honor, plow, traveler. Inside a quotation, keep the spelling of the source exactly.
7. **Answer feedback uses "Correct" and "Incorrect"** (not "Right," "Not quite," "Wrong," "Good," and so on), followed by the explanation in full sentences.
8. **Mentioned words.** An English word mentioned as a word takes double quotation marks: the word "thing." A foreign word mentioned as a word takes italics: Latin *res*, Greek *logos*. Example sentences in a foreign language are also italic; English example sentences take double quotation marks.
9. **Translations must be real.** A translation of a Latin, Greek, or other source must be a real published translation, or else Timothy's own. Record the translator in `sources/TRANSLATIONS-USED.md`, not in the app's text (see "Translators" below). Never write a translation and present it as published. Never alter a quotation, even by one letter. If you cannot verify a translation, mark it for Timothy.
10. **Prefer accuracy to a neat claim.** Add the qualification that makes the sentence true ("usually begins with a capital letter").
11. **The test.** Before keeping a sentence, ask: would this sentence, or its terms, appear in Allen and Greenough's *Latin Grammar* or in a standard English grammar? If not, rewrite it in the terms those books use.

### 1. Introduction to the Trivium (index.html)

> This interactive overview is for a motivated student or an interested adult who wants to understand the foundations of the classical trivium (which is the coordinated studies of grammar, logic, and rhetoric), in particular why the classical tradition begins all inquiry from words and takes words as its fundamental instrument.
>
> Contemporary studies often want to begin from things, from mathematical representations, from methods, from “critical thinking,” or from the felt need for the student to find how to express himself and engage with the world.
>
> But the classical tradition of Plato, Aristotle, St. Augustine, and St. Thomas Aquinas begins from speech. Human teaching and learning are fundamentally linguistic; and so that is where the tradition begins. Grammar, logic, and rhetoric are arts of language (or, to use the more proper traditional term, arts of speech), ordered to truth and to persuasion in a community of speakers.
>
> Ideally, you would have a teacher to lead you through these studies; but in our time it is often not easy to find a teacher; and this is especially true for older students.

### 2. Words, Concepts, and Things (words-things.html, opening sections)

> ## What a thing is
>
> In the classical tradition, we sometimes speak about things and sometimes about beings. What is the difference? A thing (Latin: “res”) is anything that we can speak about considered as to what it is, that is, as to its essence. Trees, numbers, colors, laws, and associations of lawyers are all things in this sense. When we consider the same things as to whether they exist, we speak instead of a “being”; so, when we consider tree in general or association in general we are considering tree or association as a thing; when we consider this tree here or this particular association, we are considering them as beings. So the word “thing” is quite general; it covers whatever can be named and considered; and in logic we are concerned with things, not beings.
>
> The first operation of the intellect is the grasp of the essence of anything that can be spoken about, that is, the grasp of the essence of a thing as we just defined it.
>
> ## The concept
>
> When we come to understand something, the mind draws out from our experience what the thing is (the tradition calls this abstraction) and forms within itself a concept of that thing. The concept is the "term" (from the Latin “terminus,” end) of our act of understanding, that is, the concept is what our act of understanding produces. A concept is also called a “term” in logic, because it is what stands as subject or predicate in a statement of the form “A is B.”
>
> Words differ from one language to another, but concepts of the same thing are the same for all. English says “cat” where another language might say “billee,” but the concept is one, because both are understandings of the same kind of animal. Compare two photographs of the same thing taken by two people: the cameras differ, but what is shown is the same; this is also true of concepts. This is a counterintuitive claim in our time, but it is a foundational one for the whole classical tradition. It is true, however, that one person's concept can be clearer than another person's concept of the same thing, but the central point is that concepts of the same thing are in fact the same.

### 3. Grammar (grammar.html)

> Grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly. It concerns significant speech, that is, speech that signifies something, and the arrangement of parts as words and phrases and clauses, so that what we mean can actually be communicated.
>
> ## What grammar studies
>
> Grammar treats the word as a conventional sign and asks how such signs are put together in a given language. A Semitic language like Hebrew where most words are formed from a three-letter root will take its conventional shape differently from a language like English that has a great deal of variety in word formation. Grammar studies letters and sounds, the parts of speech (noun, verb, adjective, preposition, and so on), and how words are joined into sentences.
>
> ## Grammar and logic
>
> A sentence can be grammatically correct and false, and it can be incorrect and true. Walshe gives a pair of examples: the grammarian judges “man is stone” correct and “man are animal” incorrect, while the logician judges the first false and the second true, though badly expressed (*Commentary on the Categories*, “What is Logic About?”). So grammar asks whether something is correctly said; logic asks whether what is said is true.
>
> For the same reason, the parts of speech are not the same as the categories. “Man” and “humanity” differ in grammatical form, but both fall under the logical category of substance. “Blue triangle” and “equilateral triangle” have the same grammatical form, adjective and noun, but the first joins something that only happens to be in its subject (i.e., triangles only happen to be blue) and the second joins two aspects of what makes a thing to be what it is, its essence. Grammar and logic consider the same words in different ways.
>
> ## Why grammar comes first
>
> Logic works on statements, and a statement is made of words. Rhetoric directs a speech to an intended audience, but there is nothing to direct if the speaker has not said or communicated anything. So, the classical tradition begins with grammar: the words must be correctly joined before anything can follow logically from them or be communicated to an audience.

## Process

1. Write one section (one panel, deck, or exercise set) at a time.
2. After drafting the section, do a separate pass for wording only, checking every sentence against this file: standard terms (rule 1), calques (rule 2), AI tics (rule 4), spelling, mention marks, feedback words, and the Allen and Greenough test.
3. Do not guess. If you are unsure of a term, a translation, or whether a phrase is standard, keep the plainest standard wording you know and list the doubtful wording for Timothy at the end of your report (location, the wording, and the question).

## Keeping this file current

Whenever Timothy flags a wording problem, append it as a new pair under "Pairs to add" (OLD → NEW, with source and date) before continuing. Do not remove or rewrite existing pairs.

## The word "fault"

The main English terms for errors of speech are "error," "barbarism," "solecism," and "impropriety." Section VI is titled "Correctness and Errors."
Use "fault" (or "vice") only when reporting what the ancient and medieval grammarians say or when translating *vitium*. For example: "Donatus treats the faults of speech (*vitia*): the barbarism and the solecism." The idea of *vitium* may stay at the center of the section, but "fault" should not be the word the app itself uses for an error.
- Not: "A barbarism is a fault in a single word." Instead: "A barbarism is an error in a single word."
- Not: "The construction is faulty." Instead: "The construction is incorrect."

## No indirect questions

Do not write indirect questions as titles, headings, descriptions or openings. Either ask a direct question or use a plain noun phrase. Titles need not be questions; a noun phrase such as "The Definition of Grammar" is fine.
- Not: "What grammar is." Instead: "What is grammar?" or "The Definition of Grammar."
- Not: "Why the parts are eight." Instead: "Why are there eight parts of speech?" or "The Reasons for the Parts."

## The word "office"

In the app's own voice, call reading, explaining, correcting and judging "the tasks of grammar" when speaking of the art (e.g., the Preliminary section description: "the definition, the tasks, and the parts of the art, and the criteria of correct speech") and "the tasks of the grammarian" when speaking of the person (deck title: "The Tasks of the Grammarian"). Use "office(s)" or "duties" only when translating *officium* or *officia* or reporting an ancient source directly. For example: "Varro, as Diomedes reports, names four duties (*officia*) of grammar."

## Translators

Do not name translators in the app's text (bodies, examples, source lines). Record them in sources/TRANSLATIONS-USED.md. Exceptions: a long quoted passage, or a place where the translation itself is important or disputed (e.g., the translation section). Never attribute a translation to T. Kearns unless he actually made it.

## Examples and sources

Examples are not mandatory. Do not give three examples on every panel by rule; give examples only where they genuinely help, and leave a panel without examples when they would only restate the body.

Do not put a source line on each panel. Each study deck has one Sources note, stored as the deck-level field `sources` and shown on the deck's first panel only. It lists only the main sources of the doctrine (the backbone texts the deck's teaching is drawn from), not every text cited. A text quoted inline carries its short title and citation in the text itself (for example, Isidore, *Etymologiae* I.5.1) and is not listed among the sources unless it is a backbone source.

## Technical terms before they are taught

Do not use "syllogism" (or similar technical terms of logic) before the student has learned it. In the early decks say "argument," "demonstration," or "account."
