# Revision Guide: Timothy's wording changes and decisions (Ars Grammatica)

Read this before drafting any new section. Each pair is BEFORE (draft) → AFTER (Timothy's revision). Learn the pattern behind each change, not only the phrase. Newest entries are at the bottom; add to this file whenever he revises a card.

**How to use the pairs:** they show Timothy's taste in particular sentences. They are illustrations, not rules. Do not turn a single change (e.g., "treats" → "considers") into a blanket substitution. Only the numbered decisions in section A apply everywhere.

## A. Decisions (apply everywhere)

1. **"Fault"** only when reporting the ancients or translating *vitium*. Our own words: error, barbarism, solecism, impropriety. Section VI is "Correctness and Errors."
2. **"Office(s)"** only when translating *officium/officia* or reporting Varro, Diomedes, Quintilian. Our own words: "the tasks of grammar" (the art), "the tasks of the grammarian" (the person).
3. **Indirect questions are not banned,** just not to be used by habit. Mix direct questions, noun phrases, and indirect questions where they read naturally. Not everything has to be a question.
4. **No example lists at the bottom of panels.** Examples go in the text. When the text illustrates a kind (a noun, a case, a figure), give more than one example.
5. **Sources:** one Sources note on a deck's first panel, listing only the backbone sources of the doctrine. A text quoted in passing is cited by its usual short title and locus in the text and is not thereby a "source."
6. **No translator names in the text,** except for a long quoted passage or where the translation itself matters or is disputed (e.g., the translation section). Record them in sources/TRANSLATIONS-USED.md. Never credit "T. Kearns" unless he actually made the translation.
7. **No technical terms before they are taught.** No "syllogism" in early decks; say "argument," "demonstration," or "account."
8. **Do not reuse the Trivium introduction's wording or arguments.** The model passages in CLAUDE.md show style only. Where general reasons are needed, summarize briefly and link to the [Introduction to the Trivium](https://studiuminstitute.org/trivium/).
9. **Prefer the ancient sources' own account first,** then the general philosophical reasons.
10. **Avoid old-fashioned renderings in our own voice** ("fitting speech," "in a fashion servile"). Use plain modern English, and keep the Latin word visible where it matters.
11. **The two parts of grammar are "precepts" and "interpretation"** (Timothy, 17 Oct 2026; replaces "principles," which was used briefly on 16 Oct). The first part (Quintilian's *ratio loquendi*, Greek *methodice*) is "precepts" or "the part concerned with precepts"; the second (*enarratio auctorum*, *historice*) is "interpretation." Never call the first part "principles," and never use *methodice*/*historice* as bare working names; when the Greek name appears, gloss it in the same sentence. Do not replace "principle" mechanically: in ordinary prose, "precepts," "rules," and "principles" may vary and illuminate one another (e.g., "a general rule or guiding principle of the art"; "the precepts and principles name the forms"). The deck is "Precepts and Interpretation" (id prelim.methodice unchanged).
12. **No references to "the previous deck," "the next deck," "the last card," and the like** (Timothy, 17 Oct 2026). Refer back or ahead in general terms ("We have seen that…," "as we saw earlier") or by the part of the course ("as we saw in the Preliminary section," "we will see in Section VI"). Decks may be read in any order, and the wording must survive reordering.

## D. Patterns from the Tasks of the Grammarian deck (16 Oct 2026)

These come from Timothy's line-by-line revision of prelim.offices.js after a self-review pass. They are tendencies to follow when drafting, not mechanical rules.

- **Connect each card to the one before it.** He opened card 2 with "We just saw that the tradition recognizes two broad parts of the grammarian's profession…" Start a new card by tying it to what came before, then narrow down: "Concerning grammar in this second way as the study of literature, Varro…"
- **Keep the frame visible.** Once a division is set up, name it again where it governs the text. Each of the four tasks became "the first (second, third, fourth) task of the grammarian as teacher and critic of literature."
- **Say why, not only what.** He added the reason one part is higher ("language reaches its most elaborate and powerful forms in writing; and, therefore, written speech requires the most detailed… study and criticism"), and he added "and why" to "which authors are to be taken as models."
- **Answer the modern student's doubt.** Where an ancient task seems strange today, say so briefly and answer it. For example: "(In our time, it is not customary to think of reading aloud as part of the study of literature but it clearly is, since if the text cannot be fluently read out loud then it will not be as easily understood.)"
- **Define the key word in the sense used here.** "Reading in this sense is…"; "literature, taken in a general sense to include everything thought to be especially well written"; "Explaining or exposition of the text."
- **English first, Latin in parentheses, in italics:** "reading (*lectio*), explaining (*enarratio*), correcting (*emendatio*), and judging (*iudicium*)." Don't give a Latin list followed by "In English we call them…"
- **Lists:** end the lead-in sentence with a colon and set the items one per line as a plain paragraph (`<br>` between items, **bold** English term). Don't use `<ul>` bullets, because they indent oddly in this layout.
- **Translations of quoted texts.** He prefers plain, modern renderings over Butler's Victorian ones: "student" for "boy"; "one must understand what one reads" for "he must understand what he reads"; "removing lines from texts" for "obelising lines." He allows ellipses to cut digressions (". . ."). Inside quotation marks, keep close to the sense of the Latin. If his preferred wording goes beyond the original, flag it for him.
- **Fill out Quintilian's elliptical wording** so it reads clearly: "what words are improperly used… what words are contrary to the laws of language."
- **Be precise about who errs and what the critic does:** "copyists and printers and editors make errors, and the grammarian seeks to restore what the author originally wrote." ("Seeks to" because restoring isn't always possible, and "originally" because later editors don't count.)
- **Always cite reports and paraphrases,** even if not exactly, e.g. "Varro, as Diomedes reports (*Ars grammatica*, in Keil, *Grammatici Latini* I, p. 426)."
- **Contrast authorities where they differ,** e.g. "Quintilian does not limit the grammarian to the poets, as Dionysius Thrax does."
- **Phrasing preferences:** "literary authors" rather than "the authors"; "the whole of grammar"; "and, finally," for the last item of a list; "is, in both, last and highest"; "its own work."

## K. Patterns from Timothy's own rewrite of P.3 and P.4 (17 Oct 2026)

These come from comparing the drafts with the text Timothy wrote himself for "Precepts and Interpretation" and "The Criteria of Correctness" (git log -p of data/decks/prelim.methodice.js and prelim.criteria.js). They are tendencies, not rules to apply mechanically. Section D still applies.

- **Titles that name the thing in the course's terms.** "What is antiquity?" became "What is the criterion of antiquity?" (likewise authority and usage); "How do the two parts depend on each other?" became "How do precepts and interpretation depend on each other?" He prefers the title to carry the frame and the actual terms, not a pronoun or a vague "the two parts."
- **Say what a term means here, and why it matters.** "Analogy here is…"; "as a standard"; "A precept, in this sense, is a general rule or guiding principle of the art"; the two meanings of the "origin" of a word, with the reason the second matters ("what the parts are can partly determine what the whole is"); *historice* "does not mean the study of history in our sense but rather… something like a survey of a whole topic."
- **Explain the grammar behind an example, not only the example.** On *puella bona*, he added what gender, number, and case each are and where each comes from, pointed to English case in pronouns ("they, their, them"), and pointed to the endings ("-a and -ae"). An example is followed far enough that a beginner sees why it shows the point.
- **Connect the ancient doctrine to the modern student's situation.** He added the twentieth-century neglect of grammar as a system (P.3 card 6), the twenty-first-century lack of a higher style (P.4 card 5), and the use of "thine" and "thou" by poets into the twentieth century and today (P.4 card 3). The ancient teaching is shown to bear on how we write and read now.
- **Tie the doctrine to the purpose of this course.** Twice he ended such a paragraph with a sentence about the course: "That is what this course teaches." and "That is also part of the purpose of this course." Use this sparingly, where the doctrine really does state the course's aim; it is not a closer for every card.
- **Judgment over rule-following.** He added that a grammarian is "a well-measured and careful judge," not "a strict rule master"; that the highest prose and poetry "follow or take account of the best writing but are not subservient to the styles of the past"; and that a sound decision requires knowing "what good style and word choice is and what the purposes of grammar and rhetoric are." Where a deck gives rules, say what the rules are for, and leave room for judgment.
- **Qualify absolutes.** "supports reason less often" → "does not always support reason"; "usage generally prevails"; "it is less common… but it is certainly possible if done well"; "not primarily errors in the strict sense." He states the case that is true rather than the neat one (compare CLAUDE.md rule 10).
- **Examples a modern student recognizes.** He added the "Our Father," the "thine"/"thou" of poets, "to wend one's way," Lincoln's "Four score," and a living author (Ward Farnsworth, *Classical English*). Prefer examples from prayer, familiar literature, and ordinary speech over obscure ones, alongside the classical examples.
- **Show how the criteria (and the parts of the art) explain one another.** Etymology explains the irregular "went" that analogy cannot; precepts and interpretation depend on each other. Where one part of the doctrine accounts for what another leaves unexplained, say so.
- **Fill out the scope.** "a form" → "a form or word or phrase"; "the origin of words" → "the origin of words or phrases"; "after him" → "through late ancient Rome and the Middle Ages down to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries"; "the authors" → "especially the literary authors and their particular works." He names the full range the doctrine covers.
- **Plain modern wording inside quoted translations where he chose it.** "sanctioning" → "giving their approval to" (Quintilian I.6.42). As in section D, flag any such change to a published translation for him rather than making it on your own.
- **Simple examples are announced as such.** "to take some simple examples," "To take a simple example." He signals the level of an example before giving it.
- **Only as much history as the point needs.** On "went" he asked for the simpler historical account rather than the fuller one with Old English forms. Give only as much history as the point needs.
