## The Word (*dictio*) and the Eight Parts (`iii.parts`)

1. A sentence can be divided into words, and each word still signifies something; if we divide further, into syllables, the parts no longer signify.
2. So the word is defined by its place in the sentence: it is the least part into which a sentence can be divided that still signifies.
3. The voice, the letter, and the syllable do not signify by imposition; signification by imposition begins with the word, and so the grammar of the word begins here.
4. Priscian defines the word (Latin *dictio*) as the least part of a constructed sentence, that is, of a sentence put together in order, and Dionysius Thrax had given the same definition in Greek.
5. The words of a sentence serve it in different ways, so they are of different kinds, and the Latin name for these kinds is *partes orationis*, the parts of speech.
6. *Oratio* here means a sentence, or connected speech, so the parts of speech are the kinds of word of which sentences are made.
7. The parts of speech are in the first place a classification of the ways in which a word can serve as a part of a sentence, not a classification of the words in a dictionary.
8. When the grammarians sorted all the words of Latin by the ways in which they serve in a sentence, they found eight kinds.
9. Donatus’s *Ars minor* begins by asking how many parts of speech there are, and answers eight: the noun, the pronoun, the verb, the adverb, the participle, the conjunction, the preposition, and the interjection.
10. Donatus has no separate part for the adjective, because the Latin grammarians count the adjective as a kind of noun.
11. The same word can be a noun in one sentence and a verb in another, so the parts of speech cannot be distinguished by the sound or spelling of a word, but only by the way it signifies in the sentence.
12. English shows this clearly, because many English words have one form for several parts of speech; Latin usually distinguishes the parts by their forms, but it also has words with more than one use (*post* as preposition and as adverb).
13. Priscian draws the same conclusion: the parts of speech can be distinguished only by attending to what is proper to the signification of each.
14. Donatus defines the **noun** as a part of speech with case, signifying a body or a thing, either properly or commonly.
15. By “body” he means something that can be seen or touched, and by “thing” something that cannot; “properly” means that the noun names one particular thing, and “commonly” that it names a kind.
16. **Case** (Latin *casus*) is a form of a noun that shows its relation to the other words of the sentence.
17. Donatus counts the adjective under the noun, because Latin adjectives have case and are declined like nouns, so that an adjective is, in his terms, a noun that signifies a quality.
18. A word put for a noun and referring to the same thing, so that the noun need not be repeated, is a **pronoun**.
19. Donatus defines the pronoun as a part of speech that, put in place of a noun, signifies almost as much as the noun and sometimes indicates a person.
20. The pronoun signifies “almost as much” as the noun because it refers to a thing without saying what kind of thing it is.
21. The pronouns of the first and second person refer to the speaker and to the one spoken to, and this is what Donatus means by indicating a person.
22. Donatus defines the **verb** as a part of speech with time and person, without case, signifying either doing something, or undergoing something, or neither.
23. Time and person are the two accidents that Donatus puts into his definition, since the verb consignifies time and is marked for person.
24. The verb has no case, that is, no form that shows it as subject or object, as a noun has.
25. A noun and a verb together make a complete sentence, and this is why the verb is the principal part of the sentence besides the noun.
26. A **participle** describes a thing, as an adjective does, but still signifies an action or an undergoing, as a verb does.
27. Donatus defines the participle as a part of speech that takes part of the noun and part of the verb, and its name comes from *pars* (“part”) and *capere* (“to take”).
28. From the noun the participle takes gender and case, and from the verb it takes time and the signification of doing or undergoing.
29. Donatus counts the participle as a part of its own because it belongs fully to neither the noun nor the verb.
30. The **adverb** (*adverbium*, “what is added to the verb”) is, in Donatus’s words, a part of speech that, added to a verb, makes its meaning clear and completes it.
31. The **preposition** (*praepositio*, “what is placed before”) is placed before a noun or pronoun and relates it to the rest of the sentence, and in Latin it takes a particular case.
32. Donatus defines the preposition as a part of speech placed before other parts of speech, which completes, changes, or lessens their meaning.
33. The **conjunction** (*coniunctio*, “joining”) joins words, or joins clauses and shows how they are related; in Donatus’s words it is a part of speech that joins and orders the thought.
34. The **interjection** (*interiectio*, “what is thrown in”) expresses a feeling and is thrown into a sentence without being constructed with its other words.
35. Donatus defines the interjection as a part of speech that signifies a feeling of the mind by an unformed utterance.
36. An interjection is a word, unlike a groan, because it signifies by imposition.
37. English has articles, the definite article “the” and the indefinite article “a” or “an,” which show whether a particular thing or any thing of the kind is meant; Latin has no article.
38. The list of eight parts was first made for Greek, which has an article, and Dionysius Thrax counts the noun, verb, participle, **article**, pronoun, preposition, adverb, and conjunction.
39. Because Latin has no article, the Latin grammarians removed it from the list, and they kept the number at eight by counting the interjection, which the Greek grammarians had placed under the adverb, as a part of its own.
40. English school grammars usually count eight parts of speech, but not the same eight: noun, pronoun, adjective, verb, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection, with the articles usually counted with the adjectives.
41. Some older English grammars count the article separately and so have nine parts.
42. The English list counts the **adjective** as a part of its own, because English adjectives are not declined like nouns, and it treats the **participle** as a form of the verb.
43. The lists differ because the languages differ and because the distinctions can be drawn more or less finely, but the ways of signifying that they describe are largely the same.
44. This course uses Donatus’s eight, because they are the parts of the tradition, and notes the English practice where it differs.
45. The noun, the pronoun, the verb, and the participle change their form to show their accidents, and they are called the **declinable** parts, from Latin *dēclīnāre*, “to turn aside.”
46. The adverb, the preposition, the conjunction, and the interjection keep one form, and they are called the **indeclinable** parts (some adverbs have degrees of comparison, but they are not declined for case or time).
47. This division orders the study of the parts: after the question why there are these eight, the declinable parts are studied with their accidents, and then the indeclinable parts.

## The Reasons for the Parts (`iii.reasons`)

1. Donatus and Priscian give the parts of speech and their definitions, but they seldom ask why the parts are as they are, for example why the verb has tense and the noun does not, or why there are eight parts.
2. The grammarians who set out to answer such questions were the **modists** (Latin *modistae*) of the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, so called because they explained the parts of speech by their modes of signifying.
3. The best known of the modists are Boethius of Dacia and Thomas of Erfurt, whose *Grammatica speculativa* became the standard textbook of the school.
4. **Speculative grammar** (from Latin *speculari*, “to look at,” “to consider”) is grammar that seeks the causes of what the art teaches.
5. Speculative grammar does not replace the precepts of Donatus and Priscian but asks why they are true, and it develops Priscian’s teaching that the parts of speech are distinguished by what is proper to the signification of each.
6. The modists name three ways in which a thing can be considered: **the mode of being** (*modus essendi*), the way the thing is in itself; **the mode of understanding** (*modus intelligendi*), the way the mind grasps it; and **the mode of signifying** (*modus significandi*), the way a word signifies it.
7. The order is fixed: the mode of understanding is taken from the mode of being, and the mode of signifying from the mode of understanding, just as words signify things by means of concepts.
8. A thing is what it is whether anyone understands it or not, but a word cannot signify anything unless someone understands what it is imposed for.
9. The mode of signifying is not the meaning: two words can signify the same thing and still be different parts of speech, because they signify it in different modes (“hope” and “hopes”).
10. The modists’ own example is a group of Latin words that all signify pain, a noun, a verb, a participle, and an interjection: the thing signified is one, but the modes of signifying are four.
11. So the part of speech of a word is decided not by what it signifies but by how it signifies.
12. The modists distinguish two kinds of mode of signifying: the **essential mode**, which makes a word the part of speech that it is, and the **accidental modes**, which give the word its accidents, such as number and case.
13. So a word remains the same part of speech when its accidents change, and only the essential modes distinguish the eight parts.
14. A noun names a thing and at the same time says what kind of thing it is, so it signifies a substance together with a quality.
15. Priscian says that it is proper to the noun to signify substance and quality.
16. Thomas of Erfurt says that the noun signifies by the mode of a being, that is, by the mode of something stable and lasting, so that any word that signifies its thing as something stable that can be talked about is a noun.
17. The modists divide the noun further: a **noun substantive** signifies its thing as standing by itself, and a **noun adjective** signifies its thing as belonging to another.
18. This is the reason that the tradition counts the adjective under the noun: it signifies in the mode of a stable thing, a quality, but as adhering to something else.
19. A pronoun refers to a thing without saying what kind of thing it is.
20. Priscian says that it is proper to the pronoun to be put in place of a proper noun and to signify definite persons, and the medieval grammarians, following him, say that the pronoun signifies substance without quality.
21. The modists explain that the pronoun signifies its thing as a being, as the noun does, but without determining what kind of thing it is.
22. So the noun and the pronoun share the mode of a stable thing but differ as a description differs from a pointing; this is why a pronoun can stand for any noun and usually needs a noun earlier in the sentence or the conversation to make its reference clear.
23. The verb signifies by the mode of becoming (*per modum fieri*), that is, as something going on, and as distinct from the thing of which it is said.
24. Because it signifies as becoming, the verb has time, and St. Thomas gives the reason that motion is measured by time.
25. The participle also signifies by the mode of becoming, but as united with the thing, not as said of it, so it has time from the verb and case and gender from the noun.
26. So the verb and the participle differ as the noun and the adjective do: one stands apart, and the other adheres.
27. The indeclinable parts do not name a thing or say what happens; each does something to other words or to what is said.
28. The **adverb** signifies by the mode of determining a word that signifies as becoming.
29. The **preposition** signifies by the mode of standing beside a word that has case and relating it to an action.
30. The **conjunction** signifies by the mode of joining two terms, whether words or clauses.
31. The **interjection** signifies by the mode of determining a verb or participle and expressing a feeling of the mind.
32. These four parts signify a relation to another word, not a thing as stable or as becoming, and this is why they are indeclinable: they do not need forms for case or time.
33. The modists’ account explains why there are eight parts: every word either signifies a thing or relates other words to one another.
34. A word that signifies a thing signifies it as stable or as becoming; as stable, it either says what the thing is (the noun) or only points to it (the pronoun); as becoming, it either stands apart to be said of a thing (the verb) or adheres to it (the participle).
35. A word that relates other words either determines a word of becoming (the adverb), relates a word with case to an action (the preposition), joins two terms (the conjunction), or expresses a feeling of the mind (the interjection).
36. This gives four declinable and four indeclinable parts, and the division does not depend on Latin alone.
37. Roger Bacon holds that grammar is one and the same in substance in all languages, although it varies in its accidents.
38. An objection arises from nouns such as “chimaera,” “nothing,” and “blindness”: if the noun signifies its thing as a stable being, and the mode of signifying is taken from the mode of being, how can there be nouns for what does not exist or is a lack?
39. Thomas of Erfurt answers that the mode of signifying may be taken from the properties of another thing, provided that it does not conflict with the thing signified.
40. So we understand a privation (the lack of something that would otherwise be present) through what it lacks and name it by those properties, and we name an imagined thing by the properties of the real things from which we imagine it.
41. St. Thomas adds that the grammarian considers substance only as to the mode of signifying: whatever a noun signifies is signified as something of which something can be said, even if it does not exist.
42. So the question whether such a thing exists belongs to logic and metaphysics, not to grammar.
43. The dialecticians counted only two parts of speech, the noun and the verb, because these alone make a statement.
44. St. Thomas explains that logic considers speech insofar as it is true or false; the pronoun is included under the noun, the participle under the verb, and the other parts are not parts of the statement but what joins its parts.
45. The grammarian counts all eight, because grammar considers speech insofar as it is correct and complete, and all eight are needed to speak correctly.
46. So the two counts do not contradict each other; they answer different questions, and the modists’ eight include the logician’s two as the principal parts.

## Figure and Species (`iii.figure`)

1. Each part of speech has its accidents, but two accidents are shared by nearly all the parts, and they concern not the relation of a word to the sentence but the way the word itself is made: **figure** (Latin *figura*) and **species** (Latin *species*).
2. Figure is the distinction between a simple word and a compound word, and species is the distinction between a primitive word, which is not formed from another word, and a derived word, which is.
3. Both accidents ask whether a word is made from other words, and if so, how, and they are studied before the individual parts because they apply to all of them.
4. Donatus says that there are two figures of nouns, the simple and the compound.
5. A **simple** word is one that is not made of two or more words or parts of words, and a **compound** word is one that is (“able” and “unable”).
6. A compound word is still one word, with one meaning, and its parts do not signify by themselves within it.
7. When two words are joined into one, each may keep its whole form or change it.
8. Donatus says that nouns are compounded in four ways: from two whole words; from two words that are changed; from a whole word and a changed one; and from a changed word and a whole one.
9. Sometimes a noun is compounded from more than two parts.
10. English forms compounds very freely, especially by joining two nouns, or an adjective and a noun.
11. English spelling does not treat all compounds alike: some are written as one word, some with a hyphen, and some as two words, and the custom often changes over time.
12. A compound is distinguished from a phrase chiefly by its meaning and, in speech, by its stress, so in English, as in Latin, a compound is one word with one meaning, even when it is written as two.
13. Figure belongs not only to the noun but to the other parts of speech, and Latin verbs are very often compounded with prepositions.
14. Pronouns, adverbs, and conjunctions can be compound as well, in Latin and in English.
15. Many English words are compounds borrowed from Latin, and English also forms compounds of its own of the same kind (“withdraw”).
16. The second accident concerns whether a word is formed from another word, usually by adding an ending.
17. Priscian calls this accident species and divides it into two: the **primitive** species, which belongs to a word that is not formed from another, and the **derived** species, which belongs to a word that is formed from another.
18. A derived word may belong to a different part of speech from its primitive (“golden” from “gold”).
19. In **composition** two words, or parts of words, are joined, and each part can usually be found as a word of the language.
20. In **derivation** one word is formed from another, usually by adding an ending that is not itself a word.
21. So the two accidents answer different questions, and one word can be both compound and derived (“unkindness”).
22. The grammarians treat figure and species as accidents of each part of speech, because in Latin they belong to the noun, the pronoun, the verb, the participle, the adverb, and the conjunction alike.
23. These distinctions are the foundation of the study of word formation: how derived words are formed by their endings, how compounds are formed and what changes their parts undergo, and how words are traced to their origins in etymology.

## Homonymy and Synonymy (`iii.homonymy`)

1. The same sound can be imposed more than once with different meanings, and a word of this kind is called a **homonym**, from the Greek *homōnymos*, “having the same name.”
2. Donatus names, among the kinds of noun, the homonyms, which signify many things by one name.
3. Different words that signify the same thing are called **synonyms**.
4. Donatus names this case as well: nouns that are many in name but signify one thing, which he calls **synonyms** or **polyonyms** (Greek *synōnymos*, “named together,” and *polyōnymos*, “many-named”).
5. English is especially rich in synonyms because it has taken many words from French and Latin beside its native ones.
6. Synonyms signify the same thing but rarely signify it in exactly the same way: they often differ in tone, in the kind of speech to which they belong, or in a shade of meaning.
7. So the grammarian, in explaining a text, asks not only what a word means but why the author chose this word rather than its synonym, and a reader who treats synonyms as interchangeable will miss much of what an author has chosen.
8. The terms “homonymous” and “synonymous” were first used in Aristotle’s *Categories*, and Aristotle uses them of things, not of words.
9. Things are homonymous, he says, when they have only a name in common and the account of what each is differs; things are synonymous when they have both the name and the account in common.
10. So for Aristotle two things of one kind called by the same name are synonymous, whereas for the grammarians synonyms are different words with the same meaning: the grammarians took the terms from the logicians but applied them to words.
11. Aristotle names a third relation: things are **paronymous** (Greek *parōnymos*, “named alongside”) when they take their name from something else with a change of ending.
12. Paronymy is closely related to species: the derived word is formed from the primitive by a change of ending, and its meaning depends on the meaning of the primitive.
13. The logician considers the relation of the things named, and the grammarian the relation of the words.
14. Modern grammars distinguish kinds of homonyms by sound and spelling: **homophones** are words that sound alike but may be spelled differently, and **homographs** are words that are spelled alike but may sound different.
15. A homophone is ambiguous only in speech, and a homograph with two pronunciations only in writing, because of the imperfect fit between English spelling and sound.
16. Ambiguity in a single word is homonymy in use, whereas synonymy does not cause ambiguity, though it raises the question why one word was chosen over another.
17. A homonym is not ambiguous in every sentence, because in most sentences the context decides which meaning is intended.
18. The grammarian’s task, in reading and explaining, is to recognize the homonym and show from the context which meaning the author intended, and where the context does not decide, to say that the passage is ambiguous.

## The Noun I: Kinds, Quality, and Comparison (`iii.noun-1`)

1. The noun signifies a body or a thing, with case, by the mode of a stable thing, and the differences among nouns are the accidents of the noun.
2. Donatus says that the noun has six accidents: **quality** (*qualitas*), **comparison** (*comparatio*), **gender** (*genus*), **number** (*numerus*), **figure** (*figura*), and **case** (*casus*).
3. Figure is the distinction between simple and compound nouns; quality divides nouns into proper and common; comparison belongs to adjectives of quality and quantity; and gender, number, and case are the accidents by which a Latin noun is declined, best studied together.
4. A **proper noun** names one particular person, place, or thing, and a **common noun** names any member of a kind.
5. Donatus calls this distinction the **quality** of the noun: a noun is either the name of one thing, and is called proper, or the name of many, and is called appellative.
6. The usual English term for the appellative noun is the **common noun**.
7. In English a proper noun usually begins with a capital letter, but the distinction is one of meaning, not of spelling.
8. A person’s given name, family name, and any title added for some deed or quality are each proper nouns, because each names one person.
9. Donatus divides the proper names of Romans into four kinds: the **praenomen** (“forename”), the personal name used in the family; the **nomen** (“name”), the name of the clan (*gēns*); the **cognomen** (“added name”), which distinguished a branch of the clan; and the **agnomen** (“further name”), given for some deed or quality.
10. Modern grammars call common nouns that name bodies that can be seen and touched **concrete nouns**, and those that name things that cannot **abstract nouns**.
11. The division follows from Donatus’s definition of the noun as signifying a body or a thing, and Donatus makes it himself, into bodily (*corporalia*) and bodiless (*incorporalia*) nouns.
12. A noun that is singular in form but names a group of many is called a **collective noun**, and Donatus describes such nouns as singular in form but plural in meaning.
13. Because a collective noun refers to many although it is singular in form, it raises questions of agreement.
14. The Latin grammarians call the adjective the **noun adjective** (*nōmen adiectīvum*, “the noun that is added”), because it is added to another noun to signify a quality or quantity of the thing that noun names.
15. The reason for counting it under the noun is that a Latin adjective has the accidents of the noun: it is declined for gender, number, and case, and it agrees with its noun in all three.
16. English adjectives are not declined, and English grammars therefore count the adjective as a separate part of speech; in both languages, however, the adjective depends on a noun and tells what sort of thing the noun refers to.
17. An adjective is used as a noun when the noun it would describe is understood, and it is then said to be used **substantively**; this is possible because the adjective and the substantive share the mode of a stable thing.
18. A noun can also be used to describe another noun; English does this very freely, whereas Latin does it rarely, preferring an adjective or a genitive.
19. So in a given sentence we must ask how the word is used.
20. The accident by which a quality is said of a thing in different degrees is called **comparison** (*comparātiō*).
21. The **positive** degree signifies the quality simply, the **comparative** signifies more of the quality in one thing than in another, and the **superlative** signifies the most of the quality among several things, or a very high degree of it.
22. Donatus gives the same three degrees: the positive, the comparative, and the superlative.
23. In Latin the comparative is usually formed by adding *-ior* to the stem of the adjective and the superlative by adding *-issimus*, and adjectives ending in *-er* form the superlative in *-rimus*.
24. In English short adjectives add *-er* and *-est*, and longer adjectives usually form the degrees with “more” and “most”; Latin uses the same method with some adjectives, especially those ending in *-us* after a vowel.
25. Some of the most common adjectives are compared irregularly, by forms taken from different words, in Latin and in English alike (*bonus*, *melior*, *optimus*).
26. It is commonly observed that the words used most often are the most likely to keep irregular forms, because they are learned by hearing rather than formed by rule.
27. Not every noun can be compared: a substantive cannot, whereas adjectives that signify something that can be more or less can.
28. Donatus says that only appellative nouns that signify quality or quantity are compared.
29. Some adjectives also do not admit comparison, because what they signify does not admit more or less (“dead”).
30. So comparison is an accident that belongs to the noun because of what the noun signifies, and the other accidents of the noun belong to it for reasons of the same kind.

## The Noun II: Gender, Number, and Case (`iii.noun-2`)

1. Nouns fall into kinds according to the words that refer to them or agree with them, and this accident is **gender** (Latin *genus*, “kind”).
2. Donatus says that there are four genders of nouns: the **masculine**; the **feminine**; the **neuter** (“neither”); and the **common**, which is masculine or feminine according to the person meant.
3. The grammarians used *hic*, *haec*, and *hoc* (“this,” in the three genders) to show the gender of a noun.
4. Donatus also names the **epicene** noun (Greek *epikoinos*, “shared”), which names an animal of either sex under one gender.
5. In Latin the gender of a noun that names a person usually follows the sex of the person, but Latin assigns a gender to every noun, including nouns that name things without sex, and this is called **grammatical gender**.
6. Grammatical gender is known partly from the ending of the noun and partly from the usage of the language, so most nouns in *-a* are feminine, but those that name men are masculine.
7. English has **natural gender**: a noun is masculine, feminine, or neuter according to what it refers to, and the gender appears only in the pronouns that refer to it.
8. English keeps a few traces of other usage, as when a ship or a nation is called “she.”
9. Number is consignified along with the principal meaning of a word, and Donatus gives two numbers, the **singular** and the **plural**.
10. Greek also has a **dual** number for two things.
11. English forms most plurals by adding *-s* or *-es* but keeps some older forms, and some nouns have the same form in both numbers.
12. Some nouns are used only in the plural, and some are rarely used in the plural at all.
13. A form of a noun that shows the relation of the noun to the other words of the sentence is called a **case** (*casus*, “falling”).
14. The Latin word translates the Greek *ptōsis*, because the Greek grammarians thought of the first form of the noun as upright and of the other forms as “falling” from it.
15. Donatus counts six cases in Latin: the **nominative** (“naming”), the **genitive** (“of origin”), the **dative** (“of giving”), the **accusative**, the **vocative** (“of calling”), and the **ablative** (“of taking away”).
16. The name “accusative” is a mistranslation, because the Greek term meant the case of what is caused or affected, but the Latin grammarians took it from the verb “to accuse.”
17. Greek has no ablative, so the Latin grammarians had to name this case themselves.
18. Each case has many uses, but each has a principal use: the nominative is the case of the subject, and the genitive usually shows possession or relation and is often translated with “of.”
19. The dative is the case of the indirect object, translated with “to” or “for,” and the accusative is the case of the direct object.
20. The vocative is the case of address, and the ablative shows means, manner, place, or time and is translated with “by,” “with,” “from,” or “in.”
21. Many uses of the cases also depend on prepositions, which govern particular cases.
22. English nouns have kept only one case ending, the **possessive**, whereas the English personal pronouns keep three cases: the subjective, the objective, and the possessive.
23. For the other relations that Latin shows by case endings, English uses the order of words and prepositions.
24. In English, exchanging the subject and the object reverses the meaning, whereas in Latin the endings show the subject and the object, so the order can change without changing the meaning.
25. The forms of a noun in all its cases and numbers make up its **declension** (*dēclīnātiō*), and to give them in order is to **decline** the noun.
26. A declension is also a class of nouns that share one set of endings, and Latin has five declensions.
27. The declension of a Latin noun is known from its genitive singular: *-ae* in the first, *-ī* in the second, *-is* in the third, *-ūs* in the fourth, and *-ēī* or *-eī* in the fifth.
28. The genitive also shows the **stem**, the part of the word to which the endings are added, which the nominative does not always show.
29. So a Latin noun is learned with its nominative, its genitive, and its gender.
30. Most nouns of the first declension are feminine, most nouns of the second declension in *-us* are masculine, and nouns of the second declension in *-um* are neuter.
31. The vocative has the same form as the nominative except in nouns in *-us* of the second declension.
32. A neuter noun always has the same form in the nominative and the accusative, and in the plural these cases end in *-a*.
33. The third declension contains nouns of all three genders, and its nominative singular takes many forms, so that the stem must be learned from the genitive.
34. Many English words come from the stems of third-declension nouns rather than from their nominatives.
35. The fourth and fifth declensions contain fewer nouns: most nouns of the fourth are masculine, and most nouns of the fifth are feminine.
36. An ending does not always show a single case, so the reader must decide from the other words of the sentence which case is meant.
37. The verb and the other words of the sentence decide such a case, which is a first example of construction: the words of a sentence determine one another.
38. A Latin adjective has forms for the genders and is declined in every case, which is why it is counted under the noun.
39. Most Latin adjectives belong to one of two classes: adjectives of the first and second declensions, with masculine, feminine, and neuter forms, and adjectives of the third declension, with one form for the masculine and feminine and one for the neuter, or one form for all three genders.
40. An adjective takes the gender, number, and case of the noun that it describes, but it need not have the same ending as the noun, because the noun may belong to another declension.
41. The modists explain the accidents of the noun by accidental modes, each taken from some property of the thing as it is understood.
42. Number, according to Thomas of Erfurt, is taken from the property of being one or many: the singular signifies the thing as undivided, the plural as divided into many.
43. Case is taken from the relation of the thing to another in the sentence: the nominative signifies the thing as that of which something is said, and the oblique cases (the cases other than the nominative) signify it as the end or object of another word.
44. Gender, the modists held, is taken from the property of acting or of being acted upon: the masculine signifies the thing under the property of acting, the feminine under the property of receiving, and the neuter under neither.
45. This account of number and case is close to the grammarians’ ordinary teaching, but the account of gender meets an objection from grammatical gender, since many things without sex have a masculine or feminine gender.
46. Priscian had already given the answer that most grammarians accept: the principal genders are two, masculine and feminine, which alone nature knows, and the others are known more from the form of the word than from nature.
47. So in names of living things gender usually follows sex, and in other nouns it follows the form of the word and the usage of the language.
48. Thomas of Erfurt must say for such nouns that the mode is taken from another thing; that answer saves the theory but does not explain why any particular noun has its gender, and here Priscian’s account is the more accurate one.

## The Pronoun (`iii.pronoun`)

1. A pronoun is put in place of a noun and signifies almost as much as the noun, because it refers to a thing without saying what kind of thing it is.
2. Donatus gives the pronoun six accidents: **quality** (*qualitas*), **gender** (*genus*), **number** (*numerus*), **figure** (*figura*), **person** (*persona*), and **case** (*casus*).
3. Gender, number, figure, and case have the same meaning here as in the noun; the two that are new are quality, which for the pronoun means something different, and person.
4. For the pronoun, **quality** is the distinction between definite and indefinite pronouns.
5. **Definite** pronouns refer to a definite person, and **indefinite** pronouns do not.
6. Donatus states that pronouns are either definite or indefinite.
7. So the quality of the pronoun concerns how definitely it refers, whereas the quality of the noun concerns whether it names one thing or a kind.
8. **Person** (*persōna*) is the relation of the thing referred to the act of speaking.
9. The **first person** is the speaker, the **second person** is the one spoken to, and the **third person** is anyone or anything spoken about.
10. The verb also consignifies person, and because the Latin verb shows the person by its ending, Latin often omits the pronoun subject.
11. When the Latin pronoun subject is expressed, it usually gives emphasis, whereas English almost always expresses the pronoun, because its verb endings no longer show the person clearly.
12. The English personal pronouns keep three cases, so they keep the distinction of subject and object that English nouns have lost.
13. A pronoun in the wrong case is an error of case (“Her and me arrived late” for “She and I arrived late”).
14. The Latin pronouns of the first and second person have no distinction of gender, because the speaker and the one spoken to are present and need no description.
15. Latin has no single personal pronoun of the third person; it uses the demonstratives *is*, *ea*, *id* or *ille*, *illa*, *illud*, which have all three genders.
16. A pronoun of the third person refers to something named elsewhere, usually earlier, in the sentence or the conversation, and the noun to which it refers is called its **antecedent** (Latin *antecēdēns*, “going before”).
17. The pronoun takes the gender and number of its antecedent, but its case depends on its own use in its clause.
18. Pronouns that point out a thing as if by gesture are called **demonstrative** pronouns (from *dēmōnstrāre*, “to point out”).
19. Latin has three demonstratives that correspond to the three persons: *hic* points to what is near the speaker, *iste* to what is near the one spoken to, and *ille* to what is farther from both.
20. English has two demonstratives, “this” and “that,” with their plurals.
21. A demonstrative can stand alone as a pronoun or be used with a noun as an adjective.
22. A pronoun that refers to an antecedent and at the same time introduces a clause that tells something about it is called a **relative** pronoun.
23. The relative pronoun takes the gender and number of its antecedent, but its case depends on its use in its own clause.
24. A pronoun that asks a question about the person or thing, marking the place in the sentence that the answer fills, is an **interrogative** pronoun.
25. An **indefinite** pronoun refers to a person or thing without identifying it, and these pronouns are the clearest examples of Donatus’s indefinite quality.
26. Several indefinite pronouns, such as “each” and “nobody,” are also syncategorematic words.
27. A pronoun that refers back to the subject of its own clause is a **reflexive** pronoun.
28. The **possessive** pronouns are adjectives formed from the personal pronouns.
29. Latin distinguishes the reflexive possessive, which refers to the subject, from the possessive of another person, and so removes an ambiguity that English often leaves; an English writer must often rephrase the sentence to remove it.
30. Because a pronoun refers to a thing without describing it, its meaning depends entirely on its antecedent, and if the antecedent is unclear, the sentence is ambiguous.
31. Latin, which marks gender, can sometimes resolve such cases, but if both nouns have the same gender, Latin is ambiguous in the same way.
32. So a pronoun is correctly used only when the hearer can tell what it refers to, and an unclear pronoun is one of the common errors in the arrangement of words.
33. The noun and the pronoun signify by the mode of a stable thing, whereas the verb signifies by the mode of becoming and also has person, as the pronoun does.

## The Verb I: Quality, Mood, and Voice (`iii.verb-1`)

1. The properties in which one form of a verb differs from another (in the manner in which the action is presented, in whether the subject acts or is acted upon, in person and number, and in time) are the verb’s **accidents**.
2. Donatus gives the verb seven accidents: quality, conjugation, voice (*genus*), number, figure, tense, and person.
3. Donatus’s word for voice, *genus*, is the same word that names the gender of the noun.
4. Quality includes the moods and the forms of the verb, and voice concerns whether the subject acts or is acted upon; tense, person, number, and conjugation are studied together, because by them the forms of the verb are arranged in its conjugation.
5. A **mood** (Latin *modus*, “manner”) is a form of the verb that shows the manner in which the speaker presents the action: as a fact, as a command, as a wish, or as something supposed.
6. The moods are the forms of the verb that mark several of the differences among sentences that state, ask, command, pray, and call.
7. Donatus says that the quality of verbs lies in moods and in forms.
8. Donatus lists six moods: the indicative, the imperative, the optative, the subjunctive, the infinitive, and the impersonal.
9. The **indicative** mood presents the action as a fact; it is the mood of statements and of most questions, in Latin and in English.
10. The **imperative** mood is the mood of command and request; the Latin present imperative is the present stem when one person is addressed and adds *-te* when more than one is addressed, and English uses the bare form of the verb, usually without a subject.
11. A command is neither true nor false, and the imperative is the form of the verb that marks a sentence as a command.
12. Donatus distinguishes by their use two moods that have the same Latin form: the **optative**, used for wishes, and the **subjunctive**, used in clauses joined under another clause, as after *cum*.
13. Greek has separate forms for the optative, and the Latin grammarians kept the Greek distinction; modern Latin grammars count one mood, the subjunctive, with many uses (wishes, commands, possibilities, and many kinds of dependent clause).
14. English has kept only a few subjunctive forms: in conditions contrary to fact, after verbs of demanding, and in some fixed phrases.
15. In most other cases English uses auxiliary verbs such as “may,” “might,” “would,” and “should” where Latin uses the subjunctive.
16. The **infinitive** (from *īnfīnītus*, “unlimited”) is the form of the verb that names the action without limiting it by person or number.
17. Because it has no person, the infinitive cannot be the main verb of a statement; it is used as a noun or to complete another verb.
18. Donatus counted the infinitive among the moods, but modern grammars usually count it as a form of the verb outside the moods.
19. The **impersonal** verb is used only in the third person singular, without a personal subject (“it rains”), and Latin also uses the passive impersonally.
20. English uses “it” or “there” as a formal subject in impersonal sentences.
21. The second part of the verb’s quality is its **form**, by which one verb is formed from another by a regular ending where English often uses separate words or auxiliary verbs.
22. Donatus gives four forms: the **perfect** form, which is the verb itself; the **meditative** or desiderative, which signifies the wish or intention to do something; the **frequentative**, which signifies doing something often; and the **inchoative**, which signifies beginning to do something or to become something.
23. These forms are derived verbs, formed by regular endings, so they belong as much to species (the accident by which a word is primitive or derived) as to quality.
24. English grammars usually count three moods: the indicative, the imperative, and the subjunctive.
25. The English subjunctive has few distinct forms, and many meanings that Latin expresses by the subjunctive are expressed in English by the **modal auxiliaries** (“may,” “might,” “can,” “could,” “shall,” “should,” “will,” “would,” and “must”).
26. English uses auxiliary verbs where Latin uses one inflected form, as it uses prepositions where Latin uses case endings; the meaning is the same, but the means of expressing it differ.
27. In the **active** voice the subject does the action; in the **passive** voice the subject receives it; and the accident that Donatus calls *genus* is called voice in English grammars.
28. In the passive the one who receives the action becomes the subject, and the doer, if it is named, is expressed in Latin with *ā* or *ab* and the ablative, and in English with “by.”
29. Latin forms the passive with its own personal endings, whereas English forms it with the verb “to be” and the past participle.
30. Donatus says that active verbs end in *-ō* and, by taking the letter *r*, make passives from themselves.
31. Verbs that signify neither acting on something nor being acted upon end in *-ō* like active verbs but do not form a personal passive; Donatus calls them **neuter** verbs (from *neuter*, “neither”).
32. The neuter verb is the verb that signifies neither doing nor undergoing, the third possibility in Donatus’s definition of the verb.
33. Modern grammars call such verbs **intransitive**, verbs that do not take a direct object, and call verbs that take a direct object **transitive**, because the action passes over to the object.
34. Many English verbs can be used both transitively and intransitively.
35. **Deponent** verbs are passive in form but active in meaning; Donatus says that they end in *-r* like passive verbs, but if the *r* is taken away they are not Latin.
36. They are called deponent (from *dēpōnere*, “to lay aside”) because they seem to have laid aside their active forms.
37. A dictionary shows that a verb is deponent by giving its principal parts in passive form, and a reader who forgets that a verb is deponent will translate it as passive and reverse the sense, so the deponent verbs must be learned as such.
38. A **common** verb, the fifth kind under voice, ends in *-r* like a deponent but has both an active and a passive meaning.
39. In classical Latin very few verbs are used in this way, and modern grammars treat them as deponents with an occasional passive use.
40. Donatus’s five voices are the active, the passive, the neuter, the deponent, and the common.
41. Modern grammars speak of two voices, active and passive, and treat the neuter verb as intransitive and the deponent and common verbs as kinds of verb irregular in form; the difference is one of classification, not of the facts.
42. The choice of voice changes which word is the subject, and therefore what the sentence is about, but it need not change what is said to have happened.
43. The passive also allows the doer to be omitted when it is unknown or unimportant.
44. Voice is the accident by which the verb, which signifies by the mode of becoming, signifies its action as proceeding from the subject or as received by it.

## The Verb II: Tense, Person, Number, and Conjugation (`iii.verb-2`)

1. The verb consignifies time, and the form of the verb that shows the time of the action is called its **tense** (Latin *tempus*, “time”).
2. Donatus gives three times, the present, the past, and the future, and divides the past into three: the imperfect, the perfect, and the pluperfect.
3. Later grammarians add a sixth tense, the **future perfect**, which signifies an action that will be complete at some future time, so the Latin verb has six tenses in the indicative.
4. Each Latin tense is a single word, whereas most English tenses are formed with auxiliary verbs (“will,” “has,” “had”).
5. The Latin perfect has two English equivalents (“he praised” and “he has praised”), because Latin uses one tense for an action completed in the past and for an action whose completion is present.
6. The English verb has lost almost all its personal endings, keeping in the present only the *-s* of the third person singular, although the verb “to be” keeps more forms.
7. The Latin verb has the same three persons and two numbers as the pronoun, and it shows them by its **personal endings**: in the active *-ō* (or *-m*), *-s*, *-t*, *-mus*, *-tis*, *-nt*, and in the passive *-r*, *-ris*, *-tur*, *-mur*, *-minī*, *-ntur*.
8. So English must express the subject, whereas Latin can leave a pronoun subject unexpressed, because the ending of the verb already shows its person and number.
9. A **conjugation** (*coniugātiō*, “a yoking together”) is a class of verbs that share one set of endings.
10. Latin has four conjugations, known from the present infinitive: the first in *-āre*, the second in *-ēre*, the third in *-ere*, and the fourth in *-īre*.
11. The second and third conjugations differ by the quantity of the *e* of the infinitive, so the macron matters here.
12. Some verbs of the third conjugation end in *-iō* in the first person and have an *i* in some forms like the fourth conjugation; they are called third conjugation *-iō* verbs.
13. A Latin verb is learned by its **principal parts**, the four forms from which all its other forms are made: the first person singular of the present indicative, the present infinitive, the first person singular of the perfect indicative, and the supine (a verbal noun) or the perfect passive participle.
14. From the principal parts come three stems: the **present stem**, which gives the present, imperfect, and future; the **perfect stem**, which gives the perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect active; and the **participial stem**, which gives the perfect passive participle.
15. The perfect stem is often irregular and must be learned.
16. The present indicative active shows the personal endings joined to the present stem in each conjugation.
17. In the third conjugation the short vowel of the stem changes before the endings, so the third conjugation is the least regular of the four and must be learned with the most care.
18. Between the stem and the personal ending, most tenses and moods have a **sign** of their own.
19. On the present stem the imperfect indicative has *-bā-* (*-ēbā-* in the third and fourth conjugations); the future indicative has *-bi-* in the first and second conjugations and *-ē-* in the third and fourth (with *-am* in the first person); the present subjunctive has *-ē-* in the first conjugation and *-ā-* in the others; and the imperfect subjunctive is the infinitive with the personal endings.
20. So a Latin verb form is read by looking first at the stem, which shows whether it is built on the present or the perfect stem; then at the sign, which shows the tense and mood; and last at the ending, which shows the person, the number, and the voice.
21. The perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect indicative active are built on the perfect stem, and their endings are the same in every conjugation, so once the third principal part is known these forms follow from it.
22. The perfect passive is formed with the perfect passive participle and a form of *sum* (“to be”).
23. The English verb has only two tenses formed by the verb itself, the present and the past; the future, perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect are formed with the auxiliaries “will,” “have,” “had,” and “will have.”
24. An English verb is learned by three principal parts: the present, the past, and the past participle.
25. **Regular** verbs add *-ed* for both the past and the past participle, whereas **irregular** verbs change their vowel or their form.
26. The irregular verbs are among the oldest and most common verbs of English, and errors in them (“throwed” for “threw”) are among the commonest barbarisms of English, that is, errors in the form of a single word.
27. A verb can show not only when an action takes place but whether it is presented as going on or as complete; this difference is called **aspect** (Latin *aspectus*, “view”), because it concerns how the action is viewed.
28. In Latin the imperfect presents an action as going on or repeated, whereas the perfect presents it as complete; and the future perfect presents a future action as complete.
29. Varro observed that the Latin tenses fall into two groups: those of the incomplete action (*īnfectum*), the present, imperfect, and future, and those of the complete action (*perfectum*), the perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect.
30. So the tenses built on the present stem and those built on the perfect stem differ not in time but in aspect.
31. English marks aspect with auxiliary verbs: the **progressive** (“be” with the form in *-ing*) presents an action as going on, and the **perfect** (“have” with the past participle) presents it as complete, with its result still present.
32. Greek shows aspect most clearly, with three stems for three aspects: the present stem for an action going on, the aorist stem for an action viewed simply as an event, and the perfect stem for a completed action whose result remains.
33. The Latin perfect serves for both the Greek aorist and the Greek perfect.
34. A few of the most common Latin verbs do not follow the four conjugations, and the most important is *sum* (“I am”), whose principal parts are *sum*, *esse*, *fuī*, *futūrus*; its compounds, such as *possum*, follow it.
35. Other irregular Latin verbs are *eō* (“I go”), *ferō* (“I carry”), *volō* (“I wish”), and *fīō* (“I become”).
36. English “to be” is irregular in the same way, because its forms come from different old roots, and the words used most often are the most likely to keep irregular forms.
37. To **parse** a verb (from Latin *pars*, “part”) is to name its part of speech and give its accidents: its person, number, tense, mood, and voice, and its conjugation and principal parts.
38. Some forms admit more than one analysis (the first person singular of the future indicative and of the present subjunctive of the third and fourth conjugations are the same), and only the sentence can decide which form is meant.
39. The modists derive the accidents of the verb from its mode of becoming.
40. **Tense**, according to Thomas of Erfurt, is the mode of signifying the action under the property of time, present, past, or future, and it belongs to the verb because what is signified as becoming is measured by time.
41. The verb signifies its action as distinct from the substance of which it is said, so it must be joined to a subject.
42. **Person** and **number** are the modes by which the verb signifies its action as belonging to a subject of the first, second, or third person, and of one or many.
43. So the modists explain why the verb agrees with its subject in person and number: these accidents are the verb’s way of signifying its relation to the subject.
44. The modists explain **mood** as the mode by which the verb signifies the inclination of the mind toward what is said: indicative when the mind simply declares, imperative when it commands, optative when it wishes, and subjunctive when it presents something as dependent on another.
45. So mood signifies not a property of the action itself but a relation of the speaker’s mind to it.
46. **Voice** (*genus*) they explain as the mode by which the verb signifies its action as proceeding from the subject, as received by it, or as neither, which corresponds to Donatus’s active, passive, and neuter.
47. In this way the modists account for each of Donatus’s accidents by a property of the thing or of the mind.

## The Participle (`iii.participle`)

1. The participle takes part of the noun and part of the verb: it describes a noun, as an adjective does, but it also signifies an action and can take its own object, as a verb does.
2. Donatus gives the participle six accidents: gender, case, tense, signification, number, and figure.
3. Gender and case it takes from the noun; tense and signification it takes from the verb; number and figure it shares with both.
4. By signification Donatus here means what the verb calls voice: whether the participle signifies doing or undergoing.
5. In every case the participle agrees with a noun, as an adjective does, but it signifies an action, as a verb does.
6. English has two participles and expresses other meanings of the same kind with several words, whereas Latin has a single word for each.
7. A Latin verb has four participles, formed from its stems: the present active (present stem with *-ns*), the perfect passive (participial stem with *-us*), the future active (participial stem with *-ūrus*), and the future passive, or gerundive (present stem with *-ndus*).
8. So Latin has no perfect active participle and no present passive participle.
9. The perfect passive, future active, and future passive participles are declined like *bonus*, *bona*, *bonum*, and the present participle like an adjective of the third declension.
10. Because it has gender, number, and case, the participle agrees with a noun in all three, as an adjective does.
11. But the participle keeps the power of the verb from which it comes: it can take an object and can be modified by an adverb.
12. So in its relation to the noun the participle is an adjective, and in its relation to its own object and adverbs it is a verb.
13. The tense of a participle shows time not absolutely, as the tense of a verb does, but relative to the time of the main verb.
14. The present participle signifies an action at the same time as the main verb; the perfect participle, an action before it; the future participle, an action after it.
15. English participles follow the same rule.
16. For this reason the participle is especially useful in Latin narrative, because one sentence can describe several actions and show their order in time by the tenses of its participles.
17. The perfect passive participle is used with forms of *sum* to make the perfect passive tenses, and in these forms it agrees with the subject in gender and number.
18. The deponent verbs, passive in form but active in meaning, have a perfect participle with active meaning (“having tried”).
19. So the deponent verbs supply what the other Latin verbs lack, a perfect active participle.
20. The future active participle signifies an action that is about to take place or is intended (“about to die”).
21. The future passive participle, called the **gerundive**, signifies something that is to be done or ought to be done, and with a form of *sum* it expresses necessity or obligation.
22. Many English words come from Latin gerundives (“agenda,” “memorandum”).
23. English has two participles: the **present participle**, ending in *-ing*, and the **past participle**, ending in *-ed* in regular verbs and having various forms in irregular verbs.
24. The past participle is used to form the perfect tenses and the passive voice, and the present participle to form the progressive.
25. The English participles are also used as adjectives, as the Latin ones are.
26. A participle with its object or modifiers makes a **participial phrase**, and such a phrase must refer clearly to a noun in the sentence.
27. The English form in *-ing* is used in two ways: as a participle when it describes a noun, and as a **gerund**, a verbal noun, when it names the action as a thing.
28. The gerund is the form in which a verb is used in the mode of a noun, since the same activity can be signified as a thing or as an action.
29. Latin distinguishes the two by their forms: the Latin **gerund** is a verbal noun declined only in the oblique cases, and for the nominative Latin uses the infinitive.
30. Whether an English *-ing* form is a participle or a gerund is decided, like every question of the parts of speech, by how the word is used in its sentence.
31. The modists explain the participle as signifying by the mode of becoming united with the substance, whereas the verb signifies by the mode of becoming as distinct from the substance.
32. Because it signifies becoming, the participle has the accidents that follow from becoming, tense and signification (voice), as the verb has.
33. Because it signifies its action as united with a substance, it has the accidents by which a word is united with a noun, gender, number, and case, as the adjective has.
34. The participle lacks mood and person, because these belong to the verb insofar as it is said of a subject and signifies the inclination of the speaker’s mind, and the participle is not said of a subject but adheres to it.
35. So the modists account for the participle’s accidents, both those it has and those it lacks, by its mode of signifying, and with the participle the declinable parts of speech are complete.

## The Adverb (`iii.adverb`)

1. The adverb is the first of the four indeclinable parts of speech, which keep one form.
2. Donatus defines the adverb as a part of speech that, added to a verb, makes its meaning clear and completes it; its name, *adverbium*, means “what is added to the verb.”
3. The modists explain the adverb as signifying by the mode of determining a word that signifies as becoming.
4. Donatus gives the adverb three accidents: signification, comparison, and figure.
5. The adverb has no case, number, gender, tense, or person, because it does not signify a thing as stable or as becoming but determines another word.
6. Its accidents concern what kind of determination it gives (signification), how much (comparison), and how it is made (figure).
7. The kind of meaning that an adverb adds is its **signification**.
8. The principal kinds of adverb by signification are adverbs of place, time, manner, quantity and number, negation and affirmation, questioning, and doubt; Donatus’s list also includes adverbs of wishing, of exhorting, and of comparison.
9. So the adverbs are a large and varied class, united not by what they signify but by the way they signify: each determines another word.
10. Many adverbs are derived from adjectives: in Latin, adjectives of the first and second declensions form adverbs in *-ē*, and adjectives of the third declension form adverbs in *-iter*.
11. Some Latin adverbs have the form of a case of the adjective.
12. English forms most adverbs of manner by adding “-ly” to the adjective, but many common adverbs have no ending, and some words have the same form as adjective and adverb.
13. So, as with every part of speech, the form does not always decide whether a word is an adverb; the use does.
14. Adverbs derived from adjectives of quality can be compared, as the adjectives are.
15. English compares most adverbs with “more” and “most”; a few short adverbs take “-er” and “-est”; and some are irregular.
16. In Latin the comparative adverb has the form of the neuter singular of the comparative adjective, and the superlative ends in *-ē*; adverbs from irregularly compared adjectives follow their adjectives.
17. Adverbs that signify place, time, or negation cannot be compared, because what they signify does not admit more and less.
18. The adverb has **figure**: it is simple or compound.
19. Many Latin adverbs are compounds of a preposition and a case, and English has many compound adverbs, some written as one word and some as several.
20. A group of words that does the work of an adverb is called an **adverbial phrase**, and whole phrases and clauses can do the work of an adverb.
21. Adverbs determine verbs, but also adjectives, other adverbs, and whole sentences.
22. Donatus defines the adverb by its relation to the verb because that is its principal use; Priscian explains that it is added to the verb chiefly, but also to the other parts that signify something like an action or a quality.
23. In English the position of an adverb can change the meaning of a sentence, so the adverb must be placed next to the word it is meant to determine.
24. An adverb placed where it determines the wrong word causes ambiguity.
25. Several words can be adverbs in one sentence and other parts of speech in another.
26. The preposition takes an object, the conjunction introduces a clause, and the adverb does neither.
27. Priscian notes that the Greeks placed the interjection under the adverb, and that the Latins separated it because it shows a feeling of the mind even without a verb.
28. So the boundaries between the indeclinable parts are drawn by their ways of signifying, and the same word may cross them.

## The Preposition (`iii.preposition`)

1. A preposition signifies nothing complete by itself; it relates the noun after it to the rest of the sentence.
2. The preposition is placed before other parts of speech and completes, changes, or lessens their meaning, and the modists explain it as relating a word that has case to an action; its name, *praepositiō*, means “placing before.”
3. Donatus gives the preposition only one accident, **case**: each Latin preposition is joined to a noun in a particular case.
4. The preposition does not itself have case, as the noun does; it requires a case in the word it governs.
5. So to learn a Latin preposition is to learn its case along with its meaning.
6. Most Latin prepositions take the accusative, among them *ad*, *ante*, *apud*, *contrā*, *inter*, *per*, *post*, and *trāns*.
7. Many of these prepositions have given English words through their use in compounds.
8. A smaller group takes the ablative: *ā* or *ab*, *cum*, *dē*, *ē* or *ex*, *prō*, and *sine*.
9. The forms *ā* and *ē* are used before consonants, and *ab* and *ex* before vowels and often before consonants as well.
10. The doer in a passive sentence is expressed by *ā* or *ab* with the ablative.
11. A few prepositions, chiefly *in* and *sub*, take either the accusative or the ablative, with a difference of meaning.
12. With the accusative they signify motion toward a place, and with the ablative rest in a place, a difference that English shows by two prepositions (“into” and “in”).
13. So case and preposition together signify a relation that neither signifies alone, which shows why the preposition’s one accident is case.
14. English prepositions are more numerous than Latin ones, because English uses them where Latin uses case endings: “of” for the genitive, “to” and “for” for the dative, and “by,” “with,” and “from” for the ablative.
15. An English preposition takes its object in the objective case when the object is a pronoun, and careful usage prefers “for her and me” to “for her and I.”
16. English also allows a preposition at the end of a clause; some writers since the seventeenth century have objected to this on the model of Latin, but the usage is long established in English and is found in its best writers.
17. Prepositions are used not only as separate words but in **composition**, that is, joined to the beginning of other words, where each changes the meaning.
18. This is why Donatus says that the preposition, placed before other parts of speech, completes, changes, or lessens their meaning.
19. In composition the last consonant of the preposition often changes to match the first consonant of the word, a change called **assimilation**.
20. English keeps the assimilated forms in words borrowed from Latin, which is why their double consonants are spelled as they are.
21. A word is a preposition when it is placed before a noun or pronoun and relates it to another word of the sentence.
22. The relation by which a preposition requires a particular case in its noun is called **government** (Latin *regimen*): the preposition governs its noun.
23. Verbs and some adjectives also govern particular cases, and government is one of the principal relations by which words are joined in a sentence.

## The Conjunction (`iii.conjunction`)

1. A conjunction may join single words, phrases, or whole clauses.
2. The conjunction joins and orders the thought, and the modists explain it as signifying by the mode of joining two terms.
3. Priscian defines the conjunction as an indeclinable part of speech that joins other parts of speech and shows their force or their order.
4. Donatus gives the conjunction three accidents: **power** (*potestas*), the kind of relation the conjunction signifies; **figure**, whether it is simple or compound; and **order**, its position in relation to the words it joins.
5. Conjunctions differ in the relation they signify: they may simply join, offer a choice, make one thing the condition of another, or draw one thing from another.
6. Donatus divides the conjunctions by their power into five kinds: **copulative**, which join; **disjunctive**, which separate or offer a choice; **expletive**, which fill out or strengthen the sense; **causal**, which state a cause or condition; and **rational**, which draw a conclusion or give a reason.
7. English conjunctions can be classed in the same way.
8. So the power of a conjunction is the logical relation it signifies between the things it joins, and the reader who knows that relation understands how the thought proceeds.
9. Modern grammars divide the conjunctions by the kind of joining rather than by its meaning.
10. **Coordinating** conjunctions join words or clauses of equal rank, so that neither depends on the other, and each clause could stand as a sentence by itself.
11. **Subordinating** conjunctions join a dependent clause to the clause on which it depends, and the dependent clause cannot stand by itself.
12. The two divisions do not conflict: a causal conjunction in Donatus’s sense, such as “if,” is a subordinating conjunction in the modern sense.
13. Conjunctions differ in their position, and Donatus calls this accident **order**: some are placed before the word they join, some after it, and some either way.
14. Latin conjunctions placed after the first word of their clause are called **postpositive**, and English has a few words that behave in a similar way (“however”).
15. A reader of Latin who expects every conjunction at the beginning of its clause will misread sentences with postpositive conjunctions.
16. The conjunction, like the other parts, has **figure**: it is simple or compound.
17. In English some compounds are written as one word, and groups such as “so that” and “in order that” serve as conjunctions.
18. Compound conjunctions show how the parts of speech can combine, yet the compound is still one word of one part of speech.
19. Some conjunctions are used in pairs, one in each of the two parts they join; they are called **correlative** conjunctions (“both … and,” “either … or”).
20. The two members of a correlative pair should join words or phrases of the same kind, so the conjunction requires that the things it joins be alike in construction.
21. Some words are conjunctions in one use and other parts of speech in another (English “that” is a conjunction, a relative pronoun, or a demonstrative).
22. The test is what the word is doing in the sentence: a conjunction joins and refers to nothing; a relative pronoun joins and also refers to an antecedent; a preposition takes a noun as its object; and an adverb determines another word without joining anything.

## The Interjection (`iii.interjection`)

1. The last of the eight parts of speech is the **interjection** (*interiectiō*, “what is thrown in”).
2. Donatus defines it as a part of speech that signifies a feeling of the mind by an unformed utterance.
3. It is called an interjection because it is thrown into the sentence without being constructed with the other words.
4. The Greek grammarians placed the interjection under the adverb, but the Latin grammarians counted it as a part of its own.
5. Donatus gives the reason that a verb does not immediately follow words of this kind, and Priscian adds that the interjection shows a complete feeling of the mind even without a verb.
6. Interjections signify different kinds of feeling, such as joy, grief, wonder, pain, disgust, and surprise, and some serve to call someone.
7. Donatus gives the interjection one accident, **signification**: it signifies joy, grief, wonder, fear, or any similar feeling.
8. So the interjection, like the other indeclinable parts, has only the accident that concerns what kind of thing it signifies.
9. Interjections differ from language to language, as other words do, so one who learns another language must learn its interjections.
10. This shows that interjections signify by imposition, not only by nature.
11. A groan signifies pain by nature and is much the same among all peoples, but an interjection, though it may resemble a natural sound, has a form fixed by the custom of each language, so it is a word, articulate and written, and a part of speech.
12. The interjection “O” (Latin *ō*) is used with the vocative to address someone with feeling.
13. Some interjections are followed by other cases as well, as Latin *heu* by the accusative and *ei* by the dative.
14. In these uses the interjection comes closer to being constructed with the other words, but it is still not a member of the sentence in the way the noun and the verb are.
15. With the interjection the eight parts of speech are complete.
16. The noun and the pronoun signify by the mode of a stable thing, the verb and the participle by the mode of becoming, and the four indeclinable parts by determining, relating, joining, or expressing feeling.
17. The accidents are gender, number, and case in the noun and the pronoun; tense, mood, voice, person, and number in the verb; and in the indeclinable parts only their kinds of signification, their comparison, their figure, and their order.
18. The parts of speech are the material of the sentence, as letters and syllables are the material of the word, and how the parts are put together so that the sentence is correct and complete is the subject of construction.
