## Derivation (`iv.derivation`)

1. Every word has a species: it is either primitive, not formed from another word, or derived, formed from another.
2. **Derivation** is the process by which a derived word is formed from a primitive, usually by adding an ending called a **suffix** (“neighbor,” “neighborhood”).
3. A derived word keeps the meaning of its primitive and adds to it a further meaning that the suffix signifies.
4. Modern grammars also count prefixes as means of derivation, but the Latin grammarians treated a word with a prefix as compound, because most Latin prefixes are prepositions.
5. Latin forms most of its derived nouns from verbs and adjectives with a small number of regular suffixes.
6. The suffix *-tor* forms the **agent**, the one who does the action.
7. The suffix *-tiō* forms the **action**.
8. The suffixes *-tās* and *-tūdō* form the **quality**.
9. The suffixes *-ulus*, *-culus*, and *-ellus* form **diminutives**, which signify something small.
10. A student who knows the Latin suffixes can often recognize the structure of English words borrowed from Latin.
11. Latin forms adjectives by suffixes in the same way: *-ōsus* signifies “full of”; *-ālis* and *-āris* signify “belonging to” or “of the kind of”; *-bilis* signifies what can be done; *-eus* signifies the material; and *-ānus* signifies belonging to a place or person.
12. The suffix *-ālis* has the variant *-āris*, used when the stem already contains an *l*, and this is an example of the regular changes of form that accompany derivation, which the grammarians recorded as rules of analogy.
13. The desiderative, the frequentative, and the inchoative, which Donatus counts among the forms of the verb, are derived verbs formed from other verbs by regular suffixes.
14. Verbs formed from nouns and adjectives are called **denominative** verbs (“from a noun”).
15. Most Latin denominative verbs belong to the first conjugation, the one to which new Latin verbs were added; English has a parallel in its verbs in “-ize” and “-ify.”
16. English has its own suffixes, inherited from Old English, and these native suffixes are among the most productive, that is, the most freely used to make new words.
17. “-ness” forms nouns of quality from adjectives; “-dom,” “-hood,” and “-ship” form nouns of state or office; “-ful” and “-less” form adjectives meaning “full of” and “without”; “-ly” forms adverbs and some adjectives; “-en” forms verbs from adjectives; and “-er” forms the agent.
18. The native suffixes correspond in meaning to Latin ones, so English often has two words for the same idea, one with a native suffix and one borrowed from Latin (“boldness” and “audacity”).
19. English has also borrowed many suffixes from Latin, Greek, and French, usually in words borrowed whole and then extended to new words.
20. Some borrowed suffixes now form English words from native roots, so that a word may be part English and part Latin; such words are called **hybrids**.
21. Some writers have objected to hybrids, but many are well established, and the criterion of usage decides their correctness.
22. English also forms new words without adding any suffix, by using a word of one part of speech as another without change of form; this is called **zero-derivation** or **conversion**.
23. Conversion is how many English pairs of words of one form and different parts of speech arose.
24. A word can also be formed by removing what looks like a suffix, and this is called **back-formation** (“to edit” from “editor”).
25. Back-formation shows that speakers recognize the patterns of derivation and apply them in reverse, even when the history of the word is different.
26. Derivation is one of the clearest examples of analogy, the regular patterns by which words are formed, which supports reason among the criteria of correct speech.
27. Because suffixes have regular meanings, a speaker can form a new word by an existing pattern and be understood.
28. Vossius gathered the Latin patterns of derivation into a systematic order, so that a student could recognize the formation of words not met before.
29. But analogy is limited by usage, since not every word that would follow a pattern exists, so derivation is governed by both analogy and usage.
30. A word formed against usage can be a barbarism.

## Composition (`iv.composition`)

1. **Composition** is the joining of two or more words, or parts of words, to make one word.
2. Composition differs from derivation because in composition each part can usually stand as a word by itself, whereas in derivation the suffix cannot (“sunrise” is compound, “booklet” derived).
3. Figure is the accident by which a word is simple or compound, and Donatus gives four ways of compounding: from two whole words, from two changed words, from a whole word and a changed one, and from a changed word and a whole one.
4. The most common Latin compounds are verbs made with a preposition, and in them the vowel of the verb is often weakened.
5. English keeps these weakened forms in words borrowed from such compounds.
6. Latin also forms compound nouns and adjectives from a noun and a verbal root, and adjectives from an adjective and a noun.
7. Latin compounds of these kinds are fewer than Greek or German ones, and the Latin poets used them as a mark of high style.
8. Greek forms compounds much more freely than Latin, and English has taken many learned words from Greek compounds or formed new ones on the Greek model.
9. Modern scientists have continued to form words in this way, often joining Greek parts that the Greeks never joined.
10. In English such words are learned as single words and most speakers do not recognize their parts, but a student who knows the parts can understand many of them on first meeting them.
11. English forms compounds of many kinds: noun and noun; adjective and noun; verb and noun; a noun and an agent noun (a noun that names the doer of an action); and an adjective and a participle.
12. In most English compounds the last part is the principal one, and the first part determines it.
13. The principal part is often called the **head** of the compound, so the order of the parts matters and the meaning depends on it (a “houseboat” is a boat, a “boathouse” a house).
14. German forms compounds very freely and writes them as one word, and, as in English, the last part is the head.
15. German compounds can be very long, because a compound can itself become part of a larger compound.
16. English expresses the same meanings either by compounds written as separate words or by phrases with prepositions.
17. The parts of a word do not signify by themselves within it, and the meaning of a compound is not always the sum of the meanings of its parts.
18. Over time a compound may come to signify something that its parts no longer suggest, and then it must be learned as a single word.
19. English distinguishes a compound from a phrase by its stress, whereas Latin and German, which write compounds as one word with one accent, make the distinction in writing as well as in speech.
20. So a compound is recognized by its unity as a word, in meaning, in accent, and often in spelling, not by the separate meanings of its parts.
21. Many words are formed by both composition and derivation, in an order that can be traced, so that one word can be both compound and derived.
22. The formation of a word can be analyzed step by step, as a sentence can, and the analysis shows how the meaning of the whole is built from its parts.
23. This analysis concerns how a word is made in the language as it is, whereas the history of where a word came from and how its form and meaning changed belongs to etymology.

## Etymology (`iv.etymology`)

1. **Etymology** is the study of how a word came to have its form and meaning, that is, the historical question of where a word came from.
2. The name is Greek, from *etymon* (“true”) and *logos* (“account”), so it means “an account of the true sense” of a word; Cicero called it *notatio*.
3. Etymology is one of the supports of reason among the criteria of correctness, and Quintilian holds that it is sometimes of the greatest use when a word needs interpretation.
4. So etymology serves the grammarian both in explaining the authors and in judging what is correct.
5. The oldest extended discussion of etymology is Plato’s *Cratylus*, in which Socrates offers many etymologies, some of them plainly playful.
6. The dialogue raises the question that every etymologist must answer, whether the origin of a word shows the true nature of the thing it names.
7. Plato’s answer is that realities are to be learned much better through themselves than through their names.
8. The origin of a word can be explained at different levels of knowledge: some origins are plain to any speaker, others need knowledge of older usage or other languages, and some cannot be found with certainty at all.
9. Varro distinguishes four levels at which the origin of a word can be explained: the lowest, which ordinary people reach; the second, reached by grammar, which shows how the poets made their words; the third, reached by philosophy, which explains the words of common usage; and the fourth, where he says he may reach only opinion, not knowledge.
10. Varro recognized that words change their form over time, so that the origin of a word is often hidden by later changes of letters and syllables.
11. Many of Varro’s etymologies are wrong by modern standards, but his method of collecting old forms, old writers, and related words is the beginning of the historical study of Latin.
12. Isidore made etymology the method of a whole encyclopedia, explaining the things of every art and science by the origins of their names.
13. Isidore defines etymology as the origin of words, when the force of a verb or noun is found by interpretation, and holds that knowing the origin of a word lets us understand its force more quickly.
14. Some of Isidore’s etymologies are still accepted, but many are guesses from a likeness of sound, and he himself says that not all words were imposed with a reason that can be found.
15. An explanation of where a word came from can be ingenious and still be false; such an explanation, with no evidence to support it, is a false etymology.
16. Quintilian accepts many etymologies but warns against the excesses of those who pursue them, whose ingenuity leads to absurdities.
17. His chief example is the derivation of a word from its opposite, and *lūcus ā nōn lūcendō* has become proverbial for an absurd explanation.
18. Quintilian’s caution is sound in principle, but it was not always right in its judgments.
19. In the nineteenth century scholars found a more reliable method: by comparing the oldest forms of related languages, they found that sounds change according to regular patterns, called **sound laws**.
20. The best known is Grimm’s law, which describes how certain consonants of the parent language changed in the Germanic languages, so that where Latin has *p*, *t*, and *c*, English has *f*, *th*, and *h* in inherited words.
21. Because these changes are regular, an etymology must now account for every sound of the word by such laws, not by a mere likeness.
22. So words that differ in sound can be shown to be the same word (“foot” and *pēs*), and words that look alike can be shown to be unrelated.
23. A **cognate** (“born together”) is a word that has descended in each language from a common ancestor in the parent language.
24. A **borrowed word** or **loanword** is a word that one language has taken from another.
25. English often has a native word and a borrowed word from the same root, such as “mother” and “maternal”; the native word has undergone the sound changes of English, whereas the borrowed word was taken much later and keeps its foreign form.
26. This is why English has so many pairs of a common native word and a learned adjective.
27. English vocabulary has several layers: a Germanic core inherited from Old English, the most common words; thousands of words borrowed from French after the Norman Conquest, especially in government, law, religion, and cooking; and many learned words borrowed directly from Latin and Greek from the Renaissance onward.
28. When English has borrowed the same Latin word twice, once through French and once directly, the two forms are called **doublets** (“frail” and “fragile”).
29. Speakers sometimes reshape an unfamiliar word to resemble familiar ones, and this is called **folk etymology**.
30. The meaning of a word can change greatly over time, so the original meaning of a word is not its true or present meaning.
31. To argue that a word must mean today what its ancestor meant is a fallacy, sometimes called the **etymological fallacy**.
32. The present meaning is fixed by usage, and etymology explains how that meaning came about but does not overrule it.
33. The great historical dictionaries record what etymology has established: for each word, its origin, its earliest recorded uses with dated quotations, and the history of its meanings.
34. For Latin, Vossius’s etymological dictionary gathered the ancient and Renaissance learning, and modern etymological dictionaries have corrected and extended it.
35. The studies of derivation, composition, and etymology complete the grammar of the single word.
36. In 1786 Sir William Jones observed that Sanskrit bears to Greek and Latin a likeness, in the roots of its verbs and in its grammatical forms, too strong to have come about by chance, so that all three must have sprung from some common source, which perhaps no longer exists.
37. This observation began the study of the history and relations of languages.
38. Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, and the Germanic, Celtic, and Slavic languages descend from a common parent language, now called Proto-Indo-European, which was never written.
39. The comparative etymology that follows, which explains cognates such as English “foot” and Latin *pēs*, rests on this discovery.
40. The classical tradition would receive this discovery as it receives any well-founded etymology: it corrects many derivations of Varro and Isidore but leaves the art of grammar as it was.
41. In the nineteenth century comparative grammar became the principal form of the study of language.
42. Bopp compared the conjugation of the verb in Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian, and the Germanic languages.
43. The Neogrammarians held that sound changes operate without exception, so that every apparent exception must have its own explanation.
44. So the nineteenth century discovered that languages have histories and change according to regular laws, a fact that the modists had no obvious place for.
45. Latin was not a fixed system outside time but a stage between an older language and the Romance languages that descended from it, and a grammar derived from unchanging modes of being does not plainly predict that a language should change at all.
46. Boethius of Dacia considered the objection that a science must be of what is necessary and unchanging.
47. He answered that there is a true science of things that come to be and pass away, through the causes that always accompany them; otherwise there would be a science only of things that never change.
48. So the classical account can admit that languages change, as long as the change follows causes that can be known: the modes of signifying would be the constant part, and the sounds and forms that change would be the accidents.
49. Whether this answer reaches far enough to account for the whole history of the Indo-European languages is a fair question, and it is one of the points at which the classical art and modern linguistics meet.
50. A **synchronic** study describes a language as a system at one time; a **diachronic** study describes how a language changes through time.
51. Saussure held that the two must be kept apart, and that the system of a language at a given moment can be described without reference to its history.
52. The nineteenth-century philologists had studied languages chiefly diachronically, and Saussure’s distinction made the synchronic description of a language the principal task of linguistics.
53. The classical grammarian had described the Latin of the authors as a system, but he also drew on its history, through the criterion of antiquity and through etymology.
