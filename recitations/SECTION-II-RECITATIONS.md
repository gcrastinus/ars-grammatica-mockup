## Voice (*vox*) (`ii.voice`)

1. The matter of speech is the word considered as a sound, the material of which spoken words are made, and the first thing to consider is the voice itself.
2. Voice, as the grammarian first considers it, is air set in motion so that it can be heard, whether or not anyone is there to hear it on a given occasion.
3. Donatus defines voice as the motion of air perceptible to hearing, as far as lies in itself.
4. That phrase means that voice is the kind of thing that can be heard, even if on some occasion no one is there to hear it.
5. Not every sound is voice in the strict sense: thunder is a sound that no living thing utters, and a sneeze, though made by a living thing, is not uttered in order to show anything.
6. In the strict sense, voice is a sound made by a living being with some act of imagination, so as to signify something (Aristotle’s example of what is not voice is the cough).
7. The grammarian does not study how the throat and tongue produce the voice, which belongs to natural philosophy and to phonetics; he studies the voice insofar as it becomes speech.
8. Some voice is made of distinct sounds that letters can record, and some is not, so that the word “river” can be written down again, whereas a sigh cannot.
9. Donatus’s first division is that every voice is either articulate or confused: articulate voice can be grasped in letters, and confused voice cannot be written.
10. The usual English term for Donatus’s confused voice is inarticulate voice.
11. Whether a sound can be written and whether it signifies are two different questions, so a gasp may show something but not be written, whereas “zib” can be written and means nothing.
12. If a voice is joined to some meaning in the mind of the speaker, Priscian calls it articulate; if it can be written, he calls it literate (Latin *litterata*, from *littera*, “letter”).
13. Priscian’s word “articulate” does not mean what Donatus’s means, so it matters which grammarian is speaking.
14. Priscian’s two questions give four kinds of voice: voice that signifies and can be written, voice that signifies but cannot be written, voice that can be written but signifies nothing, and voice that neither signifies nor can be written.
15. In this course “articulate” keeps Donatus’s sense, so articulate voice is voice that can be written, and inarticulate voice is voice that cannot.
16. That sense names the voice the matter of speech is studied in, a voice of distinct sounds that letters can record; whether the voice also signifies is the further question that tells what a word is.
17. Voice either signifies or does not, and voice that signifies does so either by nature or by imposition.
18. Articulate voice is not yet a word, since a letter pronounced alone can be written but signifies nothing.
19. Only articulate voice that signifies by imposition is a word, and the word is therefore the least part of an expression that signifies, whereas the voice, the letter, and the syllable are its material.
20. A groan signifies by nature and is inarticulate, so it is not a word, and for the same reason it cannot be a barbarism or a solecism.
21. A barbarism is an error in a single word, and a solecism is an error in the arrangement of words.
22. Both errors presuppose words, so below the word there is nothing that can be correctly or incorrectly formed in this sense (“taught” and “teached”).
23. Each language uses only a selection of articulate sounds, and the selections differ (English has “th,” which Latin did not).
24. The articulate voice of a language is the set of sounds that its speakers have learned to distinguish and to combine.
25. Phonetics studies the sounds of speech as sounds, how they are produced, how they travel, and how they are heard, whatever language they belong to.
26. Phonology studies which differences of sound a particular language treats as distinguishing one word from another, and which differences it passes over.
27. So the matter of speech is studied from the voice on to the letter, the syllable, quantity and accent, the marks of pause, and the measures of verse.

## The Letter and Orthography (`ii.letter`)

1. A letter (Latin *littera*) is the least part of articulate voice, as Donatus defines it.
2. The classical grammarians used the word “letter” both for the sound and for the written mark, because in Latin the two correspond closely.
3. A letter signifies nothing by itself, so it belongs to the material of speech and is not yet a word.
4. The sound is often called a phoneme and the written mark a grapheme, but the grammarian’s word “letter” covers both, and the two are distinguished where it matters.
5. Three things belong to every letter: its name, its figure, and its power.
6. The grammarians call these three the accidents of the letter (Latin *accidentia*), the properties by which one letter differs from another.
7. The name of a letter is what we call it when we speak of it.
8. The figure of a letter is the shape in which it is written.
9. The power of a letter is the sound that the letter stands for.
10. One figure may have more than one power, and one power may be written with more than one figure (English “c” in “cat” and in “city”).
11. The letters are divided first into vowels and consonants.
12. A vowel (Latin *vocalis*, from *vox*, “voice”) is a letter that can be pronounced by itself and can make a syllable by itself.
13. Latin has five vowels, *a*, *e*, *i*, *o*, and *u*, and *y* in words taken from Greek; English writes the same vowel letters.
14. A vowel letter is not the same thing as a vowel sound, since English has many more vowel sounds than vowel letters to write them.
15. A consonant (Latin *consonans*, “sounding with”) is a letter that cannot make a syllable without a vowel.
16. A semivowel is a consonant that can be partly pronounced without a vowel, and a mute is a consonant that cannot be pronounced at all without a vowel.
17. The name of a semivowel begins with a vowel and ends with the letter’s own sound, whereas the name of a mute begins with its own sound and ends with a vowel.
18. Among the semivowels, *l*, *m*, *n*, and *r* are called liquids (Latin *liquidae*), because they flow easily after another consonant.
19. The letter *x* is a double letter, because it stands for the two sounds of *c* and *s*, and *z* was counted double as well.
20. Many grammarians hold that *h* is not a letter but a mark of breathing (Latin *aspiratio*), since it adds breath to a vowel and makes no sound of its own.
21. An alphabet need not have exactly the letters a language needs: some letters may be superfluous, and some sounds may have no letter of their own.
22. Latin *k* survived in only a few words, because *c* already had the same power, and Quintilian raises the same question about *q*, *x*, and the mark of breathing.
23. Latin *i* and *u* each stood for a vowel and for a consonant, and the separate figures *j* and *v* were distinguished by printers and scholars of the Renaissance.
24. In this course Latin is pronounced in the restored classical pronunciation, the pronunciation reconstructed for educated Roman speech in the time of Cicero.
25. The church pronunciation, which is closer to Italian, is also traditional, but it is not the pronunciation used in this course.
26. Every Latin vowel is either long or short, and a long vowel is held about twice as long as a short one, a difference that can change the word (*os*, “bone,” and *ōs*, “mouth”).
27. Ordinary Latin spelling does not show that difference, so the Latin examples in this course mark every long vowel with a macron, and in those examples a vowel without a macron is short.
28. Quotations from Latin authors are given as the editions print them, without macrons.
29. The Latin vowels have roughly the sounds of “father,” of “they” and “pet,” of “machine” and “pit,” of “note” and “pot,” and of “rule” and “put,” the first of each pair being the long vowel.
30. A diphthong (Greek *diphthongos*, “double sound”) is two vowel sounds pronounced in one syllable, the voice gliding from one to the other.
31. The common Latin diphthongs are *ae*, *au*, and *oe*, pronounced with the vowel sounds of “aisle,” “house,” and “boy”.
32. In the restored pronunciation *c* and *g* are always hard, as in “come” and “go”.
33. Consonantal *u*, often printed *v*, is pronounced as English “w,” and consonantal *i*, often printed *j*, as English “y”.
34. Latin *s* is always voiceless, *r* is trilled, and a doubled consonant is held longer than a single one.
35. Orthography (Greek *orthographia*, “correct writing”) is the part of grammar that concerns correct spelling.
36. Spelling should follow pronunciation, because letters exist to record sounds, but only within the limits of usage, since the accepted spelling is part of the custom of the language.
37. Usage, which is one of the criteria of correct speech, here governs writing as well as speaking.
38. In Latin the spelling follows the sound closely, whereas in English the relation of spelling to sound is much looser.
39. In English the same sound may be spelled in more than one way, the same spelling may have more than one sound, and some letters are no longer pronounced.
40. English spelling was largely fixed in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, while the pronunciation continued to change, so the spelling often records an older pronunciation of the word (the Great Vowel Shift).
41. English spelling sometimes records the origin of a word rather than its present sound (“debt,” from Latin *debitum*).
42. So English spelling serves two purposes: it records the sound, and it records the history of words and the relations among them.
43. Letters are not pronounced one by one but are joined into syllables, each of which must contain a vowel or a diphthong, and the grouping depends on vowel and consonant, liquid, double letter, and length.

## The Syllable (`ii.syllable`)

1. A syllable (Greek *syllabē*, “what is taken together”) is a group of letters pronounced in one utterance of the voice, or the utterance of a single vowel.
2. Donatus defines the syllable as a combination of letters, or one vowel, capable of length, and Priscian adds that its letters are uttered under one accent and in one breath.
3. The syllable signifies nothing by itself; it is part of the material of speech, and it is the unit in which speech is pronounced and measured.
4. Every syllable has one vowel or one diphthong at its center, and consonants may stand before that center, after it, or on both sides.
5. In Latin, therefore, the number of syllables in a word is the number of its vowels and diphthongs.
6. A diphthong counts as one vowel, because the two vowels are pronounced in one syllable, whereas two vowels that are not a diphthong make two syllables.
7. Four accidents belong to every syllable: its accent, its breathing, its length, and the number of its letters.
8. The accent of a syllable (*tenor*) is the raising or lowering of the voice upon it.
9. The breathing of a syllable (*spiritus*) is whether it is pronounced with an *h* or without one.
10. The length of a syllable (*tempus*, “time”) is whether it is long or short, and this length is the foundation of Latin verse.
11. The number of letters is the count of letters in the syllable, which in Latin runs from one letter to as many as six.
12. A syllable that ends in a vowel is called open, and a syllable that ends in a consonant is called closed (*pa-ter*, *por-ta*).
13. The length of the vowel and the length of the syllable must be kept apart, since a syllable with a short vowel is short if it is open and long if a consonant closes it.
14. English syllables are counted by vowel sounds, not by vowel letters, so a silent letter does not make a syllable and two vowel sounds make two.
15. English allows larger groups of consonants than Latin does: as many as three may begin a syllable, and as many as four may end one.
16. When three consonants begin an English syllable, the first of them is always *s*.
17. A group of consonants that cannot begin an English word is not pronounced when a borrowed word brings it in (“psalm”).
18. English adds one qualification: in some unstressed syllables a liquid or a nasal consonant takes the place of the vowel, and that consonant is called syllabic (“button”).
19. Latin, as the grammarians describe it, has no syllabic consonants, so every Latin syllable has a vowel or a diphthong at its center.
20. In writing, an English word broken at the end of a line is divided by the conventions of the dictionaries, which weigh both the sound and the parts of the word, and that division need not match the spoken syllables.
21. The short vowels of English never end a word, and a stressed short vowel does not end a syllable, so the following consonant stays with it (“hab-it,” whereas Latin *habet* is *ha-bet*).
22. A long English vowel sound may end a syllable, but no single rule of sound settles the division of every English word.
23. In Latin a single consonant between two vowels goes with the following vowel, and the syllable before that consonant is open (*ro-ta*).
24. When two consonants stand between two vowels, they are usually divided, and the syllable before them is closed (*for-tis*); when three stand together, the last usually goes with the following vowel.
25. A mute followed by *l* or *r* usually goes with the following vowel, so the syllable before them stays open (*pa-tris*), though a poet may divide the two consonants, and the length of the syllable then changes.
26. The double letter *x* counts as two consonants, so the syllable that it closes is closed in sound.
27. The pair *qu* counts as one consonant, whereas the breathing *h* does not count as a consonant at all.
28. A compound word is divided between its parts.
29. The syllable is thus the least unit in which speech is pronounced: it has a vowel or a diphthong at its center, it is open or closed, and length is one of its accidents.
30. In Latin that length fixes where the accent falls and is the measure on which verse is built, so dividing a word into syllables is the first step in pronouncing it and in reading verse.

## Quantity and Accent (`ii.quantity`)

1. Quantity is how long a syllable is held, and accent is how the voice rises, or gains force, on one syllable; they are two accidents of the syllable.
2. Latin verse is built on quantity, and the Latin accent is placed by quantity, whereas English verse measures stress.
3. English length follows the stress and the sounds beside the vowel, and it does not distinguish words as Latin quantity does (*mālum*, “apple,” and *malum*, “evil”).
4. In Latin every syllable is long or short, a long syllable taking two units of time and a short syllable one, and the name is Latin *quantitas*, “how much”.
5. Donatus also names the common syllable (*communis*), which may be treated as long or as short.
6. Quantity is measured from the beginning of the vowel to the end of the syllable, so a consonant before the vowel adds nothing.
7. In these reckonings *h* is not a consonant, nor is the *u* after *q*, and sometimes after *g* or *s*.
8. A syllable is long by nature when its vowel is long or when it contains a diphthong, and long by position when a consonant closes it.
9. A vowel that comes from a diphthong is long, and so is a vowel formed by contraction, two vowels merged into one.
10. The length of a Latin vowel is not shown by ordinary spelling, and it must be learned with each word.
11. A short vowel is nevertheless long by position when two consonants follow it, or *x* or *z*, unless those consonants are a mute followed by *l* or *r*.
12. A mute and a liquid usually go with the following vowel, so the syllable stays open and short, though a poet may treat it as long.
13. In a syllable long by position the vowel itself may still be short; what is long is the syllable, not the vowel.
14. “Position” does not mean the place of the vowel in the word: Latin *positio* translates Greek *thesei*, “by convention,” as against nature.
15. The length of a vowel inside a syllable long by position is hidden quantity, and such a vowel is pronounced short unless it is known to be long.
16. Measuring a line counts syllables and does not reveal that hidden length of the vowel.
17. A vowel before *ns* or *nf* is long, a vowel before *gn* is held to be long though not certainly in every word, and a vowel before *nd* or *nt* is regularly short.
18. Those rules decide the vowel, for pronunciation; the syllable is long by position in each case, and the line is measured without them.
19. A syllable is short when its vowel is short and the syllable is open, no more than one consonant standing between that vowel and the next.
20. A vowel placed directly before another vowel is usually short, and an *h* between them is not a consonant.
21. Where a mute and a liquid may either be divided or kept together, the syllable is common, a license of the language rather than an error in the poet.
22. In verse the words run on as one stream, so a final syllable that has a short vowel and ends in a consonant takes its quantity from the next word.
23. Before a vowel, and before *h*, that syllable is short; before a consonant it is long by position; and the line is measured across the divisions of the words.
24. Consonant *i* between vowels lengthens the syllable before it, because the voice passes through a vowel *i* before the consonant (*maior*).
25. The length belongs to the syllable whether or not the vowel is long, and this course marks no macron on that vowel.
26. Latin *accentus*, from *ad* and *cantus*, “song,” translates Greek *prosōidia*, and the name takes accent for a rising and falling of the voice.
27. There are three accents: the acute, which raises the voice; the grave, which lowers it; and the circumflex, which raises and lowers it on the same syllable.
28. To place the accent on the wrong syllable is an error in one word, and it was counted a kind of barbarism.
29. The three accents were worked out for Greek, where accent was pitch, and the scholars of Alexandria, about 200 BC, made the marks so that readers could pronounce the poets.
30. Latin took the Greek names over; its accent is generally held to have been a stress, so that “acute” and “grave” mean the accented syllable and the unaccented.
31. The place of the Latin accent comes from quantity, by the law of the penult, and is not learned word by word as English stress is.
32. The penult (Latin *paenultima*, “almost the last”) is the second syllable from the end, and the antepenult is the syllable before the penult.
33. A word of one syllable is accented on it, and a word of two syllables is accented on the first.
34. In a longer word the penult takes the accent if it is long, and the antepenult if the penult is short, so the accent never falls further back than the antepenult.
35. An enclitic (Greek *enklitikos*, “leaning on”) is a short word pronounced with the preceding word, as part of it.
36. The common Latin enclitics are *-que* (“and”), *-ne*, the sign of a question, and *-ve* (“or”).
37. An enclitic draws the accent onto the syllable just before it, whatever that syllable’s quantity, and this is an exception to the law of the penult (*populusque*).
38. English accent is a stress, not a quantity: one syllable carries the primary stress, and a longer word may carry a lighter secondary stress besides.
39. Where the English stress falls is part of the word and is learned with it.
40. A stressed English syllable is not for that reason a long Latin syllable, and Latin is misread if English stresses are simply put upon it.
41. The stress can distinguish words spelled the same, the noun of two syllables on the first and the verb on the second (“record”), which Latin, with its accent fixed by quantity, never does.
42. The endings *-tion*, *-ic*, and *-ity* put the English stress on the syllable before them, whereas *-ness*, *-ment*, and *-ful* leave it where it was.
43. An unstressed English vowel is often weakened to schwa, the short neutral vowel.
44. English speech is stress-timed, the stresses coming at roughly even intervals and the unstressed syllables shortened between them, so English verse counts stresses where Latin verse counts quantity.
45. Orators arrange quantity and accent at the end of a sentence, in the cadence called the *clausula*, because the rhythms just before a pause are the most noticeable to the ear.
46. An ending of two stressed syllables at that pause can close the sentence briefly and with emphasis.

## Punctuation (*posituræ*) (`ii.punctuation`)

1. Punctuation (Latin *punctum*, “point”) is the set of marks that show, in writing, where speech pauses and where the sense of a sentence is complete.
2. Roman books were usually written with few marks and without spaces between the words, a manner called *scriptio continua* (“continuous writing”), so the reader had to divide the words and the sentences.
3. Punctuation was usually added by the reader, as a help in reading aloud, rather than by the author, and the grammarians therefore taught the marks of pause as part of the art of reading.
4. A reader does not pause in the same way everywhere: there is a full pause where the sense is complete, a shorter pause where much of the sentence is still to come, and a slight pause where only a little remains.
5. A written mark of pause can therefore show not only where to pause but how far the sense has come toward completion.
6. Donatus calls these marks *positurae* (“placings”), or *distinctiones* (“divisions”), and he says that there are three, each a single point whose meaning depends on its height beside the letter.
7. The *distinctio*, the point set high, stands where the sentence is complete.
8. The *media distinctio*, the point set at the middle of the letter, stands where about as much remains to be said as has been said, and the reader must take breath.
9. The *subdistinctio*, the point set low, stands where only a little of the sentence remains, to be held apart for a moment and then added.
10. So the three marks measure how far the sense is from completion: the higher the point, the more complete the sense, and the *distinctio* alone marks a finished sentence.
11. The words “comma,” “colon,” and “period” first named parts of a sentence, the comma its shortest part, the colon a longer part, and the period the whole sentence brought round to completion.
12. Isidore joins Donatus’s three marks to those three parts, the low point ending a comma, the middle point a colon, and the high point a period, and the names of the parts later became the names of the marks.
13. The period descends from the high point, which marked a complete sentence; for a long time one point served for pauses of different strength, and only later did it come to mark the end of a sentence alone.
14. The comma takes its name and its work from the low point, and in the manuscripts a brief pause was also marked by a slash, the *virgula* (“little rod”), or by a small low stroke.
15. The colon takes its name from the longer member that the middle point closed, and its older shape was a point with a stroke above it, the mark of a major pause inside the sentence.
16. For a long time the comma was described chiefly as a place to stop and take breath, and only gradually was its work taken to be the distinguishing of the parts of the sense.
17. For centuries the colon likewise remained a mark of pause, standing between the comma and the period, and only gradually did it take its present work of announcing what follows.
18. The semicolon is the latest of these marks: a sign of like shape had been used to close a statement, and the semicolon itself came in as a pause between the comma and the colon.
19. The question mark descends from the *punctus interrogativus*, the sign that a sentence asks rather than states, and it marks as strong a pause as the period.
20. In present use the period ends a sentence that states something, the question mark ends a question, and the exclamation point ends an exclamation.
21. The comma separates the lesser parts of a sentence, the semicolon joins two clauses that could stand alone but are closely related, and the colon introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation.
22. The marks taught by the grammarians were chiefly guides to the voice in reading aloud; the modern marks still show pauses, but the construction of the sentence governs them more than the need to breathe.
23. A comma may therefore stand where a speaker would hardly pause, and a speaker may pause where the rules of punctuation put no mark.
24. Languages do not place the comma in the same constructions: German sets a comma before a subordinate clause, whereas English often writes the same clause with no comma.
25. Before the last “and” of a series, English usage is divided, whereas French and German normally leave that comma out, though French allows it when the last item is to be stressed.
26. After a colon, French may go on with a small letter, and may use the colon for a cause or a consequence, whereas German capitalizes a complete sentence after a colon and does not capitalize a mere list.
27. A quotation is introduced differently as well: English puts a comma before it, French a colon and guillemets, with a space before the colon, and German a colon, with no space before it, and quotation marks that open low and close high.
28. Because the marks show how the words of a sentence belong together, a change in the punctuation can change the sense while the words stay the same (*ibis redibis nunquam per bella peribis*).
29. A comma setting off a clause can mean that the clause describes the whole group named, whereas the same clause without commas can mean that it limits which members of the group are meant.
30. Punctuation thus stands between sound and construction: it marks pauses of the voice, but the pauses follow the sense.
31. A line of verse ends when its pattern of syllables is complete, whether or not the sense is complete, so in reading verse the pauses of the sense and the divisions of the meter must both be kept.

## Feet and Meter (`ii.meter`)

1. Verse is speech arranged in lines, each line following a fixed pattern of syllables, and that pattern is the meter (Greek *metron*, “measure”).
2. Diomedes defines meter, in substance, as an arrangement of feet in a fixed order, limited to a definite length.
3. The pattern is called a measure because spoken syllables have their extent in time, so verse is speech measured in time, in which the same pattern of syllables returns in every line.
4. Prose has a rhythm too, but not a pattern that returns, so the same words are prose or verse according as a rule of measure governs their syllables.
5. The grammarian studies meter because the poets were the first authors read, and a line cannot be read aloud rightly, or fully understood, unless its measure is known.
6. Other traditions measure verse in other ways: classical Sanskrit verse, like Greek and Latin, by long and short syllables, and classical Chinese verse by the number of syllables and a fixed pattern of tones.
7. A foot (Latin *pes*) is the least group of syllables that a meter repeats.
8. Latin and Greek feet are made of long and short syllables, a long syllable marked – and a short one ∪.
9. A dactyl (– ∪ ∪) is a long syllable and two short ones (*Rōmulus*).
10. A spondee (– –) is two long syllables (*Rōmā*, “from Rome”).
11. A trochee (– ∪) is a long syllable and a short one (*Rōma*).
12. An iamb (∪ –) is a short syllable and a long one (*legō*, “I read”).
13. An anapest (∪ ∪ –) is two short syllables and a long one (*bonitās*, “goodness”).
14. The names of the feet are Greek, and English uses them for feet of stressed and unstressed syllables.
15. Elision (Latin *elisio*, “striking out”) leaves uncounted a final syllable that ends in a vowel, or in a vowel and *m*, when the next word begins with a vowel or with *h*.
16. Latin still writes the elided vowel, whereas English sometimes drops it from the print and marks the place with an apostrophe.
17. The dactylic hexameter, the meter of Greek and Latin epic, has six feet: the first four each a dactyl or a spondee, the fifth almost always a dactyl, and the sixth two syllables.
18. Its close, – ∪ ∪ | – –, is the fixed part of the line, and measuring begins there.
19. The first line of the *Aeneid*, *Arma virumque cano, Troiae qui primus ab oris*, is such a hexameter, and the opening of *Evangeline* imitates the six beats by stress.
20. A caesura (Latin for a cutting) is a break between words inside a foot, not at a foot’s end.
21. In the hexameter that break usually comes in the third foot, after the first syllable.
22. If every word ended where a foot ends, the line would fall into equal pieces, an effect a poet might seek only rarely, since the words are meant to form a unity, whereas a word that runs past the end of a foot binds the line into one.
23. The feet therefore divide the line by the meter, and the words divide it by the sense.
24. The caesura is the break inside a foot, and punctuation marks the pauses of the sense, which need not fall at the same place.
25. English has no fixed caesura, and the name is used more loosely for any strong pause within the line.
26. Greek verse, like Latin, is measured by quantity, and its written accents are independent of the meter.
27. The elegiac couplet is a hexameter followed by a pentameter, a shorter line that pauses in the middle and again at the end.
28. Each half of the pentameter is two and a half feet, – ∪ ∪ | – ∪ ∪ | –, and only the first half may put a spondee for a dactyl.
29. Ovid describes the pentameter as a hexameter from which one foot has been taken in every second line.
30. The hendecasyllable, a line of eleven syllables, follows the order – – – ∪ ∪ – ∪ – ∪ – –, and elision counts toward the eleven.
31. The sapphic stanza, named for Sappho, has three lines of the pattern – ∪ – – – ∪ ∪ – ∪ – – and a short fourth line, – ∪ ∪ – –.
32. An iambic dimeter is four iambs taken in pairs, and the first and third feet may be long where the iamb is short.
33. Ambrose wrote hymns in that meter, four lines to the stanza, and many English hymns descend from it in lines of eight syllables, the beat on every second one.
34. Bede counts the feet singly and so calls the same line an iambic tetrameter.
35. The hymns are measured by quantity, yet the word accent usually falls on the beat, which is where Latin verse begins to move from quantity toward accent.
36. After long and short vowels ceased to be distinguished in spoken Latin, verse came to count syllables and accents instead of quantity.
37. Bede sets meter, arrangement by the rules of quantity, apart from rhythm (*rhythmus*), arrangement by the number of syllables as the ear judges it.
38. Rhythm can stand without meter, but meter cannot stand without rhythm, and later verse still measures, by accent and by number of syllables.
39. English verse measures by stress, not by quantity, because English length follows the stress and does not by itself distinguish words.
40. An English iamb is unstressed then stressed, a trochee stressed then unstressed, an anapest two unstressed and a stressed, and a dactyl a stressed and two unstressed.
41. A line takes its name from the foot and from the number of feet: four are a tetrameter, five a pentameter, and six a hexameter.
42. The English iamb and the Latin iamb share their name and their order, but one is unstressed and stressed, whereas the other is short and long, and a stressed English syllable may be short in time.
43. The ordinary line of English verse is the iambic pentameter, five iambs, ten syllables with the stress on the even syllables.
44. Unrhymed iambic pentameter is blank verse, the line of Shakespeare’s plays and of Milton’s *Paradise Lost*.
45. A line seldom keeps the pattern without change, and the usual change is a trochee for the first iamb, heard against the pattern that is expected.
46. An English line is measured by the syllables the words themselves stress, and by the pattern that fits most of those stresses.
47. Greek and Latin measure by quantity, English and German by stress, and Opitz required the German beat to fall on the syllable the word itself stresses.
48. French measures chiefly by the number of syllables, and its classical line, the alexandrine, has twelve, with a pause after the sixth.
49. The measure follows what each language distinguishes in its own sound, so a Latin meter cannot be copied into English by putting a stress in place of each long syllable.
