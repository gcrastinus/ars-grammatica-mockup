## Voice (*vox*) (`ii.voice`)

1. The matter of speech is the word considered as a sound, the material of which spoken words are made, and the first thing to consider is the voice itself.
2. When someone speaks, the air is set in motion so that it can be heard, whether or not anyone is there to hear it on a given occasion. Voice, as the grammarian first considers it, is therefore air set in motion so that it can be heard.
3. Donatus defines voice as “the motion of air perceptible to hearing, as far as lies in itself” (*vox est aer ictus sensibilis auditu, quantum in ipso est*). The phrase “as far as lies in itself” means that voice is the kind of thing that can be heard, even if on some occasion no one is there to hear it.
4. Not every sound is voice in the strict sense. Thunder and the splash of water are sounds, but no living thing utters them. A sneeze or a hiccup is made by a living thing, but it is not uttered in order to show anything.
5. In the strict sense, voice is a sound made by a living being with some act of imagination, as a sound that signifies something. Aristotle’s example of a sound made by a living being that is not voice is the cough.
6. The grammarian does not study how the throat and tongue produce the voice, which belongs to natural philosophy and to phonetics. He studies the voice insofar as it becomes speech.
7. Some voice is made of distinct sounds that letters can record, and some is not. The spoken word “river” can be written down so that another reader can pronounce it again, whereas a sigh cannot.
8. Donatus’s first division is that “every voice is either articulate or confused” (*omnis vox aut articulata est aut confusa*): articulate voice is voice that can be grasped in letters, and confused voice is voice that cannot be written.
9. The usual English term for Donatus’s “confused” voice is **inarticulate** voice.
10. The spoken words “river,” *flūmen* (“river”), and “sing” are articulate voice, because they are made of distinct sounds that letters can record. A sigh, the howl of a wolf, and the purr of a cat are inarticulate voice, because no arrangement of letters records them exactly. We may write “purr” or “sigh,” but these are English words that name or imitate the sounds; they are not the sounds themselves.
11. Whether a sound can be written, and whether it is joined to some meaning in the mind of the speaker, are two different questions, and a sound can satisfy one without the other. A gasp of surprise shows something but cannot be written exactly, whereas a nonsense syllable such as “zib” can be written but means nothing.
12. If a voice is joined to some meaning in the mind of the speaker, Priscian calls it **articulate**; if it can be written, he calls it **literate** (Latin *litterata*, from *littera*, “letter”). Priscian’s “articulate,” then, does not mean what Donatus’s means, so it matters which grammarian is speaking.
13. Priscian’s two questions give four kinds of voice: first, voice that signifies and can be written, such as any sentence spoken in English or Latin, “The ship sails at dawn”; second, voice that signifies but cannot be written, such as a whistle or a sigh uttered to show something; third, voice that can be written but signifies nothing, such as the imitation of an animal’s cry, as when the croak of a frog is written *coax*; and fourth, voice that neither signifies nor can be written, such as a crash or the lowing of cattle.
14. In this course “articulate” always keeps Donatus’s sense: **articulate voice is voice that can be written**, and inarticulate voice is voice that cannot.
15. We take it like this because it names exactly what the study of the matter of speech needs, a voice made of distinct sounds that letters can record, and the letter, the syllable, and the measures of verse are all studied in such voice. Priscian’s other question, whether a voice signifies, is the question that finally tells us what a word is.
16. Voice either signifies or does not, and voice that signifies does so either by nature or by imposition.
17. Articulate voice is not yet a word. The letter B pronounced alone is articulate, because it can be written, but it signifies nothing.
18. Only articulate voice that signifies by imposition is a word, and that is why the word is the least part of an expression that signifies, whereas the voice, the letter, and the syllable are its material.
19. A groan is a significant voice by nature, and it is also inarticulate, so it is not a word. For the same reason a groan cannot be a barbarism or a solecism.
20. A barbarism is an error in a single word and a solecism an error in the arrangement of words. Both errors presuppose words; below the level of the word there is nothing that can be correctly or incorrectly formed in this sense.
21. A groan may be loud or soft, but it cannot be correct or incorrect, whereas “taught” and “teached” can be, because they are words.
22. Each language uses only a selection of articulate sounds, and the selections differ. English has the sound written “th” in “think” and “this,” which Latin did not have. German has the sound written “ch” in *Bach*, which most English speakers do not use. French has the nasal vowel of *bon* (“good”), in which the air passes through the nose as well as the mouth.
23. The articulate voice of a language is a set of sounds that its speakers have learned to distinguish and to combine. A child who learns English learns to hear the difference between “thin” and “tin” because it changes the word; a speaker of a language without the “th” sound may hear the two as one.
24. **Phonetics** studies the sounds of speech as sounds: how the throat, tongue, and lips produce them, how they travel through the air, and how the ear receives them, whatever language they belong to.
25. **Phonology** studies how a particular language uses its sounds: which differences of sound its speakers treat as distinguishing one word from another, as English treats “th” and “t” in “thin” and “tin,” and which differences they pass over without noticing.
26. Articulate voice is divided into its least parts, which writing records as letters; letters are joined into syllables; and syllables are measured as long or short, and accented, and arranged into feet and lines of verse. So the study of the matter of speech considers, after the voice, the letter, the syllable, quantity and accent, punctuation (the marks of the pauses of speech), and meter (the measures of verse).

## The Letter and Orthography (`ii.letter`)

1. Articulate voice is voice that can be written. When we say the word “sun” slowly, we can hear that it is made of three distinct sounds, and we write each of them with its own mark; the Latin *nāvis* (“ship”) is made of five.
2. Such a least part of articulate voice is what the grammarians call the **letter** (Latin *littera*). Donatus defines it as “the least part of articulate voice” (*pars minima vocis articulatae*).
3. The classical grammarians used the word “letter” both for the sound and for the written mark, because in Latin the two correspond closely.
4. A letter signifies nothing by itself, as we saw with the letter B pronounced alone, so the letter is part of the material of speech and not yet a word.
5. In our time the sound is often called a phoneme and the written mark a grapheme, but the grammarian’s word “letter” covers both, and we will distinguish them where it matters.
6. Three things belong to every letter: its name, its figure, and its power. Donatus gives the same three (*nomen, figura, potestas*): “for we ask what a letter is called, what figure it has, and what it can do”.
7. The grammarians call these three the **accidents** of the letter (Latin *accidentia*). They are the properties it has, and by them one letter differs from another, as a word has accidents, such as number and tense, by which “horse” differs from “horses” and “I run” from “you run.”
8. **Name** (*nomen*): what we call the letter when we speak of it. The English letter C is called “see,” and the Latin letter was called *ce*.
9. **Figure** (*figura*): the shape of the letter as it is written, such as C, c, or the cursive *c*.
10. **Power** (*potestas*): the sound that the letter stands for. In English “cat,” C stands for the sound /k/, and in Latin C always stood for that sound, even before “e” and “i,” as in *Cicerō*. A sound, as distinct from a letter, is written between slashes.
11. One figure may have more than one power, as English C has one power in “cat” and another in “city.” And one power may have more than one figure, as the English sound /k/ is written with c in “cat,” k in “kit,” and ck in “back.”
12. The letters are divided first into vowels and consonants. Some letters can be pronounced by themselves, and each of them can make a syllable alone, as in the English words “a” and “I,” and in Latin *ō* (“oh”) and *ē* (“out of”). Other letters cannot.
13. A **vowel** (Latin *vocalis*, from *vox*, “voice”) is a letter that can be pronounced by itself and can make a syllable by itself.
14. Latin has five vowels, *a, e, i, o, u*, and the Romans also used *y* in words taken from Greek, such as *lyra* (“lyre”). English writes the same five vowel letters, with *y* as a vowel in “myth” and “happy.”
15. A vowel letter is not the same as a vowel sound. English has more than a dozen vowel sounds, counting its diphthongs, but only five or six vowel letters to write them, so the letter *a* has different powers in “hat,” “hate,” “hard,” and “ago.”
16. A **consonant** (Latin *consonans*, “sounding with”) is a letter that cannot make a syllable without a vowel. When we try to pronounce the letter *t* by itself, we cannot do it without adding a vowel, as in “tee” or “tuh.”
17. A **semivowel** (*semivocalis*) is a consonant that can be partly pronounced without a vowel, such as *s*, *m*, and *l*. A **mute** (*muta*) is a consonant that cannot be pronounced at all without a vowel, such as *b*, *d*, and *t*.
18. The name of a semivowel begins with a vowel and ends with the letter’s own sound: *ef, el, em, en, er, es, ix*. The name of a mute begins with its own sound and ends with a vowel: *be, ce, de, ge, pe, te*. The English names of the letters still follow the same pattern: “ef,” “el,” “em,” “es,” but “bee,” “dee,” “tee.”
19. Among the semivowels the grammarians set apart four letters, *l, m, n, r*, and call them **liquids** (*liquidae*), because they flow easily after another consonant, as *l* does in English “play” and Latin *plēnus* (“full”), and *r* in “tree” and *frāter* (“brother”).
20. The letter *x* is called a **double letter**, because it stands for two sounds, *c* and *s*, as in Latin *rex* (“king”), which is *rec-s*. The letter *z*, used in Greek words, was also counted double.
21. The letter *h*, according to many grammarians, is not a letter but a mark of breathing (*aspiratio*, “breathing”), because it adds breath to a vowel without making a sound of its own. In Latin *homo* (“man”), the *h* is pronounced, but it does not count as a consonant for measuring syllables.
22. An alphabet does not always have exactly the letters that a language needs. Some letters may be superfluous, because another letter already has the same power; in English, *c*, *k*, and *q* can all stand for the same sound, as in “cold,” “king,” and “queen.” And some sounds may have no letter of their own; English has no single letter for the sound written “th” or for the sound written “sh.”
23. The Latin letter *k* survived only in a few words such as *Kalendae* (“the Calends,” the first day of the month), because *c* already had the same power, and *x* could be written *cs*. Quintilian, writing in the first century, asks whether the Latin alphabet has the letters it needs: “Again there is the question whether certain letters are not superfluous, not to mention the mark of the aspirate,” and he names *k*, *q*, and *x*.
24. The Latin *i* and *u* were each used for two sounds, a vowel and a consonant, as in *iam* (“now”) and *uinum* (“wine”). The Romans had no separate figures for the consonants; the distinction of *j* from *i* and of *v* from *u* was made by printers and scholars of the Renaissance.
25. In this course Latin is pronounced in the restored classical pronunciation, that is, the pronunciation that scholars have reconstructed for the speech of educated Romans in the time of Cicero, chiefly from the classical grammarians, from spelling, and from verse. The church pronunciation, which is closer to Italian, is also traditional, but it is not used in this course.
26. Every Latin vowel is either **long** or **short**. A long vowel is held about twice as long as a short one, and the difference can change the word: *os* with a short *o* means “bone,” and *ōs* with a long *o* means “mouth.”
27. Ordinary Latin spelling does not show the difference, so in the Latin examples of this course every long vowel is marked with a **macron** (a short line above the letter): *ā, ē, ī, ō, ū*, and in those examples a vowel without a macron is short. Quotations from Latin authors are given as the editions print them, without macrons.
28. The vowels have roughly these sounds: *a* as in “father” (short *a* as in “cup”); *ē* as in “they” and short *e* as in “pet”; *ī* as in “machine” and short *i* as in “pit”; *ō* as in “note” and short *o* as in “pot”; *ū* as in “rule” and short *u* as in “put.”
29. When we say “aisle,” “house,” or “boy,” the voice glides from one vowel sound to another within a single syllable. A **diphthong** (Greek *diphthongos*, “double sound”) is two vowels pronounced in one syllable in this way.
30. The common Latin diphthongs are *ae*, *au*, and *oe*, and they are pronounced as the vowels of “aisle,” “house,” and “boy,” in that order. So *Caesar* begins like “kite,” and *causa* (“cause”) like “cow.”
31. In the restored pronunciation, *c* and *g* are always hard, as in “come” and “go,” so *Cicero* is “Kikero” and *gens* (“nation”) begins like “get.”
32. Consonantal *u* (printed *v* in many books) is pronounced like English *w*, so *ventus* (“wind”) is “wentus.” Consonantal *i* (printed *j* in some books) is pronounced like English *y*, so *iūstus* (“just”) is “yoostus.”
33. And *s* is always voiceless, as in “sit,” never as in “rose.” *R* is trilled. And doubled consonants are held longer, as in *annus* (“year”).
34. The part of grammar that concerns correct spelling is **orthography** (Greek *orthographia*, “correct writing”).
35. Quintilian’s rule has two parts. Spelling should follow pronunciation, because letters exist to record sounds; but it does so “within the limits prescribed by usage,” because the accepted spelling of a word is part of the custom of the language. His statement of the rule is: “For my own part, I think that, within the limits prescribed by usage, words should be spelt as they are pronounced. For the use of letters is to preserve the sound of words and to deliver them to readers as a sacred trust: consequently they ought to represent the pronunciation which we are to use”.
36. Usage is one of the criteria of correct speech, and here it governs writing as well.
37. In Latin the spelling follows the sound closely, so that a reader who knows the powers of the letters can pronounce almost any word correctly. In English the relation is much looser.
38. The word “knight” has six letters but only three sounds, because the *k* and the *gh* are no longer pronounced. The words “I” and “eye” have the same sound and different spellings, whereas “read” (present) and “read” (past) have the same spelling and different sounds.
39. English spelling was largely fixed by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, after printing came to England, but the pronunciation of English continued to change. Between about 1400 and 1700 the long vowels of English shifted (the change called the Great Vowel Shift), so that “name,” which had once been pronounced with the vowel of “father,” came to be pronounced as it is now, but its spelling stayed the same. So English spelling often records an older pronunciation of the word.
40. English spelling sometimes records not the sound of a word but its origin. The word “debt” was spelled “dette” in Middle English, from French *dette*; the *b* was added to show its origin in Latin *debitum* (“what is owed”), although it was never pronounced. In the same way “doubt” received its *b* from Latin *dubitare* (“to doubt”).
41. So English spelling serves two purposes: it records the sound, and it records the history and the relations of words. The spelling shows that “sign” and “signature,” or “nation” and “national,” are the same word in different forms even when their vowels are pronounced differently.
42. Letters are not pronounced one by one; they are joined into syllables. Every syllable must contain a vowel or a diphthong, and the consonants are grouped around it. The distinctions on which the rules for dividing and measuring syllables depend are vowel and consonant, liquid, double letter, and the long and short vowel.

## The Syllable (`ii.syllable`)

1. Letters are not pronounced one by one but in groups. When we say the word “window” slowly, the voice divides it into two parts, “win” and “dow,” and each part is pronounced as a single unit; “pen” is pronounced as one such unit, and Latin *columba* (“dove”) as three.
2. A **syllable** (Greek *syllabē*, “what is taken together”) is a group of letters pronounced together in one utterance of the voice. Donatus defines it as a combination of letters, or the utterance of a single vowel, capable of length. Priscian adds that the letters of a syllable are uttered under one accent and in one breath.
3. English “pen” and Latin *lūx* (“light”) are each one syllable, “window” is two (win-dow), and *columba* is three (co-lum-ba).
4. Like the letter, the syllable signifies nothing by itself; the syllable “win” in “window” does not signify winning. The syllable is still part of the material of speech, but it is the unit in which speech is actually pronounced and measured.
5. Every syllable has one vowel or one diphthong at its center, and consonants may stand before it, after it, or on both sides. Latin *ā* (“from”) is a syllable of one vowel and no consonant; *ab* (“from”) has a consonant after the vowel; and *stirps* (“stock,” “root”) has two consonants before the vowel and two after it. English “O,” “on,” and “strong” show the same range.
6. Therefore in Latin the number of syllables in a word is the number of its vowels and diphthongs.
7. A diphthong counts as one vowel, because its two vowels are pronounced in one syllable. So Latin *aurum* (“gold”) has two syllables (au-rum), not three, and *poena* (“penalty”) has two (poe-na). But two vowels that are not a diphthong make two syllables, as in Latin *me-us* (“my”) and *di-ēs* (“day”). In English the same is true of “cre-ate” and “po-em,” where the two vowel letters stand for two vowel sounds.
8. Four things belong to every syllable: its accent (*tenor*), its breathing (*spiritus*), its length (*tempus*, literally “time”), and the number of its letters (*numerus litterarum*). Priscian lists these four as the accidents of the syllable.
9. The **accent** is the raising or lowering of the voice on the syllable. The **breathing** is whether the syllable is pronounced with an *h* or without one, as Latin *hōra* (“hour”) begins with a breathing and *ōra* (“shores”) does not. The **length** is whether the syllable is long or short, which is the foundation of Latin verse. The **number of letters** is the count of letters in the syllable, which in Latin runs from one letter to as many as six.
10. A syllable that ends in a vowel is called **open**, and a syllable that ends in a consonant is called **closed**. In Latin *pa-ter* (“father”) the first syllable ends in a vowel and the second in a consonant; in *por-ta* (“gate”) the first syllable ends in a consonant and the second in a vowel. In English both syllables of “ba-by” end in a vowel, and both syllables of “bas-ket” end in a consonant.
11. The length of the vowel and the length of the syllable must be kept apart. A syllable with a short vowel is short if it is open. If it is closed, the vowel stays short, but the syllable as a whole is long, because the consonant at its end adds to the time needed to pronounce it: in *canunt* (“they sing”) the “u” is short, but the syllable “-nunt” is long.
12. English syllables are counted by vowel sounds, not by vowel letters. “Make” has two vowel letters but one syllable, because its final *e* is silent, and “strength” has eight letters but one syllable, because it has one vowel sound. “lion” and “being” have two syllables each, because each has two vowel sounds.
13. English allows larger groups of consonants than Latin does. Up to three consonants can begin an English syllable, as in “street,” “spring,” and “splash” (and when there are three, the first is always *s*). Four can end one, as in “texts” and “twelfths.”
14. Many groups that are pronounceable in other languages cannot begin an English word, such as *pn* or *ps*; that is why the *p* of “pneumonia” and “psalm,” taken from Greek, is not pronounced.
15. English adds a qualification to the rule that every syllable has a vowel at its center. In some unstressed syllables a liquid or a nasal consonant takes the place of the vowel. Such a consonant is called a **syllabic consonant**.
16. The second syllable of “button” is usually pronounced with no vowel sound at all, only the *n*; so is the second syllable of “bottle” with *l*, and of “rhythm” with *m*. So the English word “rhythm” has two syllables, though it has only one vowel letter (*y*), because the final *m* makes a syllable of its own.
17. Latin, as the grammarians describe it, has no syllabic consonants; every Latin syllable has a vowel or diphthong at its center, which is why the Latin rule of counting is simpler than the English one.
18. English words are divided in two ways, which do not always agree. In writing, a word broken at the end of a line is divided according to the conventions of the dictionaries, which consider both the sound and the parts of the word: “fa-ther,” “walk-ing,” “hab-it.”
19. The short vowel sounds of English, the vowels of “cat,” “bed,” “sit,” “hot,” and “cup,” never end an English word; a consonant always closes them. In the same way, when such a vowel is stressed, it does not end a syllable, and the consonant after it stays in its syllable.
20. Take “habit”: its first vowel is the vowel of “cat,” and the stress falls on it, so the syllable cannot end with that vowel, and the “b” stays with it: “hab-it,” not “ha-bit.” So also “rob-in,” “cam-el,” and “wag-on.” A long vowel sound may end a syllable, so “bacon” is divided “ba-con” and “tiger” “ti-ger.”
21. The division into syllables is still a matter of the sound, but English has no single rule that settles every word.
22. When a single consonant stands between two vowels, it goes with the following vowel, and the syllable before the consonant is open. So *rota* (“wheel”) is divided *ro-ta*, *lupus* (“wolf”) is *lu-pus*, and *amor* (“love”) is *a-mor*. Latin *habet* (“he has”) is divided *ha-bet*, whereas English “habit” is divided “hab-it,” because in English a stressed short vowel does not end a syllable.
23. When two consonants stand between two vowels, they are usually divided, the first going with the preceding vowel and the second with the following one, and the syllable before the consonants is closed. So *fortis* (“brave”) is *for-tis*, *magnus* (“great”) is *mag-nus*, and *mittō* (“I send”) is *mit-tō*. When three consonants stand together, usually the last goes with the following vowel, as in *sanc-tus* (“holy”).
24. When a mute (*p, b, t, d, c, g*) is followed by *l* or *r*, the two consonants usually go together with the following vowel: *pa-tris* (“of the father”), *a-grī* (“of the field”), *pū-bli-cus* (“public”). In verse, however, a poet may divide them (*pat-ris*), and this choice affects the length of the syllable.
25. The double letter *x* counts as two consonants, so in *saxum* (“rock”) the first syllable is closed (*sac-sum* in sound). The pair *qu* counts as one consonant, so *aqua* (“water”) is *a-qua*. The breathing *h* does not count as a consonant at all. A compound word is divided between its parts: *ab-eō* (“I go away”), not *a-beō*.
26. The syllable is the least unit in which speech is pronounced. It has a vowel or diphthong at its center, and words are divided into syllables by rules that differ somewhat between English and Latin. A syllable may be open or closed, and length is one of its accidents.
27. In Latin, length is the most important accident of the syllable. The length of a syllable determines where the accent of a Latin word falls, and it is the measure on which Latin verse is built.
28. So the division of a word into syllables is the first step both in pronouncing Latin correctly and in reading Latin poetry.

## Quantity and Accent (`ii.quantity`)

1. **Quantity** is how long a syllable is held; **accent** is how the voice rises, or gains force, on one syllable of a word. They are two different accidents of the syllable: the accent (*tenor*) and the length (*tempus*). When we say the English word “delight,” the voice gains force on its second syllable, de-LIGHT. When we say the Latin words *malum* (“evil”) and *mālum* (“apple”), the two differ only in how long the first syllable is held.
2. Latin verse is built on quantity, and the Latin accent is itself placed by quantity. English is concerned with stress. English syllables do differ in length, but their length is almost entirely governed by the stress and by the sounds around the vowel: a stressed syllable is held longer than an unstressed one, and the vowel of “bead” is held longer than the vowel of “beat,” because a voiced consonant follows it.
3. These differences of length do not distinguish English words in the way that the length of the first syllable distinguishes *mālum* from *malum*. So English verse measures stress, not length. An English iamb is an unstressed and a stressed syllable, as in “de-LIGHT,” and the names of the feet were borrowed from Greek and Latin verse, where they name patterns of long and short syllables.
4. The Latin words *os* (“bone”) and *ōs* (“mouth”) differ only in the time for which the vowel is held. The name **quantity** comes from the Latin *quantitas*, “how much.” In Latin every syllable is either **long** or **short**, and the grammarians reckoned that a long syllable takes two units of time and a short syllable one.
5. Donatus adds a third kind, the **common** syllable (*communis*), which may be treated as long or short.
6. The quantity of a syllable is not the same as the length of its vowel, although the two are related. The quantity of a syllable is measured from the beginning of its vowel to the end of the syllable. So the consonants before the vowel add nothing to it, as the *s* and *t* of *stō* (“I stand”) add nothing, but a consonant that closes the syllable after the vowel adds to the time needed to pronounce it.
7. In these reckonings the breathing *h* is not counted as a consonant, nor is the *u* that is written after *q*, and sometimes after *g* or *s*, and pronounced almost as a consonant, as in *quis* (“who?”), *lingua* (“tongue”), and *suādeō* (“I advise”).
8. A syllable may be long because its vowel is long, or because its vowel is followed by a consonant that closes the syllable. The grammarians therefore distinguish two ways in which a syllable is long: by nature and by position.
9. A syllable is **long by nature** when its vowel is long or when it contains a diphthong. The second syllable of *a-mī-cus* (“friend”) is long because its vowel *ī* is long, and so is the first syllable of *mā-ter* (“mother”); the first syllable of *cae-lum* (“sky”) is long because *ae* is a diphthong.
10. A vowel that comes from a diphthong is long, as the *ū* of *exclūdō* (“I shut out”), formed from *ex* and *claudō* (“I shut”); and a vowel formed by contraction, that is, by two vowels merged into one, is long, as the *ō* of *cōgō* (“I drive together”), formed from *co-* and *agō* (“I drive”).
11. The length of a vowel cannot be seen from ordinary Latin spelling, and it must be learned with each word, as we learn the spelling of an English word. The Romans sometimes marked a long vowel with a stroke above the letter, called an apex, or by writing the vowel twice, but none of these devices came into general use. That is why this course marks every long vowel with a macron.
12. A syllable whose vowel is short is still **long by position** when its vowel is followed by two consonants, or by one of the double letters *x* or *z*, except when the two consonants are a mute followed by *l* or *r*.
13. In *sil-va* (“forest”) the *i* is short, but the first syllable is long because *l* and *v* follow it. In *ma-gis-ter* (“teacher”) the *i* is short, but the second syllable is long because *s* and *t* follow it. In *a-xis* (“axle”) the first syllable is long because *x* counts as two consonants.
14. The first syllable of *nigra* (“black”) is not long by position, although *g* and *r* follow its short *i*: a mute and a liquid usually go together with the following vowel, so the syllable stays open. Such a syllable is properly short, but a poet may treat it as long, and so it is called common.
15. When two consonants follow a short vowel, the first of them closes the syllable, and the closed syllable takes longer to pronounce. The vowel itself is still pronounced short; the syllable is long, not the vowel.
16. The term “position” does not refer to the place of the vowel in the word; the Latin *positio* translates the Greek *thesei*, “by convention,” as opposed to *physei*, “by nature.” It is still the established term, and the Latin grammars use it.
17. When a syllable is long by position, it is long whether its vowel is long or short, so the quantity of the syllable does not show the length of the vowel. The length of a vowel in such a syllable is therefore called **hidden** quantity.
18. The first syllable of *cōnstāns* (“steady”) is long by position, and its vowels are long; the *ī* of *īnferō* (“I bring in”) is long. The syllable *man* of *a-man-dus* (“to be loved”) and the syllable *mant* of *a-mant* (“they love”) are long by position, but the *a* is short.
19. A vowel of hidden quantity is pronounced short unless it is known to be long. This course marks it with a macron wherever it is known to be long. The measuring of a line of verse, which counts syllables, does not tell us the length of a vowel in a syllable long by position.
20. A vowel before *ns* or *nf* is long, as in *cōnstāns* and *īnferō*. A vowel before *gn* is also held to be long, but its quantity is not certain in every word. A vowel before *nd* or *nt* is regularly short, as in *amandus* and *amant*. In all these words the syllable is long by position; what the rules decide is the length of the vowel, which matters for pronouncing the word correctly but not for measuring it.
21. A syllable is **short** when its vowel is short and it is open, that is, when no more than one consonant follows the vowel before the next vowel. So the first two syllables of *ta-bu-la* (“tablet”) are short, and so is the first syllable of *ca-put* (“head”).
22. A vowel that stands directly before another vowel is usually short, even if it would otherwise be long. So the *u* of *pu-el-la* (“girl”) is short, and so is the *e* of *de-us* (“god”). The breathing *h* does not count as a consonant here, so the first *i* of *ni-hil* (“nothing”) also stands before a vowel. The second syllable of *pu-el-la* is long by position, because two consonants, *l* and *l*, follow its short *e*.
23. In ordinary speech *tenebrae* (“darkness”) is divided *te-ne-brae*, and its second syllable is open and short. But a poet may divide the two consonants, *te-neb-rae*, and then the second syllable is closed and long.
24. A syllable that can be measured either way is called **common** (*syllaba communis*). Ovid uses both measures of the same word in one line: *et primo similis volucri, mox vera volucris*, “first like a bird and then in fact a bird”. In the first *volucri* the second syllable is divided *vo-lu-cri* and is short; in *volucris* it is divided *vo-luc-ris* and is long.
25. The common syllable is not an error in the poet; it is a license that the language allows, because the mute and the liquid can be pronounced in either syllable.
26. In verse the words of a line are pronounced in one continuous stream. When a final syllable has a short vowel and ends in a consonant, its quantity in verse is decided by the word that follows.
27. If the next word begins with a vowel, the final consonant is joined to that vowel in pronunciation, and the syllable is short. In *per arva* (“through the fields”) the *r* of *per* is pronounced with the *a* of *arva*, *pe-rar-va*, and the syllable *per* is open and short.
28. If the next word begins with a consonant, the syllable is long by position. In *per campōs* (“through the plains”) the *r* is followed by the *c* of *campōs*, so the syllable *per* is closed and long by position. Since *h* is not counted as a consonant, *per hortōs* (“through the gardens”) is measured as *per arva* is, and *per* is short.
29. A line of Latin verse is therefore measured across the divisions between its words.
30. The letter *i* can stand for a consonant, as at the beginning of *iam* (“now”). Between two vowels the first syllable of *maior* (“greater”), of *peius* (“worse”), and of *aiō* (“I say”) is long, although only one consonant, the consonant *i*, seems to follow its vowel.
31. The reason is that the speaker passes from the first vowel through a vowel *i*, which forms a diphthong with it, before reaching the consonant *i*, so that *maior* is pronounced as if it were written *mai-ior*, and *aiō* as if it were *ai-iō*.
32. So a syllable whose vowel is *a*, *e*, *o*, or *u*, followed by consonant *i*, is long, whether the vowel itself is long or short. Allen and Greenough mark such a syllable with a circumflex, *mâ-ior*, *pê-ius*, *â-iō*, rather than with a macron, because the length belongs to the syllable and not necessarily to its vowel. This course marks only long vowels, so these words are written without a macron, but the syllable is long all the same.
33. When we say a word of more than one syllable, the voice does not treat every syllable alike. In “banana” the voice rises and gains force on the second syllable, and in “yesterday” on the first.
34. The Latin word *accentus* is formed from *ad* (“to”) and *cantus* (“song”), and it translates the Greek *prosōidia*, “song added to speech.” The name shows that the classical grammarians thought of accent as a rising and falling of the voice, as in song.
35. Donatus calls the accents tones (*toni*), noting that some call them *accentus* and others *tenores*, and he says that they are three: **the acute** (*acutus*), in which the voice is raised; **the grave** (*gravis*), in which it is lowered; and **the circumflex** (*circumflexus*), in which it is raised and lowered on the same syllable.
36. Quintilian counts it an error to put the accent in the wrong place. He speaks of “the substitution of the acute accent for the grave or the grave for the acute: such an example would be the placing of the acute accent on the first syllable of *Camillus*”. A misplaced accent was one kind of barbarism, an error in a single word.
37. The doctrine of the three accents was first worked out for Greek, in which the accent was a matter of pitch, that is, of the voice rising and falling in tone. The marks used to write the Greek accents were introduced by the scholars of Alexandria about 200 BC to help readers pronounce the poets correctly.
38. In the word ἄνθρωπος (*anthrōpos*, “human being”) the acute mark on the first syllable shows that the voice rose in pitch on that syllable.
39. The Latin grammarians took over the Greek terms and applied them to Latin. Most scholars now hold, with Allen, that the Latin accent was chiefly a **stress** accent, that is, that the accented syllable was pronounced with greater force, though it may also have been higher in pitch. So the Latin grammarians’ words “acute” and “grave” are inherited from Greek, and in Latin they mean, in practice, the accented and the unaccented syllable.
40. The place of the Latin accent is not learned word by word, as in English; it is found from the quantity of the syllables. The rule is called the **law of the penult**.
41. The **penult** (Latin *paenultima*, “almost the last”) is the second syllable from the end, and the **antepenult** is the syllable before the penult.
42. A word of one syllable is accented on that syllable. A word of two syllables is accented on the first: *VĪ-ta* (“life”), *MO-ra* (“delay”).
43. In a word of three or more syllables, the penult is accented if it is long, and the antepenult if the penult is short: *for-TŪ-na* (“fortune,” penult long by nature), *ar-GEN-tum* (“silver,” penult long by position), but *MĪ-li-tēs* (“soldiers”) and *a-GRI-co-la* (“farmer”), whose penults are short.
44. So the accent of a Latin word never falls further back than the antepenult, and the quantity of the penult decides between the penult and the antepenult.
45. Some short words are pronounced together with the word before them, as if they were part of it. They are called **enclitics** (Greek *enklitikos*, “leaning on”). The common Latin enclitics are *-que* (“and”), *-ne* (the sign of a question), and *-ve* (“or”).
46. So *populus* (“people”) and *-que* make *populusque* (“and the people”), as in the Roman formula *senātus populusque Rōmānus*, “the senate and the people of Rome.”
47. An enclitic moves the accent onto the syllable just before it, whatever the quantity of that syllable. *PO-pu-lus* is accented on its first syllable, but *po-pu-LUS-que* is accented on the syllable before *-que*; in the same way *LĪ-mi-na* (“thresholds”) becomes *lī-mi-NA-que* (“and thresholds”). The Roman grammarians noted this as an exception to the law of the penult.
48. English has an accent, but it is not found by quantity. In every English word of more than one syllable, one syllable is pronounced more strongly than the others, that is, louder, longer, and usually higher in pitch. This is called the **primary stress**. A longer word often has a lighter **secondary stress** as well.
49. Dictionaries mark the primary stress with ˈ and the secondary with ˌ before the syllable: “photographic” is ˌphoto-ˈgraphic, with the primary stress on “graph” and the secondary on “pho.”
50. The place of the English stress belongs to each word and must be learned with it, as the quantity of a Latin vowel must be learned. English “beautiful” is stressed on the first syllable and “tomato” on the second, and no rule of quantity accounts for this.
51. So English stress and Latin quantity are different things; the English stressed syllable is not a Latin long syllable, and it is a mistake to read Latin by putting English stresses on it.
52. Because English stress belongs to each word, it can distinguish two words that are otherwise spelled alike. Many English nouns and verbs of two syllables form such pairs: the noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second.
53. “A RE-cord” is a noun, and “to re-CORD” is a verb; so also “PER-mit” and “per-MIT,” “PRE-sent” and “pre-SENT,” “OB-ject” and “ob-JECT,” “CON-trast” and “con-TRAST.”
54. In “They will record the speech,” the stress of “record” falls on the second syllable, because the word is a verb, whereas in “The record was broken,” it falls on the first. Latin never distinguishes words in this way, because the place of the Latin accent is fixed by the quantity of the syllables.
55. Words of one family may be stressed on different syllables, because certain endings fix the place of the stress. The endings *-tion*, *-ic*, and *-ity* put the stress on the syllable just before them, wherever it stood in the shorter word: “ED-u-cate,” but “ed-u-CA-tion”; “ATH-lete,” but “ath-LET-ic”; “e-LEC-tric,” but “e-lec-TRIC-i-ty.”
56. The endings *-ness*, *-ment*, and *-ful* leave the stress where it was: “HAP-py” and “HAP-pi-ness,” “GOV-ern” and “GOV-ern-ment,” “CARE” and “CARE-ful.” These regularities do not make English stress predictable in every word, but they account for the stress of a great many words of Latin and Greek origin.
57. An unstressed English syllable is often weakened: its vowel becomes the short, neutral vowel called **schwa** (written ə), the vowel of the first syllable of “about” and of the last syllable of “sofa.”
58. The same letter can have different sounds in words of one family: the first *o* of “photograph” is a full vowel, because it is stressed, but in “photography” it is a schwa, because the stress has moved to “tog.”
59. English speech is **stress-timed**, that is, the stressed syllables come at roughly even intervals, and the unstressed syllables between them are shortened to fit. “The CAT is on the MAT” and “The CATerpillar’s on the MATtress” take about the same time to say, because each has two stresses. So English verse counts stresses, whereas Latin verse counts long and short syllables.
60. Quantity and accent are minor accidents of the syllable, matters of how a single word is pronounced. But meter is built from them, and they also serve rhetoric: orators arrange the beats of their clauses, and above all the endings of their sentences, to work on their hearers in ways the hearers seldom notice, as the Latin orators did by quantity in the cadence they called the *clausula*.
61. Lincoln, in his Second Inaugural Address, says that both parties deprecated war, “but one of them would make war rather than let the nation survive, and the other would accept war rather than let it perish, and the war came.” The last clause is heard as “and the WAR CAME”: after the long, balanced clauses, two stressed syllables fall together at the very end, and the sentence stops short and hard.
62. Churchill, in June 1940, asks the British people so to bear themselves that, if their empire lasts a thousand years, “men will still say, ‘This was their finest hour.’” The ending is heard as “THIS was their FIN-est HOUR,” with “hour” spoken almost as two syllables, “HOU-er.” The stresses stand far apart at first and then come closer together, and the last syllable is light, so the sentence quickens toward its close and then comes to rest softly.
63. Ward Farnsworth observes: “The rhythms that occur before a pause are the most noticeable to the ear.” He calls an ending of two stressed syllables, like Lincoln’s, “a pattern that can create a decisive and emphatic finish”.

## Punctuation (*posituræ*) (`ii.punctuation`)

1. Speech has pauses, and a reader who reads aloud must know where to pause and where the sense of a sentence is complete. **Punctuation** (from Latin *punctum*, “point”) is the set of marks that show these divisions in writing.
2. Roman books were usually written without spaces between the words and with few marks of any kind, a style that scholars call *scriptio continua* (“continuous writing”). So the first sentence of Caesar’s *Gallic War*, *Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres* (“All Gaul is divided into three parts”), appeared as GALLIAESTOMNISDIVISAINPARTESTRES, and the reader had to divide the words and the sentences himself.
3. Punctuation was usually added by the reader, as an aid to reading aloud, rather than by the author. The grammarians therefore taught the marks of pause as part of the art of reading.
4. When we read a sentence aloud, we do not pause in the same way everywhere. We make a full pause where the sense is complete, a shorter pause where we must take breath with much of the sentence still to come, and a slight pause where only a little remains to be added.
5. A mark of pause in writing can therefore show not only where to pause but also how far the sense has come toward completion.
6. Donatus calls the marks of pause *positurae* (“placings”), or *distinctiones* (“divisions”), and says that there are three, each a single point whose meaning depends on its height beside the letter.
7. ***Distinctio*** (“division,” the point set high) is placed where the sentence is complete, and its point is set at the top of the letter.
8. ***Media distinctio*** (“middle division,” the point set in the middle) is placed where about as much of the sentence remains as has been said, but the reader must take breath; its point is set at the middle of the letter.
9. ***Subdistinctio*** (“lower division,” the point set low) is placed where only a little of the sentence remains, which must be kept apart for a moment and then added; its point is set at the foot of the letter.
10. So the three marks measure how far the sense is from completion. The higher the point, the more complete the sense, and the *distinctio* alone marks a complete sentence.
11. The words “comma,” “colon,” and “period” first named parts of a sentence. The Greek rhetoricians called the shortest part of a sentence a **comma** (Greek *komma*, “a piece cut off”), a longer part a **colon** (Greek *kōlon*, “limb”), and the whole sentence, which comes round to completion, a **period** (Greek *periodos*, “a going round”).
12. Isidore of Seville, a scholar of the seventh century, repeats Donatus’s three marks and connects each of them with one of these parts: the *subdistinctio* ended a comma, the *media distinctio* ended a colon, and the *distinctio* ended a period. So the names of the parts of a sentence later came to name the marks that end them.
13. The **period** descends from the high point of the *distinctio*, which marked a complete sentence. For a long time a single point (“.”) served for pauses of different strength: in the books printed by Aldus Manutius in Venice around 1500, the same point could stand within a sentence, followed by a small letter, or at its end, followed by a capital. Italian grammarians dropped these distinctions in the seventeenth century, and the point came in time to mark only the end of a sentence.
14. The **comma** takes its name and its work from the low point of the *subdistinctio*, which ended the short part of a sentence called a comma. Late medieval manuscripts often mark the briefest pause with a slash (“/”), the *virgula* (“little rod”), and Italian scribes of the fourteenth century also used a small low stroke for a minor pause. Print fixed the modern comma: Aldus’s edition of Pietro Bembo’s *De Aetna* (1496) did most to spread it.
15. The **colon** takes its name from the longer part of a sentence that the *media distinctio* closed. Its ancestor in shape is the *punctus elevatus*, a point with a small hooked stroke above it, which came to mark a major pause within a sentence, where the sense is complete but the meaning is not yet finished.
16. Writers of the sixteenth century still described the comma chiefly as a place to stop and take breath, although Aldo Manuzio the Younger (1561) already gave it the work of distinguishing the parts of the sense.
17. For centuries the colon remained a mark of pause: in sixteenth-century French it stood midway between the comma and the period, and the English grammarian Robert Lowth still reckoned it, in 1762, a pause twice as long as the semicolon and half as long as the period. Only gradually did it take its present work of announcing what follows.
18. The **semicolon** is the youngest of these marks. A sign of similar shape, the *punctus versus*, was used from the late eighth century, but to end a statement. The semicolon itself appeared in print in Venice in February 1496, in the same *De Aetna* printed by Aldus, as a pause between the comma and the colon. Aldo Manuzio the Younger explained it so: a comma alone is not enough, and a colon would hold the sentence back too much. In Italian books until the eighteenth century it often stands where we would put a comma or no mark at all, before conjunctions and relative clauses.
19. The **question mark** descends from the *punctus interrogativus*, which scribes began to use in the eighth century, at the court of Charlemagne, to mark the end of a sentence that asks a question. It was drawn as a point with a wavy or broken stroke above it, and its upright modern form dates from the late fifteenth century. Grammarians of the sixteenth century already observed that it marks as strong a pause as the period, but shows that the sentence asks rather than states.
20. The **period** (in British usage, the full stop) ends a sentence that states something.
21. The **question mark** and the **exclamation point** end a question and an exclamation.
22. The **comma** separates the lesser parts of a sentence, such as the items of a series or a clause set before the main clause.
23. The **semicolon** joins two clauses that could stand as separate sentences but are closely related in sense.
24. The **colon** introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation.
25. The marks of pause taught by the grammarians were chiefly guides to pausing when reading aloud. The modern marks still show pauses, but they are governed more by the construction of the sentence than by breath. So a modern comma may separate two clauses where a speaker would hardly pause, and a speaker may pause where the rules of punctuation put no mark.
26. In English we write “I know that he is coming” with no comma. German writes “Ich weiß, dass er kommt” (“I know that he is coming”), because German sets off a subordinate clause from its main clause with a comma, by rule: “Ich komme bei euch vorbei, sobald ich Zeit habe” (“I will come by as soon as I have time”), and, when the clause stands inside the sentence, a comma on each side of it: “Ich komme, sobald ich Zeit habe, bei euch vorbei.”
27. In a series, *The Chicago Manual of Style* puts a comma before the last “and,” as in “red, white, and blue” (the serial comma), whereas newspapers that follow the Associated Press usually leave it out.
28. French and German normally put no comma before “et” and “und”: “Ses parents, ses amis et ses collègues assistaient au lancement” (“His parents, his friends, and his colleagues attended the launch”); “Feuer, Wasser, Luft und Erde” (“fire, water, air, and earth”). French allows the comma when the writer wishes to stress the last item.
29. English writers disagree about whether a complete sentence after a colon should begin with a capital; Chicago now recommends one. French uses the colon freely to give a cause or a consequence, where it can stand for “car” (“for”) or “donc” (“so”), and the next word takes a small letter. German requires a capital when a complete sentence follows the colon, but not when a mere list follows.
30. French: “Je comprends le bonheur de Rosalie : son père est enfin revenu” (“I understand Rosalie’s happiness: her father has come back at last”). German: “Beachten Sie folgenden Hinweis: Alle Bänke sind frisch gestrichen” (“Please note: all the benches have just been painted”), but “Er hat alles verspielt: sein Haus, seine Jacht, seine Pferde” (“He has gambled everything away: his house, his yacht, his horses”).
31. The three languages also introduce and mark a quotation differently. English puts a comma before the quotation: *She said, “I like it here.”* French puts a colon, with a space before it, and encloses the quotation in guillemets (« »), with a space inside each: *Elle a dit : « Je me plais ici. »* In France a thin space also comes before the semicolon, the question mark, and the exclamation point, although Quebec usage omits it. German puts a colon with no space before it, and uses quotation marks that open low and close high, or guillemets turned inward (»«): *Sie sagte: „Hier gefällt es mir.“*
32. Because punctuation shows how the words of a sentence go together, a change in the punctuation can change the sense. “Let’s eat, Grandpa” invites Grandpa to dinner, whereas “Let’s eat Grandpa” makes him the dinner.
33. “The students, who had finished, went home” says that all the students had finished and gone home, whereas “The students who had finished went home” says that only those who had finished went home.
34. A well-known Latin example is the oracle reported to a soldier going to war: *ibis redibis nunquam per bella peribis*. With a pause after *redibis*, it means “You will go, you will return, never in war will you perish”; with a pause after *nunquam*, it means “You will go, you will never return, in war you will perish.” The words are the same, and only the division of the sentence decides which prophecy was given.
35. Punctuation stands between the study of sounds and the study of construction: it marks pauses in the voice, but the pauses follow the sense.
36. A line of verse is measured by a fixed pattern of syllables, and it ends when the pattern is complete, whether or not the sense is complete. So a sentence may run on past the end of one line and come to rest in the middle of the next, as Milton’s does when *Paradise Lost* opens: “Of Man’s first disobedience, and the fruit / Of that forbidden tree…” In reading poetry, then, we must attend to two things at once: to the pauses of the sense, which punctuation marks, and to the divisions of the meter.

## Feet and Meter (`ii.meter`)

1. When we recite “Mary had a little lamb” or sing a hymn, we hear a beat that returns in the same pattern in every line, and each line ends when the pattern is complete. **Verse** is speech arranged in lines, each of which follows a fixed pattern of such syllables, and the pattern is called the **meter** (Greek *metron*, “measure”).
2. Diomedes defines meter, in substance, as an arrangement of feet in a fixed order, limited to a definite length.
3. Prose has rhythm too, but its rhythm is not fixed in a repeated pattern. So the same words may be prose or verse according to whether their syllables are arranged by a rule of measure.
4. The grammarian studies meter because the poets were the first authors that the student read, and they could not be read aloud correctly, or understood fully, without knowing how their lines were measured.
5. When we read aloud “Twinkle, twinkle, little star,” the beat falls on every other syllable, so the line divides into equal groups of a strong syllable and a weak one: TWIN-kle | TWIN-kle | LIT-tle | STAR. A **foot** (Latin *pes*) is the least group of syllables that is repeated in a meter.
6. In Latin and Greek verse the feet are made of long and short syllables. In the marks used for scansion, – stands for a long syllable and ∪ for a short one.
7. A **dactyl** (– ∪ ∪) is a long syllable and two short ones, as in Latin *Rōmulus*.
8. A **spondee** (– –) is two long syllables, as in *Rōmā* (“from Rome”).
9. A **trochee** (– ∪) is a long and a short, as in *Rōma*.
10. An **iamb** (∪ –) is a short and a long, as in *legō* (“I read”).
11. An **anapest** (∪ ∪ –) is two short syllables and a long, as in *bonitās* (“goodness”).
12. The names of the feet are Greek, and the doctrine of the feet, like the doctrine of accent, came to the Latin grammarians from the Greeks. English uses the same names for feet made of stressed and unstressed syllables.
13. When a word ends in a vowel, or in a vowel followed by *m*, and the next word begins with a vowel or with *h*, the final syllable of the first word is not counted in the meter. This is called **elision** (Latin *elisio*, “striking out”). In Latin the vowel is still written. English poets sometimes leave out of print a vowel that is not counted, and mark the gap with an apostrophe, as Shakespeare does in “Th’ expense of spirit in a waste of shame”.
14. In Virgil’s *multum ille et terris* (*Aeneid* I.3), the *-um* of *multum* and the *-e* of *ille* are elided, and the words are measured as *mult’ ill’ et terrīs*.
15. The opening of Longfellow’s *Evangeline*, “This is the forest primeval. The murmuring pines and the hemlocks,” has six beats, and most of them are followed by two weaker syllables: THIS is the | FOR-est pri | ME-val. The | MUR-mur-ing | PINES and the | HEM-locks.
16. The **dactylic hexameter** is the meter of Greek and Latin epic, the meter of Homer, Virgil, and Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*. It is a line of six feet. Each of the first four feet may be a dactyl or a spondee; the fifth foot is nearly always a dactyl; and the sixth foot has two syllables, the last of which may be long or short.
17. So the close of the line, – ∪ ∪ | – –, is the most fixed part of it, and in measuring a line we begin from there.
18. The first line of the *Aeneid* is *Arma virumque cano, Troiae qui primus ab oris*. With its long vowels marked, it is measured *Ar-ma vi | rum-que ca | nō Trō | iae quī | prī-mus ab | ō-rīs*: dactyl, dactyl, spondee, spondee, dactyl, and a final foot of two syllables. The first syllable of *Arma* is long by position, and the *a* of *ab* is short because it is followed by only one consonant before the next vowel.
19. A **caesura** (Latin, “cutting”) is a break between words that falls within a foot, not at the end of one. In the hexameter the principal caesura usually falls in the third foot, after its first syllable.
20. In the first line of Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* the caesura falls after *animus*: *In nova fert animus ‖ mutatas dicere formas*, “My mind is bent to tell of bodies changed into new forms”. It is measured *In no-va | fert a-ni | mus ‖ mū | tā-tās | dī-ce-re | for-mās*, so the break falls after the first syllable of the third foot.
21. If every word ended exactly where a foot ends, each foot would stand alone, and the line would fall into six equal pieces. When a word runs across the end of a foot, as *animus* runs from the second foot into the third, the word ties the two feet together, and the line holds as one whole.
22. So a line of verse has two kinds of division at once: the feet divide it by the meter, and the words divide it by the sense. Punctuation marks the pauses of the sense; the caesura is the place within a foot where such a pause, or at least a break between words, falls inside the measure.
23. English verse has no fixed caesura, and in speaking of it the word is used more loosely for any strong pause within the line, such as the pauses after each “tomorrow” in Macbeth’s line “Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow”.
24. Greek verse is measured by quantity in the same way as Latin. The first line of the *Iliad* is a hexameter: μῆνιν ἄειδε θεὰ Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος (*mēnin aeide thea Pēlēïadeō Achilēos*), “The wrath sing, goddess, of Peleus’ son, Achilles”. It is measured *mē-nin a | ei-de the | a Pē | lē-i-a | deō A-chi | lē-os*, with the two vowels of *deō* pronounced as one syllable.
25. The written accents of this line have nothing to do with its meter; the meter counts only long and short syllables. So in Greek the accent and the meter are independent of each other, and a reader must attend to both.
26. The **elegiac couplet** is a hexameter followed by a shorter line called the **pentameter**. Coleridge’s lines describe it: “In the hexameter rises the fountain’s silvery column; / In the pentameter aye falling in melody back.” The first line runs on to its full length, and the second is shorter and comes to rest twice.
27. The pentameter has two halves of two and a half feet each, with a break between them: – ∪ ∪ | – ∪ ∪ | – ‖ – ∪ ∪ | – ∪ ∪ | –. In the first half a spondee may take the place of either dactyl, but the second half keeps its dactyls.
28. Ovid says he meant to write of war in hexameters, but Cupid stole a foot from every second line. The second line of the first poem of the *Amores*, *Edere, materia conveniente modis*, is a pentameter; with its long vowels marked, it is measured *ē-de-re | mā-te-ri | ā ‖ con-ve-ni | en-te mo | dīs*.
29. The **hendecasyllable** (Greek, “of eleven syllables”) is a line of eleven syllables in a fixed pattern: – – – ∪ ∪ – ∪ – ∪ – –. Catullus begins a poem with *Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus* (“Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love”). The final *a* of *Lesbia* is elided before *atque*, and the final *e* of *atque* before *amemus*, so the line counts eleven syllables, not thirteen; they are measured *vī-vā-mus me-a Les-bi(a) at-qu(e) a-mē-mus*.
30. The **sapphic stanza**, named for the Greek poet Sappho, has three lines of eleven syllables, – ∪ – – – ∪ ∪ – ∪ – –, and a short fourth line, – ∪ ∪ – –. Catullus’s *Ille mi par esse deo videtur*, “He seems to me to be equal to a god”, is measured, with its long vowels marked, *il-le mī par es-se de-ō vi-dē-tur*. Horace uses the stanza in many of his odes.
31. An **iambic dimeter** is a line of four iambs, measured in pairs, in which the first and third feet may have a long syllable in place of the short one: ∪ – ∪ – ∪ – ∪ –, often – – ∪ – – – ∪ –.
32. Many English hymns are sung in lines of eight syllables with the beat on every second syllable, in stanzas of four lines, as in “Praise God, from whom all blessings flow” (praise GOD | from WHOM | all BLESS | ings FLOW). This form descends from the Latin hymns of the early Church.
33. St. Ambrose, bishop of Milan in the fourth century, wrote his hymns in the iambic dimeter, in stanzas of four lines. His morning hymn begins *Aeterne rerum conditor*; with its long vowels marked, it is measured *ae-ter-ne rē-rum con-di-tor*, – – ∪ – – – ∪ –. Bede calls this meter the iambic tetrameter because he counts single feet rather than pairs.
34. Ambrose’s hymns are measured by quantity, but in his lines the word accent usually falls on the beat of the meter. So these hymns stand at the point where Latin verse begins to move from a meter of quantity toward a meter of accent.
35. By the early Middle Ages the distinction of long and short vowels had disappeared from spoken Latin, and new kinds of verse appeared that counted syllables and accents instead of quantity. **Meter**, Bede says in substance, in his *De arte metrica* (written about 700), is an arrangement of words by the rules of quantity; **rhythm** (*rhythmus*) is an arrangement of words that is not measured by quantity but by the number of syllables, judged by the ear, as in the songs of the popular poets.
36. Bede adds that rhythm can exist without meter, but meter not without rhythm. So the later accentual verse of the Latin hymns, and the verse of the modern European languages, does not abandon the measure of verse; it measures by accent and by number of syllables instead of by quantity.
37. English verse is measured by stress, not by quantity. English syllables do differ in length, but their length follows the stress and does not distinguish words, so it is not the measure of the line.
38. An English **iamb** is unstressed and stressed, as in “be-HOLD.” An English **trochee** is stressed and unstressed, as in “TI-ger.” An English **anapest** is two unstressed and a stressed, as in “in the NIGHT.” An English **dactyl** is a stressed and two unstressed, as in “TEN-der-ly.”
39. A line is named by its foot and by the number of its feet: a line of four feet is a tetrameter, of five a pentameter, of six a hexameter.
40. The English iamb and the Latin iamb have the same name and the same order, but the English iamb is unstressed and stressed, whereas the Latin iamb is short and long. A stressed English syllable may be short in duration, and a Latin long syllable need not bear the accent.
41. The most common line of English verse is the **iambic pentameter**, a line of five iambs, which has ten syllables with the stress on the even ones. Shakespeare’s sonnet begins: “Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?” It is measured “shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer’s DAY,” with a light stress on “to.”
42. Unrhymed iambic pentameter is called **blank verse**; it is the line of Shakespeare’s plays and of Milton’s *Paradise Lost*.
43. Few lines are perfectly regular. The most common variation is a trochee in place of the first iamb, as at the beginning of John Donne’s sonnet “Death, be not proud,” where the first syllable is stressed. The meter is the pattern that the ear expects, and the variations are heard against that pattern.
44. So a reader measures an English line by marking the syllables that the words themselves stress, and then finding the pattern that fits most of the line.
45. Greek and Latin verse is measured by **quantity**, the pattern of long and short syllables. English verse is measured by **stress** as well as by the number of syllables. German verse is measured in the same way as English; Martin Opitz laid down that the beat of a German line must fall on the syllable that the word itself stresses.
46. French verse is measured chiefly by the **number of syllables**: the classical French line, the alexandrine, has twelve syllables, with a pause after the sixth, as in the first line of Racine’s *Phèdre*, *Le dessein en est pris, je pars, cher Théramène*, in which Hippolytus tells Theramenes that he has decided to leave.
47. So the measure of verse depends on the sound of the language. A language in which long and short syllables are distinct in their own right, as in Latin, can measure by quantity; a language with a strong stress, such as English or German, measures by stress; and a language with a weak stress, such as French, counts syllables.
48. This is the reason that a Latin meter cannot simply be copied into English by putting stresses where the Latin has long syllables.
