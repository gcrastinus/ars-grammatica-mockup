## Usage and Authority (`vi.usage`)

1. In this section the grammarians’ four criteria of correctness, reason (with analogy and etymology), antiquity, authority, and usage, are applied to particular questions, and when the criteria disagree, usage generally prevails.
2. The Latin name for correct speech was *Latīnitās*, as the Greek was *Hellēnismos*, and the plain English term is **correctness**.
3. The opposite of correctness is error, and the grammarians named its chief kinds the barbarism and the solecism.
4. Words change like all human works: some go out of use and some return, so a word correct in one age may be obsolete in the next.
5. For this reason the grammarian cannot judge correctness by the rules alone or by the oldest form alone; he must know the usage of his own time and of the authors he reads.
6. Horace states that usage (*ūsus*) has the decision, the right, and the standard of speech, and his line was quoted for centuries as the classic statement that usage is the judge of speech.
7. Usage, as Quintilian defines it, is the agreed practice of educated speakers, not whatever most people say, so a common form may still not be good usage.
8. The grammarians learn what educated speakers agree on from the authors, from the speech of those trained in the language, and from the agreement of good dictionaries and grammars.
9. Usage also differs according to the kind of speech, so a form may be correct in conversation and not in formal writing, or the reverse.
10. A question of usage is therefore often a question of fitness to the occasion, and the grammarian judges not only whether a form is used but where and by whom.
11. The authors are the second witness of correct usage, and Quintilian warns the teacher not to treat their departures from the rule as errors.
12. When a good author departs from the rule, the grammarian first asks whether the departure is a figure or a license made for a reason, or a form that was correct in the author’s time.
13. Only when no such reason can be found does the grammarian call the form an error, and even then he explains the author rather than rewriting him.
14. A **prescriptive** grammar states how the language should be used; a **descriptive** grammar states how it is used.
15. The English grammarians of the eighteenth century were largely prescriptive, and some of their preferences were judged by Latin models, whereas many modern linguists are strictly descriptive and decline to call any established usage incorrect.
16. The classical grammarian stands between these attitudes: he describes, because usage and authority are facts about how the best speakers and writers use the language, and he prescribes, because those facts give a standard by which a particular form can be judged.
17. A form that begins as an error may become correct when educated speakers and writers adopt it, as words change their meaning by usage.
18. The grammarian judges a new usage by whether it has been accepted by the speakers and authors whose usage sets the standard, not by whether it agrees with the origin of the word.
19. A disputed usage remains disputed while good authors disagree, and then the grammarian says that it is disputed and in which kinds of writing each form is accepted.
20. The grammarians named the principal kinds of error by where the error lies.
21. A **barbarism** is an error in a single word, in its letters, syllables, accent, or form (“growed” for “grew”).
22. A **solecism** is an error in the arrangement of words.
23. An **impropriety** (*acyrologia*, “improper speech”) is the use of a word in a sense it does not have, and Donatus counts it among the other faults of speech (*vitia*).
24. Each kind of error has a corresponding license: a change in a single word allowed in verse is a metaplasm, a departure from the rules of construction made for a reason is a figure, and a word used for a reason in a sense not its own is a trope.

## Barbarism (`vi.barbarism`)

1. An error that lies in a single word, considered by itself apart from the words around it, is called a **barbarism**.
2. Donatus defines the barbarism as one part of speech faulty in ordinary speech, and he adds that in a poem the same thing is called a metaplasm.
3. Quintilian likewise defines the barbarism as an offense occurring in single words.
4. The name comes from the Greek *barbaros*, “foreigner,” so a barbarism is in origin a word spoken as a foreigner would speak it, with the wrong sound, form, or accent.
5. A barbarism can lie in the word as it is written, as a misspelling does, or in the word as it is spoken, as a mispronunciation does.
6. Quintilian distinguishes the two: what is incorrect in writing is also incorrect in speech, but not the reverse, because errors of writing arise by addition, omission, substitution, or transposition, whereas errors of speaking arise from the separation or combination of syllables, from aspiration, and from other errors of sound.
7. Putting the accent on the wrong syllable was counted an error of the same kind.
8. English, with its loose relation of spelling to sound, has many barbarisms that occur only in writing (“would of” for “would have”).
9. Most barbarisms change a word in one of four ways: a letter or syllable is added, omitted, put in place of another, or moved to the wrong place.
10. Quintilian names these four as the most common kind of barbarism, and one word can contain two barbarisms at once.
11. English barbarisms fall into the same four kinds: **addition**, **omission**, **substitution**, and **transposition**.
12. A barbarism may also lie in the form of a word, when a speaker applies a regular pattern where usage has fixed an irregular one, or the reverse (“swimmed” for “swam”).
13. Such forms follow the regular patterns of the language but are barbarisms because usage has fixed other forms, and they are the plainest examples of analogy yielding to usage.
14. Latin has the same kind of barbarism, so learning the irregular forms of a language one by one is the way to avoid the barbarisms of form.
15. A borrowed word is not a barbarism once educated usage has accepted it, but a foreign word used where a native word would serve, or before it is understood, is open to objection.
16. Quintilian names a kind of barbarism that is due to race, the insertion of a foreign term, which was a barbarism in the original sense of the name.
17. Some foreign words were established by good authors, so the criterion of usage decides when a foreign word has become part of the language.
18. A barbarism is an error in the form of a word, whereas to use a correctly formed word in a sense that it does not have is a different error, an impropriety.
19. The distinction matters because the remedy differs: a barbarism is corrected by restoring the right form of the word, and an impropriety by choosing the right word.
20. Both are errors in a single word, so both belong with the barbarism rather than with the solecism, which lies in the arrangement of several words.
21. Donatus calls the same change a barbarism in ordinary speech and a metaplasm in a poem, and a poet is defended on the plea of poetic license.
22. Shortenings made in verse for the sake of the meter would be errors in prose.
23. The poet’s license does not abolish the rule but presupposes it, because the reader recognizes the changed form only by knowing the regular one.

## Solecism (`vi.solecism`)

1. An error not in a word by itself but in the construction of words with one another is called a **solecism**.
2. Donatus defines the solecism as a fault in the joining of the parts of speech, made against the rule of the art of grammar.
3. The name was explained in antiquity from Soli, a city in Cilicia whose settlers were said to have corrupted their Greek, and whether or not the story is true, the name came to mean an error in the arrangement of words.
4. An incongruous construction is one whose parts do not fit, and a solecism is such a construction.
5. In a solecism neither word is incorrect by itself; the error is in joining them.
6. It might be objected that the error lies in a single word, because changing one word corrects the sentence, but Quintilian answers that neither word is faulty in itself, and the error arises from its association with another word, so the fault lies in the phrase.
7. This is why the solecism belongs to construction and the barbarism to the single word: a barbarism is wrong wherever it occurs, whereas a solecism is wrong only in relation to the words with which it is joined.
8. A single word spoken alone can still be wrongly constructed, if it does not fit what it addresses or the question it answers.
9. Quintilian concludes that a solecism may occur in one word, but never in a word in isolation.
10. So the construction may be with something that is not a word, such as the situation of speaking or the question that was asked, and the solecism is still an error of agreement.
11. Quintilian reports that the fullest treatments divide solecisms into the same four kinds as barbarisms: addition, omission, transposition, and substitution.
12. Substitution, when one word is used instead of another, is the most common kind, and it can occur with all the parts of speech, but most often with the verb.
13. Because a solecism is an error of construction, it usually lies in an accident that the construction requires: a gender, number, case, person, tense, or mood that does not agree with another word or is not the one governed.
14. In English, where fewer accidents are shown, the common solecisms are of number, of case in pronouns, and of tense.
15. The study of agreement and government is therefore also the study of how to avoid solecisms, since each rule marks a place where a solecism can occur.
16. The remedy for a solecism is to restore the construction that the rule requires.
17. English handbooks name several errors of construction that are not errors of accident but of arrangement.
18. A **sentence fragment** presents a dependent clause or a phrase as a sentence.
19. A **run-on sentence** or **comma splice** joins two independent clauses without a proper conjunction or mark.
20. A **dangling modifier** is a participial phrase with no word in the sentence to depend on.
21. An **unclear pronoun** has no clear antecedent.
22. Each of these is a failure of the relations of construction: the fragment fails in completeness, the run-on in the joining of clauses, the dangling modifier in dependence, and the unclear pronoun in the reference that agreement presupposes.
23. The same departure from the rules may be a solecism or a figure, and Quintilian calls figures those phrases that have the appearance of a solecism but are used by good authors.
24. But Quintilian warns that a figure used unwittingly will be a solecism.
25. So the difference between a figure and a solecism lies not in the words alone but in the reason for them and the knowledge of the speaker, and the grammarian judges whether the departure was made with art.
26. Some sentences break no rule of agreement or government and yet use words badly, because they say the same thing more than once or can be taken in two ways.
27. Donatus lists further faults of speech (*vitia*) of this kind, which together with the barbarism and the solecism make twelve.
28. The ten besides the barbarism and the solecism are impropriety, an ill-sounding or indecent expression (*cacenphaton*), pleonasm, superfluous words (*perissologia*), long-windedness (*macrologia*), tautology, the omission of a needed word (*eclipsis*), the lowering of a great thing by a mean word (*tapinosis*), a bad arrangement of words (*cacosyntheton*), and amphibolia.
29. **Pleonasm** is a word added to a meaning already complete.
30. **Tautology** is the needless repetition of the same idea.
31. **Amphibolia** is a construction that can be taken in two ways, that is, ambiguity in the arrangement of words.
32. These other faults are not errors of agreement or government, but they are errors in the use of words in construction, and Donatus treats them with the solecism for that reason.

## Metaplasm (`vi.metaplasm`)

1. Poetry uses many changed forms of words, usually to reduce the number of syllables so that the line fits the meter, and in ordinary speech these changes would be barbarisms.
2. Donatus defines the **metaplasm** (Greek *metaplasmos*, “reshaping”) as a change of correct, plain speech into another form, made for the sake of the meter or of ornament.
3. So a metaplasm is a barbarism permitted to the poets for a reason.
4. Donatus counts fourteen kinds of metaplasm, which work on the letters and syllables of a single word, and they can be grouped by the kind of change.
5. Three metaplasms add a letter or syllable: **prosthesis** (“putting before”) adds at the beginning, **epenthesis** (“putting in”) adds in the middle, and **paragoge** (“leading past”) adds at the end.
6. The added letter or syllable usually serves the meter, by lengthening a syllable or adding one.
7. Three metaplasms remove a letter or syllable: **aphaeresis** (“taking away”) removes from the beginning, **syncope** (“cutting short”) removes from the middle, and **apocope** (“cutting off”) removes from the end (“’tis” for “it is”).
8. These are the metaplasms most often met in English poetry, because English verse counts syllables and stresses, and a poet who needs one syllable fewer can shorten a word.
9. In prose the same shortened forms would be barbarisms, or at least informal.
10. Two metaplasms change the quantity of a syllable: **ectasis** (“stretching out”) makes a short syllable long, and **systole** (“drawing together”) makes a long syllable short.
11. These belong to the quantitative verse of Latin and Greek, and they show that even the quantity of a syllable, fixed in ordinary speech, may be changed by the poet when the meter requires it.
12. So the grammarian who scans a line must know both the regular quantity and the licenses.
13. Two metaplasms change the number of syllables without adding or removing letters: **diaeresis** (“division”) divides one syllable into two, and **episynaloephe** (“joining together”) makes two syllables into one.
14. English poetry uses both, and the mark that shows that two vowels are pronounced separately takes its name from the metaplasm and is called a diaeresis.
15. Two metaplasms occur where one word meets another: **synaloepha** (“melting together”) is the elision of a final vowel before a word beginning with a vowel, and **ecthlipsis** (“squeezing out”) is the elision of a final syllable ending in *m* before a vowel.
16. These are the elisions of Latin verse, and Donatus counts them among the metaplasms because they change how a word is pronounced in the line.
17. The last two metaplasms change or move a letter: **antithesis** (“setting against”) puts one letter in place of another, and **metathesis** (“transposition”) moves letters to another place.
18. Donatus’s fourteen metaplasms are prosthesis, epenthesis, paragoge, aphaeresis, syncope, apocope, ectasis, systole, diaeresis, episynaloephe, synaloepha, ecthlipsis, antithesis, and metathesis.
19. Each corresponds to one of the four ways of the barbarism (adding, removing, changing, and moving) or to a change of quantity or of syllables, and each is permitted only in verse and for a reason.
20. A metaplasm is permitted because it is made for the meter or for ornament and because the reader can recognize the regular form behind it.
21. But the license has limits: the same forms used in prose, or used without need in verse, are barbarisms.
22. So the errors of speech (the barbarism in a single word, the solecism in the arrangement of words, and the other errors of speech) have their corresponding licenses (the metaplasm in a single word and the figure in construction).
