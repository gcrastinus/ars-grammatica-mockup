## Literal and Figurative Sense (`vii.literal`)

1. A word signifies by imposition, and what a word signifies by its imposition is its **proper signification** (Latin *propria significātiō*).
2. Speakers also use words of things other than those for which they were imposed.
3. Quintilian defines a trope as the artistic alteration of a word or phrase from its proper meaning to another.
4. In such a use the word keeps its imposition but is used of something else, and the hearer must understand both what it properly signifies and what it is used to signify here.
5. A word used in its proper signification is used **literally** (from Latin *littera*, “letter,” that is, according to the letter of the words).
6. A word used of something other than its proper signification, by some likeness or relation, is used **figuratively**.
7. In a comparison the word is still used literally and the likeness is stated, whereas in the figurative use the likeness is not stated and the word itself is carried over (“like a lion” and “He is a lion”).
8. Many figurative uses have become so common that speakers no longer recognize them as figurative, and dictionaries list them as ordinary senses of the word (the “leg” of a table).
9. Often such a use was necessary, because the language had no proper word for the thing, and Quintilian observes the same necessity in Latin.
10. So the boundary between literal and figurative use can change with usage, because a figurative use that becomes established may become a second proper signification of the word.
11. Augustine divides signs into literal and figurative: signs are literal when they point out the things for which they were imposed, and figurative when the thing that the literal word signifies is itself used to signify something else.
12. Augustine’s figurative sign differs from the trope: in a trope a word is carried over to a new thing, whereas in Augustine’s figurative sign the word keeps its literal meaning and the thing it signifies becomes a sign of something further.
13. Both require the reader first to understand what the words properly signify, which is the grammarian’s task.
14. In ordinary reading, an expression is figurative when its literal sense is impossible or absurd in its context and a related sense fits.
15. But an expression should not be taken as figurative merely because its literal sense is surprising, so the reader must know the proper signification of the words, attend to the context, and ask what the author intended.
16. Augustine’s rule for Scripture is that whatever cannot, taken literally, be referred to purity of life or soundness of doctrine should be taken as figurative.
17. Augustine also warns against the opposite error of taking figurative expressions literally, which is to take signs for things.
18. St. Thomas defines the literal sense as the sense that the author intends.
19. He holds that a figurative expression does not depart from the literal sense, because the literal sense is not the figure itself but what is figured (God’s “arm” signifies God’s power).
20. This is a precise use of “literal sense,” different from the everyday use of “literally” for the proper signification of each word.
21. So the grammarian keeps both in view: the proper signification of each word, and the sense that the author intends by the whole expression.
22. Fixed groups of words whose meaning cannot be found from the meanings of the separate words are called **idioms** (from Greek *idiōma*, “a peculiarity”).
23. An idiom is like a compound word: its parts do not signify by themselves within it, and it must be learned as a whole.
24. Idioms are one of the chief difficulties of translation, because an idiom translated word for word is usually meaningless or misleading.
25. Three marks help a reader decide whether a word is used literally or figuratively: the context, the kind of writing, and the author’s intention.
26. Poetry and oratory use figurative words more freely than law or science, and the author’s intention is learned from the whole work and its occasion.
27. The common use of “literally” for emphasis with a figurative expression is avoided in careful usage, because it confuses the very distinction that the word names.
28. A figurative use is first a fact about signification, so explaining it belongs to the grammarian, whose tasks include explaining the figures of speech in the poets.
29. Grammar asks what the words signify and how the figurative sense is related to the proper one; rhetoric asks what effect the figure has on the hearer and when it should be used.
30. The grammarians divide figurative language into two kinds, tropes and figures.
31. A **trope** changes the signification of a word.
32. A **figure** changes the arrangement of words without necessarily changing their signification.
33. The figures of construction belong to construction; the figures of words arrange words for force and ornament, and the tropes change their signification.

## Figures of Words (*schemata lexeos*) (`vii.schemata`)

1. A figure gives language an arrangement other than the plain and ordinary one.
2. A **figure of words** (Greek *schēma lexeōs*) is an arrangement of the words themselves, by repetition, omission, or order, whereas a figure of thought concerns the way the thought is presented, whatever the words.
3. Donatus distinguishes the two kinds and assigns the figures of thought to the orators and the figures of words to the grammarians.
4. Donatus names seventeen principal figures of words: prolepsis, zeugma, hypozeuxis, syllepsis, anadiplosis, anaphora, epanalepsis, epizeuxis, paronomasia, schesis onomaton, paromoeon, homoeoptoton, homoeoteleuton, polyptoton, hirmos, polysyndeton, and dialyton (asyndeton).
5. They can be grouped by what they do to the words: some join or divide constructions, some repeat a word, some repeat a sound or an ending, and some add or omit connecting words.
6. In each case the figure is recognized by comparing it with the plain arrangement from which it departs.
7. **Prolepsis** (“taking beforehand”) states a whole first and then divides it into its parts, each with its own construction (“the one … the other”).
8. In prolepsis the parts strictly have no verb of their own and depend on the verb of the whole, so the figure departs from regular construction, but it is clear because the whole has already been named.
9. **Zeugma** closes several clauses with one verb, and Donatus counts it, with syllepsis, among the figures of words as well as of construction.
10. **Syllepsis** construes one word with several that differ in gender or number, agreeing with only one of them.
11. **Hypozeuxis** (“yoking under”), the opposite of zeugma, gives each clause its own verb.
12. So zeugma and hypozeuxis are two ways of arranging the same thought, one with a single verb shared by every clause and one with a verb in each.
13. The repetition of the same word at the beginning of several clauses or lines is **anaphora** (“carrying back”), and the repeated word marks the parallel construction.
14. The repetition at the end of a line or clause of the word with which it began is **epanalepsis** (“taking up again”), and the repeated word encloses the line and marks it as one unit.
15. Repeating the last word of one clause or line at the beginning of the next, so that the second is linked to the first, is **anadiplosis** (“doubling back”).
16. When anadiplosis is repeated through several clauses, so that each rises from the last, the series is called a **climax** (“ladder”).
17. The immediate repetition of a word with no other word between is **epizeuxis** (“fastening together”).
18. The four figures of repetition differ by the place of the repeated word: at the beginning of several clauses (anaphora), at the beginning and end of one (epanalepsis), at the end of one and the beginning of the next (anadiplosis), and immediately in succession (epizeuxis).
19. In each figure of repetition the repetition adds no new meaning to the construction, but it binds the words together and marks their arrangement.
20. Placing together words that are alike in sound but different in meaning is **paronomasia** (“naming beside”).
21. Paronomasia is related to homonymy but does not use one word in two senses; it places two different words side by side because they sound alike, and the modern English term is a **pun**.
22. Beginning several words with the same letter or sound is **paromoeon** (“made alike”), in English usually called **alliteration**.
23. A series of nouns, often each with its adjective, set side by side without verbs or conjunctions is **schesis onomaton** (“a holding of nouns”).
24. Paromoeon and schesis onomaton arrange words for their sound or their accumulation rather than for any new construction, and both are common in poetry.
25. **Homoeoptoton** (“alike in case”) places together several words in the same case, so that their endings correspond.
26. **Homoeoteleuton** (“alike in ending”) places together words or clauses that end in the same sound, whether or not they are in the same case.
27. These figures are especially natural in Latin, whose endings make many words alike in sound; in English the corresponding figure is rhyme, found in prose chiefly in proverbs.
28. These figures depend on the accidents of the words, case and ending.
29. Repeating the same word in different cases or forms is **polyptoton** (“many cases”).
30. English uses polyptoton with different forms of a word, whereas Latin can repeat the same word in different cases, so the figure shows the accidents of a word at work in the construction.
31. A long sentence whose sense is carried through many members to the end without a break is **hirmos** (“a series,” “a chain”).
32. Hirmos is related to the period but is less strictly divided into members, and the subject and the words that depend on it must be held in mind until the verb completes them.
33. The plain construction of a series uses one conjunction before the last member.
34. **Polysyndeton** (“many joinings”) uses more conjunctions than are needed.
35. **Asyndeton** (“unjoined”; Donatus’s *dialyton*) omits the conjunctions altogether.
36. These two figures are recognized by comparing the number of conjunctions with the number that the construction requires.
37. The figures of words can be classified by what they do to the plain construction: some omit words the construction would supply, some add words it does not need, some change the regular agreement, and some change the order.
38. Quintilian likewise sums up the figures of words as those that consist in change, addition, omission, and the order of words.
39. Each figure of words is a departure from the plain construction and can be understood only by one who knows it, so the figures are studied after the rules of construction.
40. The grammarian explains a figure by restoring the plain form and showing how the author has departed from it.

## Tropes (`vii.tropes`)

1. A use of a word that changes its signification is a trope.
2. Donatus defines the **trope** (Greek *tropos*, “a turning”) as a word carried over from its proper signification to a likeness that is not its own, for the sake of ornament or of need.
3. Donatus counts thirteen tropes; hyperbaton concerns the order of words and belongs with the figures of order, and homoeosis, by which something less known is shown through its likeness to something better known, is not treated separately.
4. Quintilian observes that some tropes are used to help out the meaning and others to adorn the style.
5. Bede took Donatus’s list and gave examples from Scripture, so that the medieval student learned the tropes as a means of reading the Bible as well as the poets.
6. The use of a word for something that it resembles is **metaphor** (Greek *metaphora*, “carrying over”; Latin *trānslātiō*).
7. Quintilian calls metaphor the commonest and most beautiful of tropes, used where there is no literal term or where the transferred term is better than the literal one.
8. A comparison states the likeness, whereas a metaphor implies it in the word itself, so a metaphor is a shorter form of simile.
9. Donatus and Quintilian divide metaphors into four kinds: from one living thing to another, from one lifeless thing to another, from the living to the lifeless, and from the lifeless to the living.
10. The use of the nearest available word for something that has no proper name of its own is **catachresis** (Greek, “misuse”; Latin *abūsiō*).
11. Quintilian distinguishes catachresis from metaphor: catachresis is used where no proper term is available, and metaphor where another term is available.
12. A trope that passes through an intermediate step, so that one word is substituted for another by way of a third, is **metalepsis** (Greek, “substitution”; Latin *trānsūmptiō*).
13. Metalepsis is rare, and Quintilian regards it as a trope to recognize rather than to use.
14. The use of one name for another that is associated with it, not by likeness but by some other relation, is **metonymy** (“change of name”).
15. In metonymy the word keeps its proper signification but is used of something related to what it signifies, and the hearer supplies the relation.
16. Quintilian lists the kinds of metonymy: the inventor for the invention, the possessor for the possession, the container for the contained, the author for his work, and the cause for the effect.
17. Putting a description or an epithet in place of a proper name is **antonomasia** (“naming instead”).
18. Antonomasia may substitute an epithet equivalent to the name or indicate the most striking characteristics of an individual, and it also works the other way, when a proper name is used for a type.
19. An adjective or descriptive phrase joined to a noun to adorn it is an **epithet** (“something added”), and poets use it freely when it suits the word.
20. The epithet adds a description to a name, whereas antonomasia replaces the name with the description.
21. **Synecdoche** (“understanding together”) puts the part for the whole or the whole for the part, the species for the genus or the genus for the species, and also the singular for the plural.
22. Synecdoche differs from metonymy in that its relation is one of part and whole, or of species and genus, rather than of cause, container, or author.
23. The formation of a word from the sound of the thing it names is **onomatopoeia** (“the making of a name”).
24. Even imitative words are imposed, since each language chooses its own imitation, so onomatopoeia is still imposition, guided by likeness of sound.
25. Using several words where one or a few would suffice is **periphrasis** (“speaking around”; Latin *circumlocūtiō*).
26. Periphrasis is sometimes necessary, to conceal what would be indecent if said plainly, and at other times decorative, chiefly among the poets.
27. Periphrasis is a virtue only when it adorns, and in excess it becomes perissology, that is, wordiness.
28. Words that go beyond the truth, by enlarging or by lessening, make the trope called **hyperbole** (“throwing beyond”).
29. Hyperbole is not a lie, because it involves no intention to deceive and no definite assertion of what is not; the hearer takes the words as signifying only that the fact is great or small.
30. Speech that says one thing and means another is **allegory** (“speaking otherwise”).
31. Quintilian distinguishes two kinds of allegory: one presents one thing in words and another in meaning, and is usually a series of metaphors; the other says something contrary to the meaning of the words.
32. So an allegory of the first kind is a continued metaphor, and its meaning lies within the literal sense in St. Thomas’s terms, because it is what the author intends.
33. A statement whose words keep their meaning but whose sense is the contrary is **irony** (Greek *eirōneia*, “dissembling”).
34. Quintilian and Donatus count irony as a species of allegory, and Donatus names other species, such as the riddle, the proverb, and sarcasm.
35. Irony is made evident by the delivery, the character of the speaker, or the nature of the subject, because when any of these is out of keeping with the words, the speaker’s intention is plainly other than what he says.
36. In every trope the word keeps its imposition, and the figurative signification is understood through the proper one.
37. So the tropes do not abolish the order of signification but presuppose it.
