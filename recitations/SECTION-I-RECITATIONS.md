## Sign and Symbol (`i.sign`)

1. The material of grammar is speech, that is, the voice as it signifies thoughts in the mind, which the classical grammarians called **significant voice** (Latin *vox significativa*), and as it is arranged into phrases and sentences.
2. Something that signifies (that has meaning) is a sign; so a word is a kind of sign.
3. Before grammar considers the sounds and letters of which words are made, it must say what a sign is and how a word signifies.
4. A sign is a thing which, besides the impression it makes on the senses, brings something else to mind (St. Augustine); or, “that which represents something other than itself to a cognitive power” (John of St. Thomas).
5. A sign is first something we perceive (a sound, a gesture, writing), but it brings something else to mind by means of what we perceive.
6. Every sign involves three things: the sign itself, what it signifies, and someone who knows by means of it.
7. A sign signifies only to a knowing power; so the study of signs is always also a study of how something is known by someone.
8. St. Augustine divides signs into two kinds, natural signs and conventional signs.
9. A natural sign signifies because of what it is and how it is connected with what it signifies, not because anyone means it to signify (smoke signifies fire).
10. A word is not a natural sign, since the sound of a word has no natural connection with what it signifies.
11. A conventional sign (*signa data*, literally “given signs”) signifies because someone means it to signify; it is given by one mind to another.
12. Conventional signs have their meaning from agreement and the convention of a social group.
13. Among the signs by which human beings express their thoughts, words hold the first place: what gestures signify can be put into words, but words cannot be put into gestures.
14. Words, then, are conventional signs: the sound of a word signifies its meaning only by agreement in a social group.
15. The concept is what the mind forms within itself when it understands something.
16. The spoken word signifies the concept directly, and through the concept it signifies the thing.
17. The concept is itself a sign of a special kind: we do not first notice it and then think of the thing; in conceiving, we simply know the thing.
18. Writing adds one more step: the written word is a sign of the spoken word, the spoken word is a sign of the concept, and the concept is of the thing.
19. The whole later tradition took this account from the opening of Aristotle’s *De interpretatione*.
20. The words that signify the same thing differ from language to language, but the concept of the same thing is the same for all.
21. The reason: two concepts of the same thing both represent that thing, and a concept has no medium through which it represents; so nothing distinguishes two concepts of the same thing except the thing they represent.
22. What makes one concept differ from another is the thing it represents, not the mind that forms it or the language in which it is expressed.
23. An expert and a novice have concepts of the same thing; the expert’s concept is only less confused and more clear.
24. Translation confirms this: it is possible only because the concepts are the same where the words differ.
25. This is also why grammar can study what is common to all languages as well as what belongs to one language alone.
26. When we speak, we speak about things, not about our concepts; yet the word cannot reach the thing directly.
27. The word “man” signifies what is common to all men, human nature taken apart from any one man, which exists only in the mind; so it signifies first the concept, and through the concept the things.
28. We can speak of things only as we understand them, and so the word reaches the thing by way of the concept.
29. This is why one thing can be named in many ways, according to the different ways in which it is understood.
30. Today “symbol” can mean a many-layered sign that is in a way more than a sign (as in literature), or, more narrowly, a mark that does the work of a word but is not made of letters (as “+”).
31. The Greek *symbolon* was a token, half of an object kept by each party to an agreement, which meant something only because of the agreement.
32. For Aristotle and the early tradition, “symbol” is simply another name for a conventional sign: words signify by agreement, not by nature.
33. The many-layered sense of “symbol” came later; in this course it belongs to the interpretation of literary authors, and in this first part an older author’s “symbol” is read as a conventional sign.
34. An instrumental sign is first known as an object in its own right, and it leads the mind from what it perceives to something else (smoke, a footprint, a spoken word).
35. A formal sign represents something other than itself without first being known as an object; it is that by which we know the thing, and it represents through itself alone, without any medium.
36. The concept is a formal sign: we know the thing by means of the concept without first noticing the concept, and we can reflect on the concept only afterward.
37. Words are instrumental signs, but they signify by means of concepts, which are formal signs.
38. This division was worked out most fully by John of St. Thomas.
39. Grammar begins with the sign because its material is speech, and speech is made of conventional signs.
40. A sound becomes a word only when it signifies; so the grammarian must know what it is for a sound to signify before he studies the sounds, their letters and syllables, and how words are formed and arranged.
41. The grammarians divided voices into those that signify and those that do not, and the significant voices into those that signify by nature (a groan) and those that signify by imposition (a word); only the last kind belongs to grammar.
42. So grammar is not the study of sounds in natural philosophy or phonetics: of all sounds, only the word belongs to grammar, because only the word signifies by the agreement of those who use it.

## Imposition (`i.imposition`)

1. Words do not have meaning by nature; those who use them assign them a meaning, a signification.
2. **Imposition** (Latin *impositio*, from *imponere*, “to place upon”) is the act by which a sound is first given a meaning.
3. A word that signifies in this way signifies by imposition, or *ad placitum*, that is, “by agreement” or “as it has pleased” those who use it.
4. Imposition still happens whenever we name something new.
5. For most words the first imposition is in the past and unknown, and their sounds have shifted over time; still, every word was imposed by someone at some time.
6. Those who learn a language do not impose its words again; they receive meanings already imposed.
7. So learning a word means learning what it has been used to signify, and a learner cannot simply decide for himself what a word will mean.
8. Because words are so powerful (by them we teach, state what is true, and move the mind), it can seem that they must have their meaning by nature.
9. For this reason many peoples have held that names are bound to things more closely than by agreement, which can lead to the belief that certain words and sounds are powerful in themselves, as in magic spells.
10. Whether names signify by nature or by convention is one of the oldest questions in the study of language.
11. In Plato’s *Cratylus*, Cratylus holds that names belong to things by nature, whereas Hermogenes holds that they are fixed by agreement and custom; Socrates grants something to each.
12. The tradition that follows Aristotle answers that the sound of a word is imposed, and so it is not by nature.
13. That answer does not deny everything in the other view: the one who imposes a name usually has a reason for the name he chooses.
14. The clearest evidence that words signify by imposition is that languages differ: if sounds signified by nature, every people would use the same sound for the same thing, as every people groans in the same way.
15. Even words that imitate a sound are imposed, since each language chooses its own (English “buzz”).
16. In the imposition of a name we distinguish that from which the name is taken, which explains why a sound was chosen, and that which the name is imposed to signify, which is fixed by the imposition and by usage (“daisy,” from “day’s eye,” signifies the flower).
17. The reason for a name and the meaning of the name are not the same.
18. This explains what was true in the view that names are by nature: many names have a reason in the things they name, but the reason does not make the sound signify by nature; it only explains why that sound was chosen.
19. St. Thomas draws the same distinction.
20. A word receives its meaning by imposition, but it keeps or changes that meaning by usage, the agreed practice of educated speakers.
21. When the speakers of a language come to use a word for something new, the word comes to signify the new thing.
22. Change of meaning by usage is one more sign that words signify by imposition, since a natural sign does not change its meaning when people change their usage.
23. Names of the **first imposition** signify things in the usual way; they were imposed first, to signify things.
24. Names of the **second imposition** signify words considered as words; they were imposed afterward, to signify words already imposed (“noun” in “‘Horse’ is a noun”).
25. Logic makes a similar distinction between first and second intentions, that is, concepts of things and concepts of concepts, but that belongs to logic.
26. For the grammarian, the logician’s words “genus” and “species” (not Latin *genus* in the sense of grammatical gender) are names of the first imposition, since they signify concepts and not words.
27. Grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly and must speak about words; so almost every technical term of grammar is a name of the second imposition.
28. A word spoken about as a word is marked to show that it is mentioned and not used: in this course an English word goes in double quotation marks and a Latin word in italics.
29. Because each people imposes its own sounds, languages differ in their words; but what the words signify, the concepts and the things, is the same for all.
30. The ways of signifying are also common to all languages: every language names things and says what they do or undergo, and marks who acts, when, and how many, because these are features of the world as it is known.
31. Real differences between languages are different ways of speaking about the same thing, which highlight things differently.
32. So the art of grammar can be studied in English and Latin together: it considers each language individually and also what is common to every language.

## Signification and Consignification (`i.consignification`)

1. The meaning that was imposed on a word, that is, what the word signifies, is its **signification** (Latin *significatio*).
2. A word keeps its signification from one sentence to the next.
3. What a word stands for in a particular sentence can vary; logic studies this under the name of supposition, the way a word stands for something in a sentence.
4. Some words signify something more along with their principal meaning; this is **consignification** (Latin *consignificatio*, “a signifying along with”).
5. What a word was imposed principally to signify is its proper meaning; what it signifies along with that meaning is consignified.
6. The verb consignifies time: “runs,” “ran,” and “will run” have the same proper meaning and differ in the time each consignifies. Aristotle names this as the mark of the verb.
7. Latin usually shows time by a change in the form of the verb; English shows it partly by a change in the verb and partly by auxiliary verbs, that is, helping verbs such as “will” and “has.”
8. The time that a verb consignifies is called **tense** (from the Latin *tempus*, “time”).
9. The noun does not consignify time: a noun such as “race” signifies the same motion as “run” but does not say whether it is past, present, or future.
10. Some nouns, such as “day” and “year,” signify time, but as their proper meaning, as a thing we can speak about; so to signify time and to consignify time are different.
11. One and the same action can be signified with or without time: the noun signifies it as a thing we can speak about, the verb as something happening at some time (*cursus* and *curro*).
12. The reason the verb consignifies time: it signifies an action as happening and as said of something, and whatever happens happens at some time, since motion and action are measured by time and known to us in time.
13. The noun signifies its meaning as a thing under our consideration, not as something going on, and so it does not signify when.
14. Besides time, a word consignifies other things, which the grammarians call its **accidents** (Latin *accidentia*, from *accidere*, “to happen to”).
15. They are called accidents because they belong to a word without changing its proper meaning or its part of speech, and the word only happens to have a given accident in a given context.
16. Besides tense, the chief accidents are number, person, case, mood, and gender.
17. Number: whether one or more than one is meant.
18. Person: whether the speaker, the one spoken to, or someone else is meant.
19. Case: the form that shows how a noun or pronoun is related to the other words of a sentence, for example as subject or as object.
20. Mood: the form of a verb that shows the manner in which the action is stated, for example as a fact, as a command, or as a wish or a possibility.
21. Gender: the class of a noun as masculine, feminine, or neuter.
22. Latin often shows several accidents in a single ending; English has fewer such forms and shows some accidents by other means.
23. Not every accident belongs to every word: case and gender belong to the noun, tense and mood to the verb, and number and person to both.
24. A word signifies as a whole, and its parts do not signify by themselves (“butter” in “butterfly”).
25. The endings that mark the accidents signify nothing by themselves; they mark, within the word, an accident that the whole word consignifies.
26. The word, therefore, is the least part of an expression that signifies.
27. One and the same reality can be signified in different ways: as a thing (“pain”), as something happening (“hurts”), or as a quality belonging to something else (“painful”).
28. The way in which a word presents what it signifies is its **mode of signifying** (Latin *modus significandi*; *modus* means “way” or “manner”).
29. The noun signifies by way of a thing or substance, something stable that can be spoken about; the verb by way of action or becoming, as something going on; the adjective by way of a quality inhering in something.
30. The mode of signifying is not a second meaning but the way the meaning is presented; since the word signifies it along with its meaning, the mode of signifying is also consignified.
31. The reason: words signify things by means of concepts, and the mind can understand one reality as a thing, as something happening, or as a quality of something else; words follow these ways of understanding.
32. The mode of signifying makes a word the part of speech that it is, not its sound or length, nor the reality it signifies.
33. This also explains time and the accidents: the verb consignifies time because it signifies by way of something happening, and each part of speech has the accidents that fit its mode of signifying.
34. Priscian teaches that the parts of speech are told apart by what is proper to the signification of each; the modists of the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries worked this out in detail through the modes of signifying.
35. Only when words are joined, as a noun and a verb, is something said of something.
36. What joins them is not a third word but something the verb itself signifies, since the verb signifies its meaning as belonging to something of which it is said.
37. So the verb signifies, along with its proper meaning and its time, a joining of what is said with what it is said of, which is called **composition** (Latin *compositio*, “a putting together”).
38. The verb “is” shows this most plainly: by itself it states nothing; it signifies a composition that cannot be understood until the things joined are given.
39. That is why a sentence that states something needs a verb: a list of nouns joins nothing, and so it says nothing of anything.
40. The verb signifies composition not principally but along with what it signifies, and every verb includes it, since to run is to be running.
41. No word by itself is true or false; there is truth or falsity only where something is said of something.
42. A sentence is true when what it says agrees with the way things are, and false when it does not.
43. Truth and falsity begin at the sentence; signification begins at the word.
44. Not every word signifies something definite by itself: some words, such as “every,” “not,” and “if,” have no definite signification alone, yet they can change the whole meaning of a sentence.

## Categorematic and Syncategorematic Words (`i.syncategorematic`)

1. Words can be divided according to whether they signify by themselves or only together with other words.
2. A word that signifies something definite by itself is a **categorematic** word.
3. Categorematic words can stand as the subject of a sentence (what the sentence is about) or as the predicate (what is said of what the sentence is about).
4. The name comes from the Greek *katēgorēma*, “what is said of something,” because such words can be said of things.
5. A word that does not signify something definite by itself, but signifies only together with other words by determining in what way those words are to be taken, is a **syncategorematic** word (“every,” “not,” “if”).
6. The name adds the Greek *syn*, “with”: a syncategorematic word signifies only with what is said.
7. The meaning of a syncategorematic word lies in what it does to the other words of the sentence.
8. Priscian reports that the dialecticians (the teachers of logic) counted only two parts of speech, the noun and the verb, because these alone, joined together, make a complete sentence.
9. The dialecticians called the other words *syncategoremata*, “consignifying” words, because they signify only together with the noun and the verb.
10. Here “consignify” has a second, less common sense: not to signify something along with a principal meaning, but to signify only along with other words.
11. Priscian, as a grammarian, counts eight parts of speech, but he grants the dialecticians’ point that a noun and a verb can make a sentence, whereas words such as “and,” “if,” or “every” cannot; the grammarians integrate this point into their account of grammar.
12. Syncategorematic words add no new thing to what is said; they determine how the things already named are to be taken, and so they can change the whole meaning of a sentence.
13. Where sentences share the same categorematic words, the whole difference in meaning comes from the syncategorematic words (“Every horse runs,” “No horse runs”).
14. The first kind of syncategorematic word signifies how much of something is meant (“every,” “some,” “no”).
15. The second kind excludes or excepts (“only,” “except”).
16. The third kind denies (“not”).
17. The fourth kind joins statements together (“if,” “and,” “or”).
18. In each case the word determines how the other words are to be taken: how many, which ones are left out, whether the thing is affirmed or denied, and how one statement stands to another.
19. The division into categorematic and syncategorematic words does not coincide with the division into parts of speech, because the two divisions answer different questions.
20. The parts of speech are distinguished by the way each word signifies and by how it is arranged with other words; the other division asks only whether a word signifies something definite by itself.
21. So syncategorematic words belong to several parts of speech (adjectives, adverbs, conjunctions, prepositions), and one word can be described under both divisions (“every” is an adjective and a syncategorematic word).
22. Grammar needs this distinction because syncategorematic words determine how the other words are taken, so a careful reader must notice them and a careful writer must place them exactly.
23. A small change in such a word, or in its position, can change the whole meaning of a sentence, and its position can leave a sentence open to two readings.
24. Although we learn these words with our mother tongue, the aim here is to understand language with precision and clarity, so that we can use it and interpret what others say with correctness and exactitude.
25. The words that signify how much is meant, and the word that denies, are the words on which the truth of a statement most often depends; so logic studies them closely and is built in large part on them.
26. Grammar names the syncategorematic words, indicates which words in a language they are, and shows how they are placed and arranged; logic asks how they determine the truth of a statement and what follows from it.

## The Functions of Language (`i.functions`)

1. Not every sentence is used to say that something is or is not so.
2. The **functions of language**, that is, the different uses of speech, are to state, to ask, to command, to pray, and to call someone; and any of these sentences can also express what the speaker feels.
3. Each function is a use of a complete sentence; every such sentence signifies, but they do not all signify in the same way.
4. A **proposition** or **statement** (Greek *apophansis*, Latin *enuntiatio*) is a sentence that says that something is or is not so, and therefore is true or false.
5. A command or a prayer is a complete and meaningful sentence, but it is neither true nor false: every sentence signifies, but not every sentence is a statement (Aristotle’s distinction).
6. The kinds of complete sentence differ according to what the speaker does with what he understands.
7. In a statement the speaker simply expresses what he understands to be so, that is, a judgment.
8. In the other kinds he directs what he understands toward another person: to call him, to ask him, to command him, or to pray to him.
9. The traditional names of the kinds are the enunciative sentence (the statement), the vocative (which calls), the interrogative (which asks), the imperative (which commands), and the deprecative (which begs or prays).
10. Each language has its own means of showing which kind of sentence is meant: English asks a question by changing the order of the words or by adding a word such as “do”; Latin often adds the particle *-ne* to the first word.
11. For commands both English and Latin use a special form of the verb, the imperative mood; for wishes Latin often uses *utinam*, whereas English uses “may” or “if only.”
12. So the kinds of sentence are not only differences in the speaker’s purpose; they are also marked in the forms and the arrangement of the words, and that is why they belong to grammar.
13. Speech expresses feeling sometimes in a single word, called an interjection (“alas”), and sometimes in a whole sentence.
14. An expression of feeling can be joined to a statement: the statement is true or false, but the feeling expressed is neither.
15. Feeling is one function of speech among the others, and it can accompany any kind of sentence.
16. Only the statement is true or false, because only a sentence that says that something is or is not so can agree or disagree with the way things are.
17. A command asks that something be made so, a question asks whether something is so, and a prayer asks for something to be given or done; none of them claims anything about the way things are.
18. The answers show this: a statement is answered by agreement or disagreement (“That is true”), a question by a statement, a command by obedience or refusal, and a prayer by its being granted or not.
19. Grammar considers every kind of sentence, because every kind must be correctly formed; any kind can contain a solecism, that is, an error in construction.
20. Logic considers the statement, because it is concerned with truth and with what follows from what is true.
21. Rhetoric and poetry consider commands, questions, prayers, and expressions of feeling as well, because they are concerned with moving and delighting the hearer, although they also use statements.
22. So grammar is broader than logic in this respect: logic studies one kind of sentence, the statement, whereas grammar studies all of them.
23. Modern accounts of the functions of language agree with the tradition that speech does more than state, and that stating is the function on which truth depends.
24. Bühler’s three functions (expression, appeal, representation) correspond to the tradition: representation to the statement, and appeal to the command and the prayer.
25. Jakobson’s added functions also fit the classical account: keeping contact between speaker and hearer is the work of the vocative, and speaking about the language itself is done with names of the second imposition.
26. The poetic function is not a separate function in the classical tradition, whose ideal is to use the precise words that have the most beauty and the most power for what the poet intends; drawing attention to the words themselves is used only where the circumstances call for it.

## Ambiguity (`i.ambiguity`)

1. A word or a group of words that can be understood in more than one sense is **ambiguous** (Latin *ambiguus*, “going two ways”).
2. The grammarian’s first question about an ambiguous expression is a division: does the double sense lie in a single word, or in the way the words are arranged with one another?
3. Quintilian makes the same division: ambiguity has countless kinds but only two sources, a single word or a group of words.
4. Within these two sources ambiguity has four causes: first, one word imposed with more than one meaning; second, a construction in which a word can belong to more than one other word; third, letters that can be read as one word or as two; and fourth, writing that does not show how a word is pronounced.
5. Ambiguity in a single word arises when one sound has been imposed with more than one meaning; only the rest of the sentence, or the occasion, shows which is meant (“bank”).
6. Quintilian calls this kind homonymy, and Aristotle calls it **equivocation**.
7. Ambiguity in the construction (the arrangement of words into phrases and sentences) arises when every word has one clear meaning but it is doubtful which word another word belongs to (“old men and women”).
8. Aristotle calls this kind **amphiboly** (Greek *amphibolia*, “thrown both ways”), and Quintilian judges that it gives rise to more serious ambiguity.
9. Many ambiguities of construction are settled by the way the words are spoken: a pause groups together the words that belong together.
10. In writing, a mark of punctuation does the work of the pause.
11. Sometimes the same letters can be read as one word or as two (“nowhere” and “now here”); in speech the pauses and stress prevent this, and in writing the spaces between words.
12. Aristotle names these kinds of ambiguity **composition** and **division**, according to whether words are wrongly joined or wrongly separated.
13. Some written words are ambiguous only because the writing does not show how they are pronounced (the stress of an English word, the length of a Latin vowel); in speech they are not ambiguous.
14. Aristotle names this kind of ambiguity **accent**, because it depends on how a word is pronounced.
15. Once an ambiguity has been located, the grammarian shows how it can be removed: by changing the order of the words, by adding a word, or, in a language such as Latin, by changing an ending (a change of case).
16. Ambiguity is not always a defect: a writer may use a word in two senses on purpose, so that the hearer understands both at once; this is a pun or play on words.
17. With an intended ambiguity the grammarian’s task is the same, to locate the double sense and say what each sense is; what differs is the judgment.
18. An ambiguity that the writer did not intend is a defect of clarity, which the grammarian corrects; an ambiguity that the writer intended is a figure, which the grammarian explains.
19. Grammar locates the double sense and shows how it can be removed; logic asks what happens when an argument depends on the double sense.
20. Aristotle lists six ways in which arguments that seem to prove something depend on language: equivocation, amphiboly, composition, division, accent, and the form of expression (words of the same form taken to signify things of the same kind).
21. The study of such arguments belongs to logic.
