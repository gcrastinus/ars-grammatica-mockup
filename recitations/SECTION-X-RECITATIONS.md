## Composition (`x.composition`)

1. Analysis goes from the finished sentence to its parts, whereas composition (Latin *compositiō*, “putting together”) goes from the thought to the words, choosing the forms and constructions that will express it.
2. Making a sentence uses the same knowledge as parsing and construing one, in the other direction.
3. The principles and techniques of composition apply to every language, although this section applies them to English.
4. The classical tradition joined analysis and composition, so grammar, which begins with reading and explaining the authors, ends with writing.
5. Two short sentences about the same thing can often become one, so that one of them becomes a part of the other, for example an adjective.
6. Two sentences with the same predicate can share it in a compound subject, and two sentences with the same subject can share it in a compound predicate.
7. The combined sentence must keep the construction correct: two independent clauses need a conjunction or a semicolon, not a comma alone, and a compound subject joined by “and” takes a plural verb.
8. When two sentences are combined, one can be made dependent on the other, and the writer must decide which thought is principal.
9. The dependent sentence may become a temporal or causal clause, a relative clause, an appositive, or a participial phrase.
10. The conjunction chosen must state the true relation between the thoughts: “because” gives a reason, “although” a contrast, “when” and “after” a time, and “if” a condition.
11. So subordination concerns more than form, because a wrong conjunction misstates how the two thoughts are related.
12. English order carries the construction, so a misplaced or dangling modifier is an error of construction.
13. The remedy is to place each modifier next to the word it describes.
14. A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence describes the subject that follows it, so the subject must be the one who does the action of the participle.
15. A pronoun must refer clearly to one noun; if it could refer to two, the writer names the person again or changes the order.
16. Words and phrases joined by a coordinating conjunction should have the same construction, and this is called **parallel construction**.
17. The same rule applies to every series as to the members of a correlative pair.
18. Parallel construction is required by grammar, because the conjunction joins words of equal rank, and words of equal rank should be alike in construction.
19. It also makes the sentence clear, because the reader recognizes the series by the likeness of its members, and the figures of repetition, such as anaphora, deliberately strengthen the same principle.
20. The active and the passive describe the same event with different subjects, so in composition the choice of voice decides what the sentence is about.
21. The active is usually shorter and clearer, because the doer is the subject.
22. The passive is better when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious, or when the sentence should continue to speak about the thing acted upon.
23. So neither voice is always better: the writer chooses the voice that keeps the subject of the paragraph in the place of the grammatical subject, so that the sentences follow one another clearly.
24. English shows the giver, the receiver, and the thing given by the order of the words, whereas Latin shows them by the forms of the words.
25. So in Latin composition the student puts the subject in the nominative, an adjective in the gender, number, and case of its noun, the indirect object in the dative, the direct object in the accusative, and the verb in the person and number of its subject, usually last in prose.
26. Each choice applies a rule of agreement, of government, or of order, so Latin composition is a strict test of whether the rules have been understood, because nothing can be left to the order of the words.
27. One thought can be expressed by different constructions, each correct, with different subjects, voices, and figures, and the choice among them is a matter of judgment.
28. Erasmus’s *De copia* (“On Abundance”) taught students to express the same thought in many ways, by changing the words, the voice, the construction, and the figures.
29. So composition draws on the whole of grammar, and the student who can vary a sentence correctly has understood its construction.
30. Writing in haste produces words that do not say what was meant and sentences that do not fit together, so good writing needs a method that begins with care.
31. Quintilian’s method is to write slowly at first, choosing and weighing the thoughts and the words one by one, then considering their order, and to revise often.
32. The speed that comes with practice is the result of care in the beginning, not a substitute for it.
33. The order Quintilian describes, first the thoughts, then the words, then their order, is the order in which the grammar of composition proceeds.
34. A student learns to compose by reading good sentences closely, analyzing them, and then making sentences of the same kinds, because the best authors supply the words, the figures, and the methods of composition.
35. So grammar, which begins with the voice and the letter, ends with composition: the analysis of the authors shows what good sentences are, and composition makes that knowledge one’s own.
36. The tradition gave two further exercises for this purpose: imitation, in which the student copies and models his writing on a passage of a good author, and translation, in which he carries the sense of one language into another.

## Imitation (`x.imitation`)

1. Many arts are learned by copying a model, and writing well is learned in the same way, by imitating good writers.
2. Quintilian holds that imitation is a large part of art, since it is expedient to imitate whatever has been invented with success.
3. But imitation alone is not sufficient, because the aim is to write well oneself, not merely to repeat another.
4. Ascham made imitation the chief method of teaching Latin style: the student studied a passage of Cicero closely, translated it, and then wrote in the same manner.
5. So the student learns not only the words of a good author but the way in which they are put together.
6. Copying out a text was once the only way to possess it, and it was also a way of learning it.
7. Writing out a passage word for word requires attention to every word, every construction, and every mark of punctuation, and it fixes the movement of the sentences in the mind.
8. So copying brings the student into the closest contact with good sentences.

## Translation (`x.translation`)

1. Different peoples have imposed different words for the same things, so a speaker of one language cannot understand a text in another without learning that language or reading a translation.
2. **Translation** (Latin *trānslātiō*, “carrying across”) is the expression in one language of what has been said in another.
3. Translation has always been necessary for the transmission of learning, so the grammarian must understand what a translation can and cannot carry over, and why it can carry anything over at all.
4. Aristotle holds that speech sounds differ among peoples, but what they signify in the mind is the same for all, and so are the things themselves.
5. So what is said in one language can be said in another, because the translator carries over the concept, not the sound.
6. This answer does not claim that translation is easy or perfect, since languages differ in their words, constructions, and sounds; it claims that the thing understood is common, so a translation can be faithful to it.
7. A version that follows the original word for word often sounds strange, and a good translation must sometimes change the words in order to keep the sense.
8. Cicero translated not as an interpreter, who renders word for word, but as an orator, keeping the same thoughts and their figures in words suited to his own language, and keeping the character and force of the words.
9. Cicero chose this way because his purpose was to give Romans a model of Greek eloquence in Latin, and a word-for-word version would not have been eloquent Latin.
10. Jerome rendered sense for sense, following Cicero, except in the Holy Scriptures, where he held that even the order of the words may carry meaning, so he kept closer to the original.
11. So the method of translation depends on the kind of text and the purpose of the translation: the more the exact words matter, the closer the translation must keep to them.
12. A word-for-word version of a Latin sentence is often not English, or not natural English, because the two languages express the same relation by different constructions.
13. A sense-for-sense translation finds the English construction that expresses the same relation (“peace must be sought” for *pāx petenda est*).
14. The closer version still has a use, because it shows the construction of the original and so serves the student of the language.
15. A translation can be accurate in every word and still be dull where the original is eloquent.
16. Bruni holds that the translator must know both languages thoroughly, their idioms and figures as well as their words, and must preserve the style of the original as well as its sense.
17. Bruni judged word-for-word translations faithful to the words but false to the author, and he joined Cicero’s method to a demand for knowledge: the translator must understand the original exactly, or he will render a sense the author did not intend.
18. In Ascham’s double translation the student translates a passage of Cicero into English, later translates his English back into Latin, and compares his Latin with Cicero’s.
19. The exercise uses translation in both directions to teach composition, so it joins analysis, composition, and imitation in one exercise.
20. Some things cannot be carried over: the meter of a Latin poem, which depends on quantity, cannot be reproduced in English, which measures verse by stress.
21. Figures that depend on sound, such as paronomasia and alliteration, are usually lost, and synonyms differ in tone and association, which a translation may not match.
22. So every translation gives up something, and the translator must decide what matters most for the purpose; the grammarian, who knows both languages, can say what has been given up.
23. Sapir and Whorf held that the grammar of a language shapes the way its speakers perceive and think, and this view is called the **Sapir–Whorf hypothesis**.
24. If its strong form were true, translation between very different languages would be impossible, because the concepts themselves would differ.
25. Whorf’s account of the Hopi conception of time has been widely disputed, and most linguists now accept only a weak form: a language makes some distinctions easier or more habitual to express, without preventing its speakers from understanding distinctions it does not mark.
26. Quine argued that no evidence of behavior could settle which translation of a word in an unknown language is right, and concluded that translation is **indeterminate**, that is, there is no fact of the matter about which translation is correct.
27. Quine’s argument depends on his view that meaning is nothing beyond the observable behavior of speakers, so the objection concerns not the difficulty of translation but the existence of a common meaning to translate.
28. The classical account answers both objections from the doctrine of signification: words signify things by means of concepts, and the concepts are likenesses of the things.
29. So meaning is neither only a pattern of behavior nor a creation of the grammar of each language, and the concept of one thing is one, whatever words different peoples have imposed.
30. The answer admits that languages divide and mark things differently and that a translator can be uncertain what a word means.
31. Such uncertainty is overcome by attending to the things spoken about and to the use of the words in many sentences, so translation is difficult and always imperfect in some respects, but possible, because what is understood is common to all.
32. Translation draws on every part of grammar: the translator parses and construes the original, recognizes its figures and tropes, knows the signification of its words and the differences among synonyms, and composes in the other language.
33. So translation is both a test of understanding and an exercise in composition, and the tradition used it to teach both the foreign language and one’s own.

## Classical Composition Exercises (`x.progymnasmata`)

1. The classical tradition gave students a series of written exercises, growing harder, between the study of grammar and the study of rhetoric, called the **progymnasmata** (Greek, “preliminary exercises”).
2. They were preliminary to the practice of declamation in the schools of rhetoric, and the grammarians assigned them to students who had learned to read and write.
3. Quintilian assigns the first of them, the paraphrase of Aesop’s fables in simple language, to the Roman grammarian.
4. So the progymnasmata stand at the meeting of grammar and rhetoric: they begin with stories that children know and end with exercises in argument.
5. Several Greek handbooks survive, which vary in the number, names, and sequence of the exercises, and the handbook of Aphthonius became the standard one.
6. Aphthonius gives fourteen exercises in this order: fable, narrative, chreia, maxim, refutation, confirmation, common-place, encomion, invective, syncrisis, ethopoeia, ecphrasis, thesis, and introduction of a law.
7. A **fable** is a short story, usually about animals, that teaches a moral truth; Aphthonius calls it a fictive statement imaging truth, which began with the poets and is used by orators for the sake of the moral.
8. The student retells a fable in his own words, sometimes shortening it and sometimes adding to it, and states its moral, so the work is in the telling.
9. A **narrative** is a story told in order, whether true or invented, setting out who acted, what was done, when, where, how, and why.
10. The narrative is longer and more varied than the fable and has no moral attached; it teaches the student to order events in time, connect them by their causes, and keep the main action in view.
11. A **chreia** reports a memorable saying or action of a named person, and the student expands it in a set order: praise of the speaker, a paraphrase, the reason, a contrast, a comparison, an example, the testimony of others, and a conclusion.
12. The chreia is the first exercise in which the student argues for something, but it remains easy, because the argument is supported by a famous authority and the steps are fixed.
13. A **maxim** is a general saying, urging or dissuading something, and it is expanded in the same way as the chreia.
14. The maxim differs from the chreia in one point: the chreia is attributed to a person and may report an action, whereas the maxim is a general statement attributed to no one in particular.
15. In **refutation** the student argues that a story, usually from myth or poetry, is not credible, because it is unclear, impossible, inconsistent, or improper.
16. Refutation is harder than the chreia and the maxim, because it argues against a received view, and it prepares for the part of a speech that answers the other side.
17. **Confirmation** is the opposite of refutation: the student argues that a story is credible, clear, possible, consistent, and fitting.
18. The two exercises together train the student to examine a narrative as a judge would, so they connect the grammarian’s task of judging with the orator’s task of proving.
19. In the **common-place** the student writes against a type of wrongdoer, not a particular person, amplifying the wrong by showing how great it is, what harm it causes, and why it deserves punishment.
20. It is called “common” because its arguments apply to any person of that type, and it teaches the student to amplify, that is, to show the importance of a matter by its circumstances and consequences.
21. The **encomion** praises a person, a city, an animal, or a thing under set headings: origin, upbringing, deeds, and qualities, compared with others, and a conclusion.
22. As a preliminary exercise, the encomion teaches the student to arrange material under fixed headings and to describe virtues in fitting language.
23. **Invective** is the opposite of the encomion and follows the same headings, showing vices instead of virtues.
24. Invective differs from the common-place because it is directed against a particular person, not a type, and together the encomion and invective prepare for the kind of speech that praises or blames.
25. In **syncrisis** the student compares two persons or things, to show that they are equal or that one is better or worse, following the headings of the encomion for both side by side.
26. Syncrisis requires the student to hold two subjects in view and arrange his sentences so that the parallel is clear, so it draws on parallel construction and the figures of balance.
27. In **ethopoeia** (characterization) the student writes the words a known person might have said in a given situation, fitting the character, age, and circumstances of the speaker.
28. Ethopoeia teaches the student to write in a voice other than his own, adapting his words to a person and an occasion.
29. In **ecphrasis** (description) the student describes a person, place, time, event, or work of art so vividly that the reader seems to see it.
30. Ecphrasis teaches the student to choose details and order them so that the description can be followed, drawing on the vocabulary gathered from reading and on epithets and periphrases.
31. A **thesis** is a general question, not tied to particular persons or circumstances, on which the student argues for one answer and answers the objections.
32. The thesis is the first exercise that argues a general question without the support of a story or a famous saying, so it stands close to philosophy and prepares for the arguments of rhetoric.
33. The **introduction of a law** argues that a proposed law is or is not just, lawful, advantageous, and practicable, considering what it would require and what effects it would have.
34. It is the most difficult and the last of the exercises, because the student argues about a particular measure as a citizen would in an assembly, and it is the last step before declamation.
35. The handbooks give two reasons for placing the fable first: it is plain, simple, and related to the poems the students have been reading, and it is morally useful for forming the young.
36. Both reasons belong to the teaching of grammar, and the exercise was assigned by the grammarian.
37. The fable also belongs to the kind of speech that gives advice, and the general rule is to begin from the easier exercises, which is the fable.
38. So the fable comes first because it is the easiest exercise, because it continues the reading of the poets, and because it already contains in a simple form the advising and persuading that later exercises develop.
39. The exercises are arranged so that each is harder than the one before, on Theon’s principle that easier things should be learned before more difficult ones, and that it is easier to amplify what is clear than to demonstrate what is unclear.
40. So the order runs from retelling, through arguing for an accepted saying, to arguing for and against a story, to amplifying and praising, to writing in another’s voice and describing, and finally to arguing general and practical questions.
41. The later exercises are also longer, and Nicolaus explains the order as a movement from lesser to greater things, from the simpler, relatively non-argumentative exercises to those involving argument.
42. Nicolaus also places the common-place after refutation and confirmation because the exercises imitate the order of the parts of a speech, so the order follows both the difficulty of the exercises and the parts of a speech.
43. The handbooks do not all keep this order: Theon begins with the chreia, because it is short and easily remembered, and then the fable and the narrative.
44. Theon’s chapters were later rearranged to conform to the order of Aphthonius, and the Armenian manuscripts preserve his original order.
45. Nicolaus holds that the chreia may reasonably be placed after the fable and the narrative, because it requires more logical divisions.
46. The progymnasmata belong to grammar because they are exercises in composition, in which the student retells, expands, describes, and argues in correct and clear sentences, using the constructions, figures, and tropes that grammar has taught.
47. So they complete the course of grammar and open the way to rhetoric, beginning from the reading of the poets and ending at the threshold of the full speech.
