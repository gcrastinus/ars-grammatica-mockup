## The Definition of Grammar (`prelim.definition`)

1. Grammar, logic, and rhetoric are the arts of speech: grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly, logic the art of reasoning correctly; and rhetoric is ordered to persuasion and truth, especially since the true and the just are by nature more powerful than their opposites (cf. Aristotle, *Rhetoric* I.1).
2. Grammar comes first because logic considers statements, which are made of words, so the words must be correctly joined before anything can be asserted, denied, inferred, or communicated.
3. Rhetoric directs a speech to an audience, but there is nothing to direct until something has been said.
4. There are also historical reasons for the order: Quintilian sends the boy to the grammarian before the teacher of rhetoric, and calls grammar the foundation of the whole building.
5. The later Roman handbooks and the medieval scholars likewise begin the liberal arts with grammar, and so the medieval schools called grammar the gate of the arts, the first of the trivium.
6. The name “grammar” comes from the Greek word for a letter, *gramma* (plural *grammata*).
7. The art nevertheless begins with the voice, because a letter is a sound written down.
8. The art is named from writing because correct speech reaches its fullest and most lasting form in written works, and the advanced study of connected speech depends on written texts.
9. Grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly; Isidore calls it the knowledge of speaking correctly and the origin and foundation of liberal letters.
10. Quintilian divides the profession of the grammarian into two parts: the art of speaking correctly and the interpretation of the poets.
11. The word “correctly” is the center of the definition: grammar does not ask whether a sentence is true, which logic asks, or whether it persuades, which rhetoric asks, but whether the words are correctly formed and correctly arranged with one another.
12. Grammar is an **art**, that is, a habit of making something well, acquired by practice and directed by rules, and the work it makes is a correctly formed sentence.
13. The arts whose works are made in matters of reason are called **liberal** arts, to distinguish them from the arts whose products are made through bodily labor, because the soul is free (Latin *liber*).
14. Grammar also seeks to know why sentences are formed as they are and why the parts of speech are what they are; this part is called speculative grammar.
15. It is one art, not two, because the same art that makes correct sentences also asks for the reasons of what it makes.
16. The material of grammar is speech, that is, the voice as words signifying thoughts in the mind and arranged into phrases and sentences.
17. Grammar does not study things in themselves, which belong to the other sciences, or thoughts in themselves, which belong to the study of the mind.
18. Isidore says that speech is made up of three things: sense, voice, and letters.
19. A sound is considered in grammar only in so far as it belongs to speech, so a sound that signifies nothing, or signifies only by nature and not by a speaker’s intention, is not part of its material.
20. A groan shows pain by nature and so is not a word, but “Water!” is speech, because it signifies by the agreement of those who use it.
21. Speech is built in steps: the **voice**, the motion of air perceptible to hearing; the **letter**, the least part of the voice that can be written; the **syllable**, a sound pronounced in one utterance; the **word**, the least part of an expression that signifies; and the **sentence**, words arranged so that they express a complete thought.
22. Meaning begins at the word, not at the voice, the letter, or the syllable.
23. Truth and falsity begin only at the sentence, because only a sentence says something about something.
24. Grammar studies speech as far as the sentence, and a sentence is complete when something is named and something is said of it.
25. A **period** is a sentence of several clauses that together express one complete thought, and grammar considers it because it is still one sentence.
26. A speech or a book is many sentences ordered to prove or to persuade, and the order of those sentences belongs to logic and rhetoric, so the proper end of grammar is the sentence, including the period.
27. Grammar asks whether a sentence is correctly formed, whereas logic asks whether what it says is true or follows from what we know, and either may hold without the other.
28. So grammar cannot tell us what is true, but logic cannot begin without grammar, because until a sentence is correctly formed there is nothing definite for logic to judge.
29. The ends of grammar can be set out in order: first, a correctly formed sentence; second, the student’s habit of forming and judging such sentences; third, that the sentence express a complete thought in the mind of the hearer; and fourth, readiness for logic and rhetoric.
30. Thomas of Erfurt names the complete thought in the mind of the hearer as the remote end of a grammatical construction, the arrangement of words into a sentence.
31. Grammar is therefore a ministerial art, an art that serves other arts and sciences, and not an ornament of style for its own sake.
32. Grammar gives clarity about what words and sentences signify and serves the understanding of connected speech, so it culminates in the interpretation of the authors.
33. Boethius of Dacia calls grammar an introductory science: it is not a part of philosophy as natural philosophy and mathematics are, but it is needed in order to learn them.
34. Therefore grammar is studied before logic, and Quintilian holds that without the foundation laid by grammar the study of oratory will collapse.
35. The study of grammar begins with its smallest parts, the voice and the letter, and proceeds to the word and the sentence.

## The Tasks of the Grammarian (`prelim.offices`)

1. Quintilian divides the profession of the grammarian into two parts, the art of speaking correctly and the interpretation of the poets, but says that there is more in them than appears.
2. Beneath the two parts lie several distinct tasks: writing is joined to speaking, correct reading comes before interpretation, and in each case criticism, that is, judging, has its own work.
3. The two broad parts of the profession are the evaluation and correction of speaking and the evaluation and correction of writing.
4. The first task of the grammarian is to direct speaking and writing toward clarity and correctness, because writing and reading depend on the spoken language.
5. The evaluation and correction of writing is the higher part, because language reaches its most elaborate and powerful forms in writing, and so written speech requires the most thorough study and criticism.
6. This higher part studies literature, taken in a general sense to include everything thought to be especially well written.
7. Varro, as Diomedes reports, names four duties of grammar in this second sense: reading, explaining, correcting, and judging.
8. **Reading** (*lectio*) is reading a text aloud correctly.
9. **Explaining** (*enarratio*) is explaining the words, the construction, and the matter of a text.
10. **Correcting** (*emendatio*) is correcting what is corrupt in a text or incorrect in speech.
11. **Judging** (*iudicium*) is weighing the merit of a poem or other writing, and, Quintilian adds, whether it is genuine.
12. The list begins with reading and ends with judging because a text must be read before it can be explained, and understood before it can be judged.
13. Reading, the first task, is the care that a reader should have for the text that he reads aloud, and it belongs to the study of literature because a text that cannot be read fluently is not as easily understood.
14. Quintilian says that the reader must know where to take breath, where to pause in a line, where the sense ends or begins, and when to raise or lower the voice, and that to do all this one must understand what one reads.
15. So correct reading already requires grammatical knowledge: the quantity and accent of each word, and where each clause begins and ends.
16. Explaining, the second task, covers the parts of speech, the meter, the meaning of rare words, and the matter of the text, such as the stories to which the poets refer.
17. Correcting, the third task, has two objects: the text itself, in which the grammarian seeks to restore what the author wrote against the errors of copyists, printers, and editors; and speech.
18. An error in a single word is called a **barbarism**, and an error in the arrangement of words is called a **solecism**.
19. Judging is the fourth and highest task.
20. Judging includes three things: deciding whether a line is genuine, whether a work belongs to the author whose name it bears, and which authors are to be taken as models and why.
21. Dionysius Thrax divides grammar into six parts: reading aloud with attention to accents and quantities; explaining the figures of speech in the poets; explaining rare words and stories; finding the origin of words (etymology); working out the regular patterns of forms (analogy); and the criticism of poems, the finest part of the art.
22. The six parts and the four tasks describe the same work: in both, reading comes first, and criticism, that is, judging, comes last and highest.
23. Quintilian does not limit the grammarian to the poets, as Dionysius Thrax does, but holds that every kind of writer must be studied, for the vocabulary as well as the matter, because words acquire authority from their use by a particular author.
24. Literary authors belong to grammar for two reasons: interpreting them is one of the two parts of the grammarian’s profession, and they show how words are used correctly, so that they give authority to the forms and constructions that grammar teaches.

## Precepts and Interpretation (`prelim.methodice`)

1. Quintilian divides the profession of the grammarian into two parts, the art of speaking correctly and the interpretation of the authors, and says that the Greeks call the first *methodice* and the second *historice*.
2. The part of grammar that teaches the precepts of the art is the part concerned with precepts (*methodice*), and the part that interprets the authors is interpretation (*historice*).
3. The first part of grammar is the part concerned with precepts, that is, the general rules of the art.
4. Its Greek name, *methodice*, comes from *methodos*, an orderly way of proceeding, so this part teaches grammar as an ordered body of precepts.
5. A precept, in this sense, is a general rule or guiding principle of the art: it defines each thing that grammar treats, divides it into its kinds, and gives the rules that govern it.
6. For example, the precepts divide nouns into proper and common nouns, and give the rule that a Latin adjective agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case.
7. The precepts are general because they hold for every sentence of their kind, and so they can be learned once and applied to any text.
8. The second part of grammar is interpretation, that is, the interpretation of the authors.
9. Its Greek name, *historice*, comes from *historia*, inquiry or account; it does not mean the study of history in our sense but something like a survey of a whole topic.
10. Interpretation, the highest part of grammar, is the reading and explanation of the authors and their particular works: it explains the words, the constructions, and the matter of a particular text.
11. Whereas the precepts are general, the work of interpretation is particular, because it explains this line or this sentence in this text.
12. For that reason interpretation is learned by reading many authors with a teacher.
13. The two parts are not separate studies, because each depends on the other: the precepts are drawn from the usage of the authors and tested against them, and the authors are read and explained by means of the precepts.
14. A student who knows the precepts without reading the authors knows rules that he has never seen used, and a student who reads the authors without the precepts cannot say why a sentence is correctly formed.
15. Both parts are at work whenever one sentence is read with a teacher: the precepts name the forms and constructions, and interpretation explains what the sentence means in its place in the work.
16. No general precept can say what a phrase refers to in a particular passage, so that kind of explanation needs the reading of the particular text.
17. The division into precepts and interpretation continued after Quintilian, through late antiquity and the Middle Ages to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
18. The precepts were learned from short grammars written for that purpose, and the authors were read with commentaries that explained them line by line.
19. In the twentieth century grammar came to be thought an unnecessary imposition, and teachers were told to focus on reading comprehension and clarity of writing instead of teaching precepts.
20. But teachers still had to give students many rules, and because the rules were not presented as a system, grammar appeared to be an incoherent set of disconnected rules.
21. In fact grammar forms an ordered whole that considers in a systematic way each part of every utterance, from sound to sentence.

## The Criteria of Correctness (`prelim.criteria`)

1. The precepts of grammar are drawn from the usage of the authors and tested against them, but when a form or construction is doubtful, the grammarian needs a standard by which to judge it, and such a standard is a criterion.
2. Quintilian names four criteria of correctness: reason, antiquity, authority, and usage.
3. **Reason** (*ratio*) is the comparison of a doubtful form with a certain one (analogy), and sometimes the study of the origin of a word (etymology).
4. **Antiquity** (*vetustas*) is the standard of older speech.
5. **Authority** (*auctoritas*) is the standard of the best authors, in general and in the kind of text concerned.
6. **Usage** (*consuetudo*, also translated “custom”) is the standard of present speech, the agreed practice of educated speakers.
7. Each criterion answers the question “Is this correct?” in a different way, so the grammarian must know how to weigh them, and the evaluation of correctness requires wide knowledge and sound judgment, not a mechanical rule.
8. Reason finds its chief support in analogy and sometimes in etymology.
9. **Analogy** is the comparison of a doubtful form with a certain one of the same kind, the proof of the uncertain by reference to the certain (as the plural “zebras” is judged by “cats” and “dogs”).
10. **Etymology**, the study of the origin of words or phrases, does not always support reason, because the origin of a word does not always settle how it is used now.
11. The origin of a word has two meanings: the causes that brought about its formation and its use in past ages, and the parts of which it is made, since what the parts are can partly determine what the whole is.
12. Antiquity gives old words a certain majesty and charm, but such words must be used sparingly and must not draw attention to themselves.
13. So antiquity makes a word acceptable in some kinds of speech, such as the reading of Scripture and prayer, but it does not by itself make an old word the ordinary form.
14. Authority is drawn chiefly from orators and historians, and poets have less weight because they are allowed some license for the sake of the meter.
15. Authority has limits: a form used once by a good author does not become correct unless other speakers and writers have accepted it.
16. Quintilian calls usage the surest of the four criteria.
17. Usage does not mean whatever most people say, but the agreed practice of educated speakers and writers, so a form is not correct merely because it is common.
18. In our time the pursuit of a higher style, clear, precise, and powerful without pretension, is not common among educated writers, but there is no reason that it cannot be.
19. When the criteria disagree, the grammarian must judge between them.
20. When analogy and usage disagree, usage generally prevails, because a form may follow the rules and still not be the form that speakers use.
21. Antiquity and authority also yield to usage in ordinary speech, though they keep their place in some kinds of writing.
22. In prose and poetry of the highest kind, judgment takes account of the best writing without being subservient to the styles of the past, and this requires knowing what good style is and what grammar and rhetoric are for.
23. The ancient grammarians disputed whether language follows regular patterns (**analogy**) or admits irregular forms that no pattern explains (**anomaly**).
24. Varro sets out both sides and concludes that both must be admitted: there are regular patterns, and there are irregular forms that usage has fixed.
25. The grammarian therefore learns the regular patterns as precepts and the irregular forms one by one as exceptions.
26. Other criteria can sometimes explain why a form is irregular; etymology shows, for example, that “went” was originally the past tense of “wend” and came to serve as the past of “go.”
27. The criteria are used mostly to judge the more correct and the less correct, not errors in the strict sense, so the grammarian is not primarily a strict master of rules but a careful judge.
28. Carefulness becomes mere strictness if it is not guided by a sense of the purpose of the art.
29. The criteria are also the standard by which errors are judged: an error in a single word is a **barbarism**, and an error in the arrangement of words is a **solecism**.
30. A barbarism or a solecism is a departure from the forms that reason, antiquity, authority, and usage approve.
