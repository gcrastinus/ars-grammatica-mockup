## Congruity (`v.congruity`)

1. The arrangement of words in the sentence is called **construction** (Latin *cōnstrūctiō*), or **syntax** (Greek *syntaxis*, “arrangement”).
2. The word “construction” has two standard senses: the arrangement of words in general, and a particular group of words arranged together.
3. The sentence is the first thing that can be correctly or incorrectly formed as a whole, and, once it is complete, the first thing that can be true or false.
4. A group of words can fail to be a sentence in two ways: its words may fit together but leave the sense incomplete, or something may be said but the words may not fit one another.
5. So a sentence requires a fitting arrangement of words and a complete sense.
6. Priscian defines the sentence by these two conditions: a sentence is a fitting arrangement of words, showing a complete sense.
7. Priscian reports that the dialecticians counted only two parts of the sentence, the noun and the verb, because these alone, joined to each other, make a complete sentence.
8. A construction whose words fit one another is called **congruous**, and its fit is called **congruity** (*congruitās*).
9. An adjective signifies something that belongs to another, so it does not fit unless there is a word for it to belong to; a word used substantively can, however, supply that word itself.
10. The modists name Priscian’s two conditions congruity and **completeness** (*perfectiō*), and they explain congruity by the modes of signifying.
11. Thomas of Erfurt explains that an adjective signifies by the mode of adhering to another, so it requires a word that signifies by the mode of standing by itself, and when the two are joined the modes fit.
12. So congruity depends on how the words signify in the sentence, not merely on what part of speech they usually are.
13. A construction is **complete** when it produces a complete sense in the mind of the hearer, so that the hearer has been told something and waits for nothing more.
14. A construction can be congruous but incomplete (“the old bridge”), or congruous and complete, as a sentence is.
15. Thomas of Erfurt says that completeness requires a subject and a verb joined together, because a noun alone or a verb alone does not yet say that anything is or is not so.
16. A construction can have a subject and a verb and still be incomplete, because a word such as “if” leaves the sense unfinished; so a subordinate clause can be well formed and still not be complete by itself.
17. Of any group of words we may make one of three judgments: incongruous (the parts do not fit with one another, so the construction fails); congruous but incomplete (the parts fit, but the sense is not yet complete); and congruous and complete.
18. Incongruity is a failure of fit and incompleteness is a failure to finish, so they are two different ways in which a group of words can fall short of a sentence.
19. An incongruous construction is a solecism, an error in the arrangement of words, whereas an incomplete construction is not an error at all if it is used as a part of a larger sentence.
20. Every sentence raises two questions, whether it is well formed and whether what it says is true; grammar answers the first, and logic the second.
21. A congruous and complete sentence can be false, and a word that signifies by the mode of a stable thing can be a subject whether or not anything answers to it (“The chimaera runs”).
22. So a sentence can be congruous and true, congruous and false, or incongruous, and an incongruous sentence is not yet fit to be true or false.
23. Congruity is the condition of truth or falsity, but it does not decide which.
24. Every complete sentence has two principal parts: what the sentence is about, and what is said of it.
25. The grammarians of the twelfth century called these the *suppositum* (“what is placed under”) and the *appositum* (“what is placed beside”); modern grammars call them the **subject** and the **predicate**.
26. The subject is usually a noun or pronoun in the nominative case with the words that depend on it, and the predicate is the verb with the words that depend on it: its object, its adverbs, and its phrases.
27. In Latin the subject may be contained in the verb itself and shown only by the ending.
28. Anything that is made can be explained by four causes, which Aristotle names the material, the formal, the efficient, and the final cause.
29. Thomas of Erfurt explains construction by the same four causes.
30. The **material cause** of a construction is the words that are arranged, and the **formal cause** is their union into one construction.
31. The **efficient cause** is, within the words, their modes of signifying, which fit one word to another, and, outside the words, the mind of the speaker who arranges them.
32. The **final cause** is to express the speaker’s thought, and so to produce a complete sense in the mind of the hearer.
33. Priscian gave the definition of the sentence, and Thomas gives its causes; this is one point at which the modists added something that the Roman grammarians had not worked out.
34. Logic also speaks of subject and predicate, but in a related and different sense: in logic they are the terms of a statement considered as true or false, joined by the copula “is.”
35. In grammar the subject of “Every man is mortal” is “every man” and the predicate is “is mortal,” and grammar asks only whether they are correctly arranged and complete.
36. So a false sentence is as good a sentence as a true one if its subject and predicate are correctly joined, and only logic asks which is true.
37. The words of a sentence are joined by the relations of agreement, government, and dependence; the order of words and the larger units of the phrase, the clause, and the period are further subjects of construction.
38. Chomsky proposed that a grammar of a language should be a set of rules that generates all the grammatical sentences of the language and none of the ungrammatical ones; such a grammar is called **generative**.
39. Chomsky argued that a speaker’s knowledge of grammar is independent of meaning, since a sentence can be grammatical though it makes no sense.
40. The classical grammarians likewise distinguished congruity from truth, so that a sentence can be congruous though false.
41. The modists would add that such a sentence is congruous because its words fit one another by their modes of signifying, one adhering as a quality and the other bearing it, whatever the words mean, so for them grammar is never wholly independent of signification.

## Agreement (*concordantia*) (`v.agreement`)

1. The correspondence of two words in the accidents that they share is called **agreement** (Latin *concordantia*), and it is one of the chief ways in which the words of a congruous construction fit one another.
2. Grammarians traditionally name three agreements, the three concords.
3. An adjective (or participle) agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case.
4. A verb agrees with its subject in person and number.
5. A relative pronoun agrees with its antecedent in gender and number.
6. A Latin adjective must agree with its noun in all three accidents, gender, number, and case, and an error in any of them makes the construction incongruous.
7. English adjectives do not change their form, so English shows no agreement of adjective and noun, except in the demonstratives (“this book,” “these books”).
8. So “these kind of books” is an error of agreement in English, because a plural demonstrative is joined to a singular noun.
9. The adjective names a quality as belonging to the thing that the noun names, so it is reasonable that, in a language that marks gender, number, and case, the adjective should carry the same marks as its noun.
10. The adjective agrees with its noun in gender, number, and case, not in the sound of the ending, so a masculine noun with feminine-looking endings still takes a masculine adjective.
11. In the same way the verb signifies its action as belonging to a subject, so it takes the person and number of that subject.
12. The modists give this reason: Thomas of Erfurt says that the adjective signifies by the mode of adhering to another, so it requires in that other the mode of standing by itself, and the accidents that the two share must conform.
13. So agreement is not a mere custom of Latin; it follows from the way the dependent word signifies, since what adheres to another must correspond to it.
14. Languages differ in how much of this correspondence they show by endings, but the relation that the endings show is the same.
15. A finite verb, that is, a verb that has person and number, agrees with its subject in both.
16. Latin shows the agreement of verb and subject in every form; English shows it chiefly in the third person singular of the present and in the verb “to be.”
17. A predicate noun or adjective after “to be” agrees with the subject as well.
18. In English the predicate pronoun takes the subjective case in formal usage (“It is I”), although ordinary speech commonly says “It’s me.”
19. A relative pronoun takes the gender and number of its antecedent but takes its case from its own clause.
20. So the relative pronoun stands in two constructions at once: by its gender and number it agrees with a word in the main clause, and by its case it is part of the construction of its own clause.
21. English shows the same in “who” and “whom,” whose case is decided in the relative clause, not by the case of the antecedent.
22. A phrase that stands between the subject and the verb does not change the subject.
23. Two subjects joined by “and” are plural.
24. With “or,” “nor,” “either … or,” and “neither … nor,” the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
25. In sentences beginning “there is” and “there are,” the verb agrees with the real subject, which follows it.
26. “Each,” “every,” “either,” and “neither” used as subjects are singular.
27. In each of these cases the rule follows from finding the word that is really the subject, and then making the verb agree with it.
28. Sometimes a word agrees not with the form of another word but with its meaning, as when a collective noun takes a plural verb because its members are thought of separately.
29. The grammarians call this agreement according to the sense (Greek *synesis*, “understanding”; Latin *cōnstrūctiō ad sēnsum*).
30. Such constructions are not solecisms when they are established in good usage, because agreement is a rule that serves the meaning: where form and meaning part, good authors may follow the meaning.
31. The rules of agreement describe what correct speech does, but the grammarian must also read authors whose usage differs from a modern handbook.
32. When an author is quoted, the quotation is the text, and the grammarian’s task is to explain what the text does, not to rewrite it.
33. The criteria of antiquity and authority weigh the usage of the best authors, and they decide against a rule applied without regard to the history of the language.

## Government (*regimen*) (`v.government`)

1. **Government** (Latin *regimen*, “ruling”) is the relation in which one word requires another word to be in a particular case that the first word does not itself have.
2. Government is not agreement, in which two words share an accident.
3. A verb that requires its object in the accusative is said to **govern** the accusative.
4. The grammarians of the twelfth century, especially Peter Helias, made government one of the principal ideas of syntax: a word governs another when it requires that word in a certain form in order to complete the construction.
5. The preposition, which governs a particular case in its noun, is the clearest case of government.
6. Most Latin transitive verbs govern the accusative of the **direct object**, the person or thing on which the action falls.
7. A verb that takes a direct object is called transitive, because its action passes over to the object.
8. English shows the same government by the position of the object after the verb, and in pronouns by the objective case.
9. Some Latin verbs govern two accusatives, one of the person and one of the thing, as English “teach” takes two objects.
10. Some Latin verbs govern a case other than the accusative, and they must be learned with their case.
11. Many verbs of favoring, helping, obeying, trusting, and their opposites govern the **dative**.
12. Some verbs of remembering and forgetting govern the **genitive**.
13. Five deponent verbs and their compounds govern the **ablative**: *ūtor*, *fruor*, *fungor*, *potior*, and *vescor*.
14. So the case that a verb governs is a fact of each language, and a word-for-word translation will often choose the wrong case.
15. Each Latin preposition governs a particular case: some the accusative, some the ablative, and *in* and *sub* either case, motion toward a place with the accusative and rest in a place with the ablative.
16. The preposition has no case of its own and exists only to relate its noun to another word, so its government shows most clearly what government means.
17. The case that a preposition governs is part of what it signifies.
18. Nouns and adjectives, like verbs, can require another word to complete their meaning, and so they also govern cases.
19. A noun governs the genitive of another noun that completes or limits its meaning.
20. Some adjectives govern the dative, especially adjectives of likeness, nearness, and fitness; others govern the genitive, especially adjectives of desire, knowledge, and fullness.
21. English expresses most of these relations with prepositions, which are themselves governed by the noun or adjective, so in English government has moved from the endings of the noun to the choice of the preposition.
22. English shows government less plainly than Latin, because its nouns no longer show case, but government remains in two places.
23. First, many English verbs, nouns, and adjectives require a particular preposition, and to change the preposition is an error.
24. Second, a preposition or verb governs the objective case of a pronoun, so “for you and I” is a failure of government.
25. So where English has lost its endings, government still exists, but its marks are fewer, and a speaker must know the rule even where the form does not show it.
26. In every case of government there is a **governing** word and a **governed** word.
27. The governing word is the one whose meaning requires completion, and the governed word supplies it in the required form.
28. A sentence usually contains several relations of government at once, and to analyze a sentence is in part to find each governing word and what it governs.
29. Agreement and government are the two principal relations of form between words.
30. In agreement the two words share the same accident; in government one word requires in another an accident that it does not have itself.
31. Both relations rest on a deeper one, the relation of dependence, since in each pair one word depends on the other.

## Dependence (`v.dependence`)

1. A word **depends** on another when its mode of signifying requires something that the other supplies, and the word that supplies it **terminates** the dependence, that is, completes what the dependent word requires.
2. Thomas of Erfurt gives this as the reason why one word is constructed with another at all: every construction has two parts, one word that depends (*dependēns*) and one word that terminates the dependence (*termināns*).
3. So dependence is the relation that explains why agreement and government occur: the dependent word takes the form required by the word on which it depends.
4. Thomas gives the principal kinds of dependence.
5. The adjective depends on the noun.
6. The adverb depends on the verb.
7. The verb depends on its subject.
8. The verb also depends on its object.
9. Thomas reasons that the verb signifies something going on, which must be the action of something and, if it passes over, an action upon something; so the verb requires a subject and, if it is transitive, an object, and in this sense it depends on both.
10. Because each construction joins two words, a sentence of more than two words contains more than one construction, and the article depends on its noun as an adjective does.
11. So the analysis of a sentence finds every dependence, one pair at a time.
12. A sentence is congruous when every one of its constructions is congruous, and it is complete when the dependences together produce a complete sense, which requires at least the construction of a subject and a verb.
13. Priscian sorts constructions according to whether the action passes from one person to another, and from him later grammarians took the names **transitive** and **intransitive** construction.
14. Thomas of Erfurt uses these names more broadly: a construction is intransitive when its two words refer to the same thing, and transitive when the dependent word passes over to something else.
15. So Thomas’s transitive construction includes the noun with a genitive as well as the verb with its object, which shows that these two relations are of the same kind.
16. On another view the verb is the center of the sentence, and the other words depend on it, since each answers a question that the verb raises.
17. The French linguist Lucien Tesnière developed a grammar on this view, which describes the sentence as a set of words depending on words, drawn as a tree (*stemma*) with the governing word above and its dependents below.
18. For the adjective and the adverb, Tesnière’s account agrees with Thomas’s.
19. For the verb the two accounts differ: Tesnière makes the verb the head of the whole sentence, with the subject and the object depending on it, whereas Thomas makes the verb depend on the subject and on the object.
20. Both agree that the verb and its subject and object are joined by dependence and disagree about which way it runs; Thomas reasons from the verb’s mode of signifying, Tesnière from its place at the center of the sentence.
21. Verbs differ in how many words they require, from none to three.
22. Tesnière called the number of words that a verb requires to complete it its **valency**, by comparison with the number of other atoms with which an atom can combine.
23. The idea of valency agrees with the older doctrine of government, so the number of words that a verb requires, and the cases in which it requires them, are both part of its meaning.
24. A sentence can be analyzed by marking each dependence with an arrow drawn from the word that depends to the word that terminates its dependence; in this course the arrows follow Thomas of Erfurt.
25. Marking makes visible what the endings of Latin show and the order of English implies, and in both languages the dependences are the same.
26. A preposition and its noun make a phrase, and the whole phrase depends on another word, as an adjective or an adverb would.
27. Within the phrase, the noun depends on the preposition, which governs its case.
28. So dependence works at more than one level: single words depend on single words, and groups of words depend on words as wholes, which is why the phrase and the clause are studied as units of construction.
29. Dependence is the relation that every construction has, and agreement and government are the forms in which it is shown.
30. When the dependent word shares accidents with the terminant there is agreement; when one word requires a case in another there is government.
31. These relations do not depend on the order of the words.
32. Tesnière’s **translation**, in his technical sense, is the process by which a word or phrase of one category is made to function as another (“of” makes the noun in “the book of Peter” function as an adjective).
33. Dependency grammar is widely used, especially in the computer analysis of language, because it describes the relations among words directly and works well for languages with free word order.
34. The project called Universal Dependencies has annotated sentences in more than a hundred languages with the same set of dependency relations, so that their constructions can be compared.
35. In these modern systems, as in Tesnière, the verb is usually the root of the sentence, and the subject and objects depend on it, whereas Thomas of Erfurt makes the verb depend on its subject and object.
36. So the direction of a dependence is a question of theory: the modern systems follow the verb’s central place in the sentence, and Thomas follows the verb’s mode of signifying, which requires a subject.
37. Thomas and Tesnière agree on most dependences, the adjective on its noun, the adverb on its verb, the article on its noun, and the object in a relation to its verb, and they agree that every construction joins two words.
38. They differ chiefly on the relation of the verb to its subject, and on the reason for the dependences.
39. For Thomas, a word depends on another because its mode of signifying requires what the other supplies, so the reason lies in signification; for Tesnière, the reason lies in the structure of the sentence itself.
40. So modern dependency grammar describes the same relations that the modists described, but it does not ask the modists’ question why the relations hold.
41. The minimalist program seeks to reduce universal grammar to as few operations as possible.
42. Its central operation is **Merge**, which takes two elements and combines them into one, and repeated Merge builds every structure of every language.
43. This recalls Thomas of Erfurt’s doctrine that every construction joins two words, one depending and one terminating, so the modern and the medieval accounts arrive, by different routes, at the binary construction as the basic unit of syntax.

## Order (*ordo*) (`v.order`)

1. The relations of dependence, agreement, and government do not depend on the order of the words, but every sentence must be spoken in some order, and the order has its own effects.
2. The grammarians call the arrangement of words in sequence their **order** (Latin *ordō*).
3. Languages differ in how much the order of words does: in English the order is the chief means of showing construction, so it is largely fixed.
4. In Latin the endings show the construction, so the order is free to do other work, especially to give emphasis and to arrange the sentence for effect.
5. Some orders of words seem to follow the nature of things or of thought, and the grammarians call such an order a **natural order** (*ōrdō nātūrālis*).
6. Quintilian uses the term for the order in which things are customarily named.
7. Priscian uses it for the order of the parts of the sentence, holding that the nominative is naturally placed before the verb, because the substance is prior to the action that is said of it.
8. The medieval grammarians held that the natural order of a sentence is the subject first, then the verb, then what depends on the verb, because this is the order in which the mind conceives the thought.
9. Any other order they called **artificial** (*ōrdō artificiālis*), that is, arranged by art for some effect.
10. Latin authors follow no fixed rule of order, and Quintilian rejects rules of order that are applied without regard to the effect.
11. Quintilian gives one general preference: if the demands of artistic structure permit, it is best to end the sentence with a verb, because the strength of language lies in verbs.
12. So the usual Latin order places the subject first and the verb last, with the object and other words between, but this is a usual order, not a rule of correctness.
13. When a verb does not end the sentence, Quintilian counts the order as a figure, an ornament rather than an error.
14. The first and last places in a sentence are the most emphatic.
15. In Latin a word moved from its usual place to the beginning or the end of the sentence is thereby emphasized.
16. English uses the same positions for emphasis within the limits of its fixed order, but more often uses other means, because moving the object before the verb would usually change the construction.
17. Because Latin endings show which words belong together, Latin can separate words that are constructed together.
18. The separation of words that belong together is called **hyperbaton** (Greek, “stepping over”), and Quintilian counts it among the figures.
19. In reading Latin the reader must join the words by their endings, not by their positions, so the endings and the relations of agreement and government are the only sure guide to the construction.
20. English order is fixed because it carries the construction; the usual English order is subject, verb, object, and questions invert the subject and an auxiliary verb.
21. Because the order carries the construction, a word placed in the wrong position can attach itself to the wrong word, so a wrong order leads to ambiguity.
22. So in English correct order is part of correct construction, not only a matter of style.
23. For many centuries grammarians taught students to read Latin by **construing** it, that is, by putting its words into the natural order: the subject with its adjectives, then the verb, then the object and the other words that depend on the verb.
24. Construing does not change the meaning of the sentence; it makes the dependences visible by placing each word beside the word on which it depends.
25. So construing shows that the natural order is the order of the dependences, whereas the artificial order of the author arranges them for emphasis, sound, and effect.
26. Modern linguists, following Joseph Greenberg, class languages by the usual order of subject (S), verb (V), and object (O) in a statement.
27. The three common orders are SVO, as in English; SOV, as in the usual order of Latin; and VSO, as in Classical Arabic.
28. English has a fixed SVO order; Latin has a usual SOV order, which its endings free it to change.
29. Greenberg found that the order of subject, verb, and object tends to go together with other facts of order.
30. Languages in which the verb comes before the object usually have prepositions, and languages in which the verb comes after the object usually have **postpositions**, placed after the noun.
31. Greenberg stated such regularities as universals, for example that languages whose usual order is VSO always have prepositions.
32. Latin is mixed, with a usual SOV order but prepositions and a few postpositions in fixed phrases, so the facts of order come in bundles, but the bundles are tendencies, not rules without exception.
33. In a German main clause the finite verb stands in the second place, whatever comes first, but in a subordinate clause it goes to the end, so German order distinguishes main and subordinate clauses.
34. French usually has SVO order, but a pronoun object stands before the verb, so French order depends on the part of speech of the object.
35. In each language the order of words is a part of its grammar, and it must be learned as the endings of Latin must be learned.
36. In English order shows the construction, so a change of order is a change of meaning; in Latin the endings show the construction, so order can give emphasis, separate words for effect, and follow the rhythm of the sentence.
37. In both languages the first and last positions are emphatic, and in both there is a natural order that follows the dependences of the words.
38. So the order of words is a part of construction, but not the whole of it, and the natural order is the order in which the relations of construction are easiest to see.
39. Linguists of the Prague School observed that a sentence usually begins with what is already known or under discussion, called the **theme**, and continues with what is new, called the **rheme**.
40. So the choice of voice and order serves to place the known before the new.
41. The Arabic distinction of topic and comment and the Latin placing of emphatic words first or last describe the same arrangement of a sentence for the hearer, which explains why the same thought is often expressed by different constructions in different contexts.
42. Many of Greenberg’s universals are **implicational**: they state that if a language has one property, it has another (languages with the order verb, subject, object always have prepositions).
43. Such universals do not say that every language has a given property; they say how properties are related.
44. They differ from the universals of the general grammarians, who sought properties present in every language, and typology has shown that implicational universals are far more common than absolute ones.
45. Comrie distinguishes universals that hold without exception from those that hold for most languages.
46. An **absolute universal** holds for every known language; few have been found, and they are often very general (every language has vowels).
47. A **statistical universal** holds for most languages (the tendency of languages with the order subject, object, verb to have postpositions).
48. A universal can be tested only on a sample of languages chosen from many families and regions, because languages that are related or neighbors share properties by inheritance or by contact.
49. So the modern search for universals is a careful comparison of many languages, and its results are often tendencies rather than laws, which is a caution for any universal grammar, classical or modern.
50. Latin marks the subject of every verb in the nominative and the object in the accusative.
51. An **ergative** language marks the subject of a transitive verb with a special ending but treats the subject of an intransitive verb like the object of a transitive one.
52. The ergative pattern shows that the grouping of subject against object is one possibility among others.
53. The relations of doer, thing done, and the one who undergoes are present in every language, but languages group them differently in their endings, so the facts of construction are common, whereas their marking varies.

## The Phrase (`v.phrase`)

1. A **phrase** is a group of words that is used as a single part of speech and does not contain a subject and a finite verb of its own.
2. A **finite verb** is a verb that has person and number and can make a statement with its subject; the infinitive and the participle are not finite, so a group built on them can still be a phrase.
3. A group of words with its own subject and finite verb is a clause, not a phrase, so the phrase is the unit of construction between the word and the clause.
4. A phrase can be named in two ways: by its **form**, that is, by what it is built from, and by its **function**, that is, by how it is used in the sentence.
5. By form, a phrase may be prepositional, participial, gerund, infinitive, or appositive; by function, it may be used as a noun, as an adjective, or as an adverb.
6. The two names answer different questions, so one phrase built in the same way may be used as an adjective in one sentence and as an adverb in another.
7. This follows the principle used with the parts of speech: a word, or a group of words, is named by how it is used in the sentence.
8. A **prepositional phrase** begins with a preposition and ends with its object, with any words that depend on the object.
9. It is usually used as an adjective, telling which one or what kind, or as an adverb, telling when, where, how, or why.
10. Latin prepositional phrases are used in the same ways, but Latin also expresses many such relations by a case alone, without a preposition.
11. A **participial phrase** is built on a participle and is used as an adjective, because the participle depends on a noun.
12. A **gerund phrase** is built on the form in *-ing* used as a noun.
13. The participle and the gerund have the same form in English, so their use decides which they are, and the same is true of the phrases.
14. An **infinitive phrase** is an infinitive with “to” and the words that depend on it, and it may be used as a noun, as an adjective, or as an adverb.
15. Latin uses the infinitive chiefly as a noun or to complete another verb; to express purpose Latin uses other constructions, such as a clause with *ut* or a phrase with the gerund.
16. A noun or noun phrase placed beside another noun to identify or describe it, and referring to the same thing, is called an **appositive** (from Latin *appositus*, “placed beside”).
17. In Latin an appositive agrees with its noun in case, so apposition is a kind of agreement, in which two nouns that refer to the same thing take the same case.
18. English sometimes sets a noun and a participle apart from the rest of the sentence to tell the circumstances of the action; this is the **nominative absolute**, and English usually prefers a clause.
19. Latin uses such a phrase much more often, in the ablative case: the **ablative absolute**, a noun and a participle (or another noun or adjective) in the ablative, telling the time, cause, or circumstances of the action.
20. It is called absolute (from *absolūtus*, “loosed”) because it is loosed from the construction of the main clause.
21. The dependences of a sentence can be shown in a diagram, in which each word or phrase is placed under the word on which it depends; Reed and Kellogg taught the analysis of sentences in this way.
22. In their diagrams the subject and the verb stand on a horizontal base (the main line), divided by a vertical line, and the words and phrases that depend on them are drawn on slanting lines below.
23. A prepositional phrase is drawn below the word it depends on, with the preposition on a slanting line and its object on a horizontal line beneath, so the diagram makes the dependence of the phrase visible.

## The Clause (`v.clause`)

1. A **clause** is a group of words that has its own subject and finite verb.
2. Clauses that can stand as a sentence by themselves are called **independent** (or main) clauses; clauses that cannot, because they depend on another clause, are called **subordinate** clauses.
3. A subordinate clause is congruous but incomplete; it depends on a word in another clause and is used as a single part of speech: a noun, an adjective, or an adverb.
4. Every clause has a subject and a predicate.
5. The **simple subject** is the noun or pronoun itself, and the **complete subject** is that word with all the words that depend on it.
6. The **simple predicate** is the finite verb, and the **complete predicate** is the verb with all the words that depend on it.
7. The predicate of an English clause takes one of a few patterns, according to what the verb requires.
8. The patterns are these: subject and verb; subject, verb, and direct object; subject, verb, indirect object, and direct object; subject, linking verb, and predicate noun; subject, linking verb, and predicate adjective; and subject, verb, direct object, and objective complement.
9. A **linking verb**, such as “be,” “seem,” or “become,” joins the subject to a word that renames or describes it, rather than carrying an action to an object.
10. These patterns correspond to the valency of the verb, since each verb requires a certain number of words to complete it, and the patterns show what those words are.
11. A subordinate clause used as a noun, that is, as the subject, the object, or the complement of a verb, is called a **noun clause** (older grammars call it a substantive clause).
12. A noun clause after a verb of saying, thinking, or knowing reports what was said or thought, and this report is called **indirect speech** (*ōrātiō oblīqua*).
13. English uses a clause with “that” for indirect speech, whereas Latin usually uses the accusative and the infinitive.
14. The report of a question is an **indirect question**, and in Latin its verb is in the subjunctive.
15. An **adjective clause**, also called a **relative clause**, depends on a noun and tells which one or what kind; it usually begins with a relative pronoun, “who,” “which,” or “that.”
16. The relative clause joins two constructions, because the relative belongs by its gender and number to the main clause and by its case to the subordinate clause.
17. An **adverb clause** depends on a verb, an adjective, or another clause and tells when, where, why, how, under what condition, or in spite of what.
18. The principal kinds of adverb clause are named by what they tell: **temporal** (when), **causal** (why), **conditional** (under what condition), **concessive** (in spite of what), **purpose** (the end aimed at), and **result** (what followed).
19. A condition grants a case, and the main clause holds only in that case; a concession grants the case and holds that the main clause is true all the same.
20. English marks the kinds of subordinate clause chiefly by the conjunction or relative word that begins them; Latin marks many of them by the mood of the verb as well.
21. In Latin a clause of purpose and a clause of result both begin with *ut* and take the subjunctive, but a purpose clause is negated by *nē* and a result clause by *ut nōn*, and a result clause is usually prepared by a word of degree in the main clause.
22. So the question to ask of a clause with *ut* is whether it states what was aimed at or what followed.
23. The subjunctive marks what is not stated as a fact but as aimed at, supposed, or dependent on another.
24. Sentences are classed by the clauses they contain.
25. A **simple** sentence has one independent clause and no subordinate clause, however long it is.
26. A **compound** sentence has two or more independent clauses.
27. A **complex** sentence has one independent clause and at least one subordinate clause.
28. A **compound-complex** sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one subordinate clause.
29. The joining of clauses of equal rank, side by side, is called **parataxis** (Greek, “placing beside”); the joining of a subordinate clause to a main clause is called **hypotaxis** (Greek, “placing under”).
30. Latin prose, especially Cicero’s, favors hypotaxis, and its long sentences of many clauses are studied as the period.
31. In a Reed and Kellogg diagram a subordinate clause is drawn on a base (main line) of its own below the main line and joined by a dotted line to the word on which it depends.
32. An adjective clause is joined to its noun, an adverb clause to the word it modifies with the conjunction on the dotted line, and a noun clause used as an object is set on a support (a stilt) in the place of the object.
33. The analysis of a sentence finds the independent clause and its pattern, then each subordinate clause and the word on which it depends, then the phrases and single words that depend within each clause.
34. A sentence with only one independent clause is complex, not compound, however many subordinate clauses it has.
35. Bloomfield sought to describe languages by strict procedures based on the observable forms of speech.
36. Because meaning could not be observed in the same way, he and his followers set it aside as far as possible and described each language by the distribution of its sounds and forms.
37. Bloomfield analyzed sentences into their immediate constituents, dividing a sentence first into two parts and then each part into its parts.
38. This analysis resembles the classical division of the sentence into subject and predicate, but it was made without appeal to meaning, whereas the classical analysis rests on signification throughout.
39. A generative grammar divides the sentence into its constituents by rules called **phrase-structure rules**, the first of which may state that a sentence consists of a noun phrase and a verb phrase (S → NP VP).
40. Further rules divide each phrase, and the result can be drawn as a tree, with the sentence at the top and the words at the bottom.
41. The phrase-structure tree groups words into phrases, whereas the dependency diagram of Thomas and Tesnière joins each word to the word on which it depends; the two describe the same sentence in different ways, and modern linguists continue to debate which is more fundamental.
42. The classical tradition would grant that a sentence can be divided into phrases, but it would explain the division by the dependences, since for Thomas of Erfurt every construction joins a dependent and a terminant.
43. Chomsky proposed rules called **transformations**, which relate one structure to another and derive the one form from the other (the passive from the active).
44. Questions and negations were also derived by transformations from simpler structures.
45. The classical grammarians had observed the same relations without this apparatus, and Chomsky’s transformations gave such observations a precise and general form.
46. Chomsky distinguished the **deep structure** of a sentence, which represents its relations of meaning, from its **surface structure**, the form in which it is spoken.
47. Two sentences can have the same surface form but different deep structures (“John is easy to please” and “John is eager to please”), and the classical analysis by dependences and sense distinguishes them in the same way.

## The Period (`v.period`)

1. The **period** (from the Greek for “a going round”) is a sentence of several members that comes round to completion.
2. Aristotle defines the period as a sentence that has a beginning and end in itself and a magnitude that can be easily grasped.
3. So a period is not merely a long sentence: its parts are arranged so that the sense is held in suspense until the end and then completed, and its length is such that the hearer can take it in as a whole.
4. The period is the largest unit of construction that grammar studies, because it is still one sentence.
5. Aristotle distinguishes two kinds of style: in the continuous style clauses are added one after another and the style stops only when the sense is complete; in the periodic style each sentence is shaped as a whole, with a beginning and an end.
6. Aristotle gives the reason the periodic style pleases: at every moment the hearer thinks that he is securing something and that a conclusion has been reached, and the period is easily retained in the memory.
7. So the period is a construction made for the mind of the hearer; its effect on the hearer belongs to rhetoric, and grammar studies how it is built.
8. A period is made of parts called **members**, which the voice divides at its pauses.
9. The longer member is called a **colon** (Latin *membrum*) and the shorter a **comma** (Latin *incīsum*); a colon is usually a clause, and a comma a phrase or a short clause.
10. Quintilian names the three parts of the closely constructed style the comma, the colon, and the period.
11. Aristotle says that a period may be simple, of one member, or composed of members, and that the members should be neither too short nor too long.
12. Demetrius adds that the largest periods have four members, and that a longer one would no longer keep the proportion of a period.
13. So the period is measured by its members, as a line of verse is measured by its feet, but the members must also end where the sense ends.
14. Aristotle insists that the period must be completed with the sense and not stop short, because a pause where the words seem complete leads the hearer to take the first part as complete.
15. So the period joins the two conditions of a good construction: every member must be congruous, and the whole must be complete, but the completeness is deliberately withheld until the last member.
16. This is why a Latin period often places its main verb last, since the verb completes the construction and the sense at the same moment.
17. The first half of a period, which the rhetoricians call the **protasis** (Greek, “stretching forward”), may contain several dependent clauses, such as conditions.
18. The second half, the **apodosis** (Greek, “giving back”), is the main clause.
19. The sense is held in suspense through the protasis, because the hearer does not know what follows until the main clause, whose verb may stand last.
20. So the analysis of a period applies all the doctrine of construction: the clauses and their dependences, agreement and government within each, and the order that holds the main verb to the end.
21. English also has periods, in which the main verb is held back until the protasis is complete, although English order does not allow the main verb to stand last.
22. Modern grammars call a sentence whose main clause is completed only at or near the end a **periodic sentence**, and a sentence whose main clause comes first, with further clauses and phrases added after it, a **loose sentence**.
23. The loose sentence corresponds to Aristotle’s continuous style, and the periodic sentence to his period.
24. English, with its fixed order of subject and verb, uses loose sentences more naturally than Latin does, but both kinds are correct, and good writers use both.
25. The period belongs both to grammar and to rhetoric: grammar studies how its members are constructed, and rhetoric studies what it does to the hearer, how its rhythm pleases, its suspense holds attention, and its completion persuades.
26. Quintilian treats the period under composition (*compositiō*), the art of arranging words.

## Mood and Voice in Construction (`v.mood`)

1. Mood and voice are accidents of the verb, but they are also relations of construction, because they decide how the verb is joined to other words and how one clause is joined to another.
2. The voice of a verb decides which word is its subject and how the doer of the action is expressed.
3. The mood of a verb often shows whether its clause is independent or subordinate, and what kind of subordinate clause it is.
4. Priscian devotes the last book of his grammar to the construction of the verb with its cases and moods, and medieval grammarians learned from it that the verb is the center around which the construction of the sentence is arranged.
5. In a passive sentence the one who receives the action becomes the subject, and the doer may be named or left out.
6. When the doer is a person, Latin expresses it by *ā* or *ab* with the ablative, the **ablative of agent**; when the means is a thing, Latin uses the ablative without a preposition, the **ablative of means**.
7. So Latin distinguishes in its construction what English leaves to the sense: whether the cause of the action is a person who acts or a thing that is used.
8. Latin can use an intransitive verb in the passive, in the third person singular, without a personal subject, to say that an action took place without naming who did it; this is the **impersonal passive**.
9. English cannot form a passive from an intransitive verb, so it uses other expressions instead.
10. Verbs that govern the dative also form the passive impersonally, so the voice of the verb decides what kind of subject, if any, the sentence can have.
11. The infinitive names an action without person or number, and in construction it is used in three chief ways.
12. It is used as the **subject** of a verb, as a noun.
13. It is used as the **object** of a verb.
14. It is used to **complete** a verb that requires another verb to finish its sense, and this is called the **complementary infinitive**.
15. English uses the infinitive in the same three ways and also to express purpose, which classical Latin prose does not ordinarily do with the infinitive.
16. After a verb of saying, thinking, knowing, or perceiving, Latin usually puts the subject of what is reported in the accusative and its verb in the infinitive; this construction, the **accusative and infinitive**, is the regular form of indirect statement.
17. English uses a clause with “that” instead, though it has a similar construction after some verbs.
18. The tense of the infinitive shows time relative to the main verb: the present infinitive the same time, the perfect infinitive an earlier time, and the future infinitive a later time.
19. Latin uses a participle where English often uses a subordinate clause or a second verb.
20. The participle shows time relative to the main verb, and it can take the place of a temporal, causal, conditional, or concessive clause, the sense deciding which.
21. So a single Latin participle often contains a relation between two actions that English must express with a conjunction, and the reader must supply the conjunction from the sense.
22. Where English uses a gerund with an object, Latin usually uses the gerundive agreeing with the noun, a passive participle signifying what is to be done.
23. With *ad* or with *causā* (“for the sake of”), the gerundive construction expresses purpose.
24. The gerundive with a form of *sum* expresses necessity; this is the **passive periphrastic** construction, and the person who must do the action is put in the dative, the **dative of agent**.
25. Latin uses the subjunctive in some independent sentences, which do not state a fact, each kind with its own sense.
26. The **hortatory** subjunctive urges, in the first person plural.
27. The **jussive** subjunctive commands, in the third person.
28. The **optative** subjunctive wishes or prays, often with *utinam*.
29. The **deliberative** subjunctive asks what one should do.
30. The **potential** subjunctive says what may or would happen.
31. English expresses all of these with auxiliary verbs, and the independent subjunctive is one of the chief means by which Latin marks sentences that command, ask, and pray as well as state.
32. The verbs of clauses with *cum*, of clauses of purpose and result, and of indirect questions are in the subjunctive.
33. In such clauses the tense of the subjunctive depends on the tense of the main verb, according to the **sequence of tenses**.
34. After a present or future main verb the subordinate clause has the present (or perfect) subjunctive; after a past main verb it has the imperfect (or pluperfect) subjunctive.
35. So the tense of the dependent verb depends on the main verb, as its mood depends on the kind of clause; English shows a similar sequence in “may” and “might.”
36. English expresses most of these constructions without special forms of the verb: auxiliary verbs or the infinitive where Latin has the subjunctive, a clause with “that” where Latin has the accusative and infinitive, and a clause with a conjunction where Latin has a participle or an ablative absolute.
37. So a translation between the two languages often changes the construction but keeps the meaning, and what remains the same is the relation of dependence that the construction expresses.
38. Several modern theories describe the relations of the participants in an action as **semantic roles**, such as the agent, the instrument, the experiencer, the location, the goal, and the recipient.
39. These roles are distinguished from the grammatical subject and object, since the agent can be the subject or not (an active and a passive sentence).
40. The semantic roles closely resemble Pāṇini’s kārakas, roles in the action distinct from the endings that mark them, and the uses of the Latin cases, such as the ablative of means and the ablative of agent.
41. So modern linguistics has rediscovered, in its own terms, a distinction that the Indian grammarians made central more than two thousand years earlier.
42. **Functional** accounts of grammar explain the forms of a language by the uses to which language is put: to represent things, to address others, and to build a connected text.
43. **Formal** accounts, such as Chomsky’s, describe the forms of a language apart from their uses.
44. The tradition distinguished the functions of language, and Bühler and Jakobson classified them; functional grammar goes further and seeks to explain the grammatical forms themselves, such as the order of words or the choice of voice, by the functions they serve.
45. Formal and functional accounts are often presented as rivals, but they answer different questions: a formal account asks what the structures of a language are and what rules generate them, and a functional account asks why the structures are as they are, by what they do.
46. The classical art combined something of both, describing the forms of words and constructions precisely and explaining them by their signification and their use.
47. The modists’ explanation of the parts of speech by their modes of signifying is in this sense a functional explanation, though its functions are those of signifying rather than of communicating.
48. Halliday held that every clause serves three functions at once, which he called metafunctions.
49. The **ideational** function represents things and events: who did what to whom.
50. The **interpersonal** function establishes a relation between speaker and hearer: whether the clause states, asks, or commands, and with what attitude.
51. The **textual** function joins the clause to the text around it: what is theme and what is new.
52. Bühler’s representation corresponds to the ideational function and his appeal to the hearer to the interpersonal, whereas the textual function adds what the classical rhetoricians treated under the arrangement of words.
53. So Halliday’s account brings together, within the grammar of the clause, what the tradition divided among grammar, logic, and rhetoric.

## Figures of Construction (`v.figures`)

1. Good authors sometimes depart from the regular construction of the sentence, and their departures are not errors.
2. The grammarians call such a departure, made for a reason, a **figure** (Latin *figūra*, Greek *schēma*), and a figure that concerns the construction of the sentence is called a **figure of construction**.
3. Donatus defines the solecism as an error in the construction of the parts of speech, made against the rule of the art of grammar, and adds that in prose it is called a solecism and in a poem a figure.
4. So the same arrangement of words may be an error or a figure, according to whether it is made by ignorance or for a reason that the authors accept.
5. **Ellipsis** (Greek, “a falling short”; the medieval grammarians wrote *eclipsis*) is the omission of a word that is needed for the construction but is easily understood from the context.
6. Latin often omits the verb “to be,” and Donatus names this figure *eclipsis*, the lack of a word that the sentence requires.
7. Ellipsis is the most common of the figures of construction, and ordinary speech uses it constantly.
8. Ellipsis does not make a sentence incomplete, because the construction is complete in the mind of the hearer, who supplies the omitted word from the context.
9. **Zeugma** (Greek, “a yoking”) is the figure in which one word, usually a verb, serves for several clauses or phrases, though it is expressed only once.
10. Donatus defines zeugma as one word closing several clauses that are fitly joined.
11. Later grammarians also use the name for a harder kind of yoking, in which one word is joined to two others but fits only one of them strictly, and English handbooks often use it for a word that applies in different senses to two others.
12. In every kind of zeugma one word does the work of several, but it need not agree with each of them.
13. **Syllepsis** (Greek, “a taking together”) is the figure in which one word is construed with several others that differ in gender, number, or person, and agrees with only one of them.
14. English agreement with the nearer subject is a rule, but the grammarians described it as a figure, because one form serves for words that would each require a different form.
15. Donatus does not have a separate class of figures of construction; he counts zeugma and syllepsis among the figures of words (*schēmata lexeōs*), with figures of repetition and arrangement.
16. Later grammarians, especially in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, gathered the figures that concern construction into a group of their own.
17. So the same figures can be studied as departures from the rules of construction and as figures of words; the division is one of viewpoint, not of things.
18. Agreement by the sense, in which a collective noun takes a plural verb, is named by later grammarians the figure **synthesis**.
19. Another figure puts one case in place of another, as when a noun is attracted into the case of a relative; the grammarians call this **antiptosis** (Greek, “a case falling for another case”).
20. Such figures show that authors sometimes let the construction follow the sense or the nearness of another word rather than the strict rule.
21. The grammarian’s task is to recognize the figure, to explain the regular construction from which it departs, and to show the reason for the departure.
22. English sometimes inverts its fixed order, and Latin departs from the usual order much more freely; the separation of words that belong together is hyperbaton.
23. **Anastrophe** (Greek, “turning back”) is a narrower figure of order, in which a preposition is placed after its noun.
24. **Hysteron proteron** (Greek, “the later earlier”) is the figure in which what happens later is said first.
25. Donatus counts anastrophe and hysteron proteron among the kinds of hyperbaton.
26. In each figure of order the regular order can be restored, and the figure is understood by comparison with it.
27. **Hendiadys** (Greek, “one through two”) expresses one idea by two nouns joined by “and,” instead of a noun and an adjective or a noun and a dependent noun.
28. Hendiadys changes the construction by making two words equal that are related as a word and its dependent, so the figure must be recognized from the sense, since the grammar alone presents two coordinate nouns.
29. The grammarians judged whether a departure is a figure or a solecism by the criteria of correct speech.
30. A departure is a figure when it has a reason that the hearer can grasp, such as brevity, emphasis, the meter, or agreement with the sense, and when it is supported by the authority and usage of good authors.
31. A departure is a solecism when it comes from ignorance of the rule and serves no purpose.
32. So the use of a figure requires knowing the rule from which it departs, and the reader who meets one must recognize both the rule and the reason; this is why the figures are studied after the regular construction, and why Donatus places them after the errors of speech.
