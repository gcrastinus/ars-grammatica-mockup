## XI.1 The Ancient Near East (`xi.neareast`)

1. Other peoples besides the Greeks and Romans made the words of a classical language the object of a school study, and some did so long before Donatus.
2. Six such traditions are considered: the scribal lists of Babylon, the scribal school of Egypt, the Sanskrit grammar of India, the Arabic grammar of the Islamic world, the Hebrew grammar of the medieval Jewish scholars, and the Chinese study of the written character.
3. We study them with the terms of our own art, but the aim is not to find Donatus’s eight parts in every tradition; it is to see where the other traditions asked the same questions as ours, where they asked different ones, and what each found.
4. Each tradition is first described by its own name for the art and its own first questions, and only then compared with the art we have studied.
5. When a language is no longer anyone’s mother tongue but its books are still read, it must be taught in school, as Latin was in the West.
6. The scribes of Mesopotamia, who spoke Akkadian, kept Sumerian in this way as a language of learning, some two thousand years before Donatus.
7. They wrote no treatise called grammar, but lists and paradigms on clay tablets, with a Sumerian form in the left column and its Akkadian equivalent in the right.
8. The simplest line of such a list is a pair of a Sumerian word and its Akkadian equivalent, so the art begins with this pairing, not with a definition of the noun.
9. So the scribal art of words is a bilingual art: its unit is the match between two languages, not the analysis of one.
10. The scribes’ first concern was the written sign itself.
11. Our art begins with the letter, the least part of articulate voice that can be written; the scribal art begins with the sign, a cuneiform character that may write a whole word, a syllable, or several different words.
12. One sign may have several powers, and the powers are different words.
13. The scribes compiled sign lists, which give each sign with its readings and often an Akkadian equivalent, and lists of signs written together for one reading.
14. So the scribal “letter” is not a sound without meaning, as ours is, but a written sign that often already has a meaning.
15. The sign lists record for each sign the same three things as the name, figure, and power of a letter, but the power of a sign is often a word.
16. Beside the sign lists, the scribes copied great thematic lists in which Sumerian words are grouped by subject, each entry a pair of a Sumerian word and its Akkadian equivalent.
17. These lists are the scribes’ account of the word: they do not discuss imposition or define the noun, but the list itself fixes what each word means, by its Akkadian equivalent and its place among the things of its kind.
18. So the thematic list is closer to a dictionary arranged by subject than to a grammar, but it was the foundation of the scribe’s knowledge of the language.
19. The most ambitious tablets are paradigms of the Sumerian verb, in which one verb is run through hundreds of forms, each paired with an Akkadian form.
20. The paradigms often give the persons in the order third, first, second, not our first, second, third.
21. Later texts isolate the pieces of a Sumerian form, such as a prefix or a suffix, and give each an Akkadian explanation, which is the nearest the scribes come to our accidents of the word.
22. But they do not name a genitive or a tense; they say that this Sumerian piece is the equivalent of that Akkadian relation.
23. So the scribal grammar is a grammar of correspondences, and its categories are the forms of the other language.
24. The scribal tablets never say how many parts of speech there are.
25. Their questions were how a sign is written and read, what the Akkadian is for a Sumerian word, what form a verb takes for each person and relation, and what a piece of a form signifies; these are real grammatical questions, though not Donatus’s.
26. So the scribal art differs from ours in three ways: it is bilingual, explaining one language by another; it begins with the written sign, not with the voice; and it arranges forms in lists and paradigms instead of defining classes and rules.
27. Yet it shows the same need that produced our art: a classical language had to be kept and understood in a school, and that required a systematic study of its words.
28. Egyptian was written for more than three thousand years, and Egyptologists divide it into five stages: Old Egyptian, Middle Egyptian, Late Egyptian, Demotic, and Coptic.
29. Middle Egyptian, spoken from about 2100 BC for some five centuries, was the classical stage, and it remained the language of monuments and of the school long after it ceased to be spoken.
30. So scribes who spoke a later Egyptian learned Middle Egyptian from its classic texts, as the Babylonian scribes learned Sumerian and as the West learned Latin.
31. No Egyptian treatise on grammar survives, and the scribes learned their art by copying model texts, such as the school book called the Kemit.
32. They also copied onomastica, lists of words arranged by subject, such as the Onomasticon of Amenope, which are like the Babylonian thematic lists but written in one language.
33. In Plato, the Egyptian god Theuth invents writing and first divides the sounds of speech into vowels, sounds with some voice, and mutes, but that is a Greek story, not an Egyptian grammar.
34. The grammar of Egyptian that we study was made by modern scholars, beginning with Champollion and set in order by Gardiner, and it describes Egyptian with the terms of our own art.
35. Hieroglyphic writing mixes logograms, which write whole words, often with a stroke, and phonograms, which write one, two, or three consonants.
36. The vowels are not written, so we do not know exactly how most words were pronounced.
37. Phonetic complements repeat part of the sound of a longer sign and help the reader choose its reading.
38. So the Egyptian sign, like the cuneiform sign, has a figure and a power, and its power may be a word or only a sound.
39. A determinative is a sign at the end of a word that is not read but shows the kind of thing the word means, such as the seated man for men, the papyrus roll for abstract ideas, and the walking legs for motion.
40. Since vowels were not written, the determinative told apart words with the same consonants and marked the end of the word.
41. Later writers in Greek, such as Horapollo, thought that all hieroglyphs were symbols of ideas, but most signs write sounds, and only the determinatives come near to signifying things directly.
42. Egyptian nouns are masculine or feminine, and the feminine usually ends in -t.
43. They have three numbers, singular, dual, and plural, so that Egyptian, like Greek, keeps a dual.
44. Adjectives follow their noun and agree with it in gender and number, as Latin adjectives do.
45. The suffix pronouns are short pronouns joined to the end of a word, such as .i “I, my,” .k “you, your,” .f “he, his,” and .s “she, her.”
46. Joined to a noun a suffix shows the possessor, as in pr.f “his house”; joined to a verb it is the subject, as in sdjm.f “he hears.”
47. So one set of forms does the work that Latin divides between the possessive adjectives and the personal endings of the verb.
48. Egyptian has no case endings, so it shows the genitive by placing the nouns.
49. In the direct genitive the possessor follows the thing possessed, as in nb tawy “lord of the Two Lands”; in the indirect genitive the nouns are joined by a word for “of” that agrees with the first noun.
50. Latin shows the same relation by the ending of the genitive case.
51. Many Egyptian sentences have no verb, and they are divided by their predicate into adverbial, nominal, and adjectival sentences.
52. iw.f m pr “he is in the house” is adverbial, ntjr pw “he is a god” is nominal, and nfr sw “he is good” is adjectival.
53. Latin can leave out the verb “to be,” but our art supplies it when it parses the sentence, whereas Egyptian grammar does not need to.
54. Egyptologists use sdjm, “hear,” as the model verb, as Donatus used legō.
55. The commonest forms take the subject as a suffix pronoun: sdjm.f “he hears,” and sdjm.n.f “he has heard,” in which .n marks the action as completed.
56. Because the vowels are not written, Egyptologists still dispute how far these forms express time and how far they express other things, so their names describe their shape, not their meaning.
57. The stative, which Gardiner called the old perfective, is a form of the verb with its own endings that describes a state, often one that results from an action.
58. It does part of the work of the Latin perfect participle with esse, as in scrīptum est, “it is written.”
59. In the Middle Egyptian sentence with a verb, the verb comes first, then the subject, then the object.
60. Particles at the front of the sentence, such as iw and mk, “behold,” set the sentence in its place in the story or the speech.
61. Egyptian, which has few endings, shows its construction by order, as English does, and in Late Egyptian and Coptic the subject came more often first.
62. Demotic is both the most cursive Egyptian script, which grew out of hieratic about 650 BC, and the stage of the language written in it, between Late Egyptian and Coptic.
63. The Rosetta Stone carries a decree of 196 BC written in hieroglyphs, in Demotic, and in Greek, and the Greek made it the key to the other two.
64. Coptic, the last stage of Egyptian, is written in the Greek alphabet with six or seven letters taken from Demotic.
65. Champollion, who knew Coptic, showed in 1822 and 1824 that hieroglyphs write sounds as well as ideas.
66. The Babylonian and Egyptian scribes both kept a classical language in a school after speech had moved on, as the West kept Latin.
67. Both began with the written sign and taught by copying lists and model texts, and neither left a treatise on the parts of speech.
68. So our account of their languages uses the terms of our own art, which fit well enough to teach them but are ours.
69. What they share with our art is the need that made it: a classical language had to be kept and understood.

## XI.2 Sanskrit Grammar (`xi.sanskrit`)

1. A text that must be recited word for word for many centuries requires a careful study of its words.
2. The Indian name for that study is vyākaraṇa, “analysis” or “separation,” that is, the separating of speech into its parts.
3. It was counted among the six auxiliary sciences of the Veda, because the exact wording of the Veda had to be preserved and understood, and that required the ability to take a word apart and put it together again.
4. The central work of the tradition is Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyāyī (“The Eight Chapters”), composed perhaps in the fourth century BC; before it stands Yāska’s Nirukta, and after it come Patañjali’s Great Commentary and Bhartṛhari’s Vākyapadīya.
5. So the Indian tradition is older than the Greek grammar of Dionysius Thrax and developed independently of it, and its central work has a form unlike any Greek or Latin grammar.
6. Donatus teaches grammar by questions and answers, defining each part of speech and naming its accidents, but Pāṇini’s grammar does not explain.
7. It consists of nearly four thousand rules called sūtras (“threads”), each stated in as few syllables as possible, with technical abbreviations of Pāṇini’s own invention.
8. The rules are ordered so that a later rule can rely on an earlier one, and together they generate the correct forms of Sanskrit from roots and affixes.
9. So the Indian grammar is learned by learning the rules in their order, with the commentaries that explain them; Patañjali’s Great Commentary discusses why each rule is needed and what would go wrong without it.
10. This is a different form of art from Donatus’s: it aims at a complete and economical set of rules from which every correct form can be derived.
11. The Indian tradition gave close attention to the sounds of speech, which it studied in a separate science (śikṣā, “instruction”) as well as in grammar.
12. The sounds were classified by the place in the mouth where they are made and by the manner of making them, and the Devanagari alphabet is arranged in that order, the vowels first and then the consonants by their place, from the throat to the lips.
13. Pāṇini’s grammar begins with fourteen short lists of the sounds, arranged so that any group of sounds can be named by its first sound and a marker, so the order of the alphabet is itself part of the grammar.
14. The Indian classification of sounds is more exact than the Latin division into vowels, semivowels, and mutes, because it describes how each sound is made.
15. When two sounds meet in Sanskrit, at the end of one word and the beginning of the next or within a word, they often change; this change is called sandhi (“putting together”), and its rules are part of the grammar.
16. A change in a single word is a barbarism in Latin prose and a metaplasm in verse, but in Sanskrit the changes of sandhi are required in correct speech, and omitting them would be an error.
17. English has a few such changes (“an apple” but “a pear”), and Latin elision in verse is a kind of sandhi, but no other classical tradition made the joining of sounds so large a part of the grammar.
18. The Indian tradition divided words into kinds and gave a reason for its first division.
19. Yāska divides words into four kinds: the name (nāman), the verb (ākhyāta), the preverb (upasarga), which is placed before a verb and changes its sense, and the particle (nipāta).
20. Yāska says that the verb has becoming as its principal meaning, and that names have a being, a thing, as theirs.
21. This account comes strikingly close to the modists’ distinction between the mode of becoming and the mode of a stable thing, although the two traditions had no contact.
22. But Yāska counts four kinds, not eight: the adjective belongs with the name, and there is no participle or interjection as a separate kind.
23. Sanskrit, like Latin, has stems that cannot stand in a sentence until they receive an ending.
24. Pāṇini defines a word in use (pada) by its ending: a word is that which ends in a nominal or a verbal ending, so a stem without its ending is not yet a word of the sentence.
25. Priscian defines the word as the least part of a constructed sentence; both relate the word to the sentence, but Pāṇini makes the ending the mark of a word, so that a stem belongs to a level below the word.
26. The difference reflects the languages: in Sanskrit, as in Latin, nouns and verbs almost never appear without an ending, whereas in English many words do.
27. The Indian grammar derives almost every form from a root (dhātu) and one or more affixes (pratyaya); the roots are listed in a separate catalogue with their meanings.
28. A verb of the sentence is made by adding affixes to the root, so the Indian grammarian does not parse a finished form, as we do when we name its accidents, but builds the form from its root.
29. Pāṇini made derivation the method of the whole grammar, so that nouns, verbs, and even many particles are derived from roots by rules.
30. The center of Pāṇini’s syntax is the doctrine of the kārakas (“factors” of an action); a kāraka is a role that a thing plays in the action signified by the verb.
31. Pāṇini names six: the agent (kartṛ), the independent doer; the object (karman), what the agent most wishes to reach; the instrument (karaṇa), the most effective means; the recipient (sampradāna), the one to whom something is given; the source (apādāna), the fixed point from which something departs; and the locus (adhikaraṇa), the place or time in which the action occurs.
32. The kārakas are relations in the action, not names of endings: the endings of the noun mark them, but not one for one, since the same role may be marked by different endings in different constructions, as the agent is in an active and a passive sentence.
33. So the kārakas resemble the uses of the Latin cases, but they belong to the action and are prior to the endings that express them.
34. In a simple Sanskrit sentence the agent is marked by the first ending, which corresponds to our nominative, and the object by the second, which corresponds to our accusative.
35. Because the endings mark the roles, Sanskrit, like Latin, can change the order of the words without changing the sense.
36. The Indian explanation of this freedom is the marking of the kārakas by endings; ours is the marking of construction by cases; the two describe the same fact from different starting points, the Indian from the action and its factors, ours from the noun and its accidents.
37. The Indian grammar counts seven sets of nominal endings, called vibhakti (“division”), with the vocative as a use of the first; they correspond roughly to the Latin cases with two more, the instrumental and the locative.
38. The endings also mark relations that are not kārakas, such as possession, so the endings and the kārakas form two systems, and a Latin table of cases imposed on Sanskrit would misrepresent both.
39. Sanskrit also forms compounds (samāsa) very freely, expressing within the compound a relation that Latin expresses by the genitive, so that much of its construction takes place within single words.
40. Patañjali opens his Great Commentary by asking what grammar is for, and he gives five chief purposes: the preservation of the Veda; the adapting of its formulas to new occasions; the command of scripture; brevity, since rules teach more quickly than lists of words; and the removal of doubt about the correct form.
41. These purposes resemble those of the Greek and Latin art, to speak correctly and to explain the authors, but Patañjali’s first purpose is the preservation of a sacred text.
42. His fourth purpose, brevity, explains the form of Pāṇini’s grammar: a short set of rules that generates every correct form is easier to learn than a list of all the forms.
43. Bhartṛhari argued that the true unit of meaning is the sentence (vākya), and that its meaning is grasped as a whole, in a single act of understanding, not by adding the meanings of the words one after another.
44. He called the meaning-bearing unit that the hearer grasps the sphoṭa, distinct from the sequence of sounds that reveal it.
45. So the Indian tradition, which built its grammar from roots and affixes, ended in a philosophy that put the sentence first, sharing with the modists the conviction that understanding a sentence is more than adding its words.
46. The Sanskrit grammar shares much with the Greek and Latin art: both serve a classical language and its texts; both distinguish noun and verb and connect the verb with becoming; and both describe the endings that mark the construction of the noun.
47. But the Sanskrit art differs in method and center: it is a system of rules that generates forms from roots, not a set of definitions; its syntax is centered on the kārakas, not on the cases; and it gives the sounds and their combinations a much larger place.
48. Unlike the scribal lists, the Sanskrit grammar explains Sanskrit from itself, and in the nineteenth century it influenced the beginnings of modern linguistics.

## XI.3 Arabic Grammar (`xi.arabic`)

1. The Arabic name for the description of correct Arabic is naḥw, “way” or “direction,” that is, the way in which speech goes when it is correct Arabic.
2. Arabic grammar arose in the eighth century in Iraq, when the spread of Islam brought many speakers of other languages into the Arabic-speaking world, and errors in reading the Qurʾān and in ordinary speech made it necessary to describe and teach correct Arabic.
3. The founding work is the Kitāb (“The Book”) of Sībawayh, which described the Arabic of the Qurʾān, of the old poets, and of the Bedouin, whose speech was regarded as the standard.
4. So, like the Sanskrit grammar, the Arabic grammar served a sacred text, but its standard was also the living speech of those who spoke Arabic best.
5. Sībawayh counts three parts of speech: the noun (ism), the verb (fiʿl), and the particle (ḥarf), which comes for a meaning that is neither noun nor verb.
6. The Arabic noun includes our noun, adjective, and pronoun, and the participle; the particle includes our prepositions, conjunctions, and many words that we class as adverbs or interjections.
7. So the Arabic division resembles the logicians’ division into noun, verb, and the rest, but it is a grammarian’s division, made for the construction of Arabic, and it has remained the standard of the Arabic tradition.
8. The word ḥarf means both a particle and a letter of the alphabet.
9. The Arabic alphabet writes the consonants and the long vowels; the short vowels are marked by small signs, which are usually omitted in ordinary writing.
10. Most Arabic words are formed from a root of three consonants, which carries a general meaning, and a pattern of vowels and added letters, which gives the particular form.
11. This system resembles the Sanskrit derivation from roots, but the Arabic root is a set of consonants into which vowels are inserted, not a syllable to which affixes are added.
12. The Arabic noun changes its final vowel according to its use in the sentence, and this change is called iʿrāb (“making Arabic,” that is, giving a word its correct ending).
13. There are three states: rafʿ, with the ending -u, for the subject and the predicate; naṣb, with the ending -a, for the object; and jarr, with the ending -i, after prepositions and for the second noun of a possessive construction.
14. Words that do not change their endings are called mabnī (“built,” fixed), and the distinction between words with iʿrāb and fixed words corresponds to our distinction between declinable and indeclinable words.
15. The three states resemble the Latin nominative, accusative, and genitive, but there are only three, and their uses are defined by the Arabic theory of government.
16. The Arabic grammarians explained each ending by something that governs it, an ʿāmil (“operator,” “governor”), and called the word governed the maʿmūl.
17. The verb governs its agent in rafʿ and its object in naṣb, and a preposition governs its noun in jarr.
18. In Arabic grammar government is the center of the whole syntax: every ending is explained by naming its governor, and where no word can be found to govern, the grammarians posited an abstract governor.
19. So the Arabic theory of the ʿāmil is a fuller development of the idea that the medieval Latin grammarians called regimen.
20. The Arabic grammarians distinguished two kinds of sentence by their first word.
21. A verbal sentence (jumla fiʿliyya) begins with a verb, and the verb governs the agent in rafʿ and the object in naṣb; the usual order is verb, agent, object.
22. As in Latin, the endings show the roles, so the order can change without changing the sense.
23. The Arabic names for verb, agent, and object (fiʿl, fāʿil, mafʿūl) are all formed from the root f-ʿ-l, “doing,” which the grammarians used as the model root for naming the patterns of words.
24. A nominal sentence (jumla ismiyya) begins with a noun and may contain no verb at all.
25. Its first noun is the topic (mubtadaʾ, “that with which one begins”), and its second is the comment (khabar, “information”); both are in rafʿ, and the topic is governed by the act of beginning itself, an abstract governor.
26. So in Arabic a sentence can be complete without a verb: the Arabic grammarians treated the nominal sentence as a kind of sentence in its own right, not as an omission, whereas the modists held a construction complete only with a subject and a verb.
27. In Arabic some particles govern like verbs, such as the particle inna (“indeed”), which puts the following noun into naṣb.
28. Other particles govern the imperfect verb, which, unlike the Latin verb, takes different endings according to what governs it.
29. So the list of governors is long and varied, and it includes particles that Latin grammar would class as adverbs or conjunctions, each ending explained in the same way, by naming the word that governs it.
30. Arabic has a definite article, al-, which the grammarians classed as a particle, whereas the Latin grammarians removed the article from the Greek list because Latin has none.
31. Arabic expresses possession by annexation (iḍāfa): the first noun is annexed to the second, which stands in jarr, and the first noun takes no article.
32. This construction resembles the Latin genitive, but it changes the form of the first noun as well as the second.
33. Arabic nouns have three numbers: singular, dual (for two), and plural, and the dual has its own endings; Latin has no dual, so Donatus had no reason to name it.
34. The sound plural adds an ending to the unchanged singular, and the broken plural changes the vowels and pattern within the root.
35. English has a few plurals of the second kind (“man,” “men”), but in Arabic they are very common, which shows again how much of Arabic grammar is worked out within the root.
36. Al-Zajjājī asked why the rules of grammar are as they are, and he distinguished three kinds of reason: educational reasons, which simply state the rule for the learner; analogical reasons, which explain a rule by its likeness to another; and theoretical reasons, which ask why the rule itself holds.
37. So the Arabic tradition, like the medieval Latin one, came to ask for the causes of the rules; al-Zajjājī’s theoretical reasons are the counterpart of the modists’ question, though sought in the internal logic of the language rather than in the modes of being.
38. An error in Arabic, whether a wrong ending, a wrong government, or a form that correct speech does not have, is called laḥn.
39. The word covers both the barbarism and the solecism, because an error in the ending of a word is at the same time an error in its construction.
40. The Arabic grammarians had a body of authors, as the Latin grammarians did: the Qurʾān, the pre-Islamic poets, and the speech of the Bedouin.
41. They weighed the same kinds of evidence as the Latin criteria of correctness, under names of their own, such as transmitted usage (samāʿ, “hearing”) and analogy (qiyās).
42. Of the traditions studied, the Arabic is in some ways the closest to the Greek and Latin art: it names parts of speech, describes case endings and their uses, has theories of government and agreement, and has a doctrine of the complete sentence and a standard of correctness drawn from authors and usage.
43. Whether early Arabic grammar was influenced by Greek logic or grammar is disputed, and the Arabic system is in any case original in its form.
44. It differs in placing government at the center of the syntax, in admitting complete sentences without a verb, and in its system of roots and patterns.
45. Compared with Pāṇini, it begins not from the roles in the action but from the governor of each ending, so the Sanskrit, the Arabic, and the Latin traditions found the same facts on the page but explained them from different starting points.

## XI.4 Hebrew Grammar (`xi.hebrew`)

1. Jewish scholars studied the text of the Hebrew Bible with great care from ancient times, but a systematic grammar of Hebrew appeared only in the tenth century, in the Islamic world, among scholars who applied the methods of Arabic grammar to Hebrew.
2. So Hebrew grammar developed later than the Greek, Sanskrit, and Arabic traditions, and under the influence of the Arabic one.
3. Its development ran from Saadia Gaon in Babylonia, through the scholars of Muslim Spain, to David Kimḥi in Provence about 1200; most of the early works were written in Arabic, and only later in Hebrew.
4. A text written only in consonants can be read in more than one way, so the Hebrew Bible needed a fixed tradition of how it was to be read.
5. Before the grammarians came the Masoretes (from masorah, “tradition”), who fixed the text of the Hebrew Bible as it was to be read and added small signs for the vowels (niqqud, “points”) and for the accents, which mark both the stress of each word and the divisions of the verse for chanting.
6. The Masoretic text became the grammarians’ standard, so the Masoretes recorded the pronunciation of a sacred text so that it could be read correctly, as the Alexandrian scholars did for Homer, and their marks made the grammar of the vowels possible.
7. Saadia Gaon wrote the first works of Hebrew grammar and lexicography: a list of Hebrew words arranged by their first and last letters, and a grammar of Hebrew written in Arabic.
8. Saadia applied to Hebrew the categories of Arabic grammar, including the three parts of speech, and compared Hebrew words with Aramaic and Arabic ones.
9. So the first Hebrew grammarian was also, in a sense, the first comparative grammarian of the Semitic languages, since he saw that Hebrew, Aramaic, and Arabic share many roots and forms.
10. The Hebrew grammarians took over the Arabic division of words into three parts: the noun (shem, “name”), the verb (poʿal, “action”), and the particle (millah).
11. Kimḥi’s grammar, like the Arabic ones, is arranged by these three parts, with the verb treated first and at greatest length.
12. The choice follows the model of Arabic grammar, but it also fits Hebrew, in which the adjective is formed and inflected like a noun and the prepositions are often joined to the following word as prefixes.
13. Some forms of a Hebrew verb show all the letters of its root and some only one or two, so the Hebrew grammarians had to decide how many letters a root has.
14. Menahem ben Saruq held that many Hebrew roots consist of one or two letters, because in many forms only one or two letters appear, and Dunash ben Labrat criticized many of his analyses.
15. The dispute was settled by Judah Ḥayyūj, who showed that Hebrew roots, like Arabic roots, consist regularly of three consonants.
16. The letters that seem to disappear in some forms are weak letters (such as w, y, and n), which drop out or change by rule, so the root is still three letters.
17. Ḥayyūj wrote two treatises in Arabic on the verbs with weak letters and on the verbs whose second and third letters are the same.
18. He established that every Hebrew verb has a root of three consonants, and he explained the apparently irregular forms by rules of how the weak letters behave.
19. His principle made a systematic grammar of the Hebrew verb possible, because it allowed every form to be traced to a regular root.
20. It is the Hebrew counterpart of the Arabic doctrine of the root and of the Sanskrit derivation from roots, although in Hebrew, as in Arabic, the root is a set of consonants into which vowels are inserted.
21. Jonah ibn Janāḥ built on Ḥayyūj’s work the most complete Hebrew grammar of the Middle Ages, written in Arabic, in two parts: a grammar and a dictionary of roots.
22. He drew freely on Arabic grammar, compared Hebrew with Arabic throughout, and also treated the syntax of Hebrew and the figures of the biblical text, which the Latin grammarians called figures of construction.
23. So his work covers much of the ground that Donatus and Priscian covered for Latin, and it shows how much the Hebrew grammarians learned from Arabic grammar while describing a different language.
24. The Hebrew verb is formed from its root in several patterns, which modern grammars call binyanim (“buildings”), each with its own meaning.
25. The patterns express voice and kinds of action: active and passive, intensive, causative, and reflexive.
26. Following the Arabic practice of naming patterns by a model root, the Hebrew grammarians named the patterns by the root p-ʿ-l, “do”: paʿal (also called qal, “light”), nifʿal, piʿel, hifʿil, and the rest.
27. The Hebrew patterns do the work of voice and of some of Donatus’s forms of the verb, such as the frequentative, within a single system.
28. Hebrew has a definite article, ha-, joined to the front of the noun.
29. Like Arabic, Hebrew expresses possession by joining two nouns, the first in a shortened form called the construct state (semikhut, “leaning”).
30. The first noun loses its article and often changes its vowels, and the second noun carries the article for the whole phrase.
31. Hebrew nouns have no case endings, so the construct state does the work that the Latin genitive does by an ending; Arabic marks annexation by changing both nouns, Hebrew by changing only the first.
32. So the Semitic traditions share a construction that the Latin grammarians had no name for, and the Hebrew grammarians described it in terms taken from Arabic.
33. Most early Hebrew grammars were written in Arabic, but David Kimḥi wrote in Hebrew, for Jewish readers in Christian Europe who did not know Arabic.
34. His Mikhlol (“Compendium”) has two parts, a grammar and a dictionary of roots, following the plan of Ibn Janāḥ, and he also wrote commentaries on the Bible that apply his grammar to the explanation of the text.
35. Kimḥi’s grammar was clear, complete, and written in Hebrew, and it became the standard grammar of Hebrew for three centuries.
36. In the Renaissance, Christian scholars began to study Hebrew in order to read the Old Testament in its original language, using grammars and dictionaries drawn from Kimḥi.
37. Through such works the Hebrew grammar of the medieval Jewish scholars, itself shaped by Arabic grammar, entered the European tradition, bringing a grammar organized by roots and patterns and by three parts of speech, quite different from Donatus’s.
38. The translators of the Bible in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries learned their Hebrew from grammars in this tradition.
39. The Hebrew tradition differs from the others in that it was formed largely by applying the methods of Arabic grammar to a related language.
40. Its achievements were the doctrine of the three-letter root, the systematic description of the verb by its patterns, and the dictionaries of roots that made the Hebrew Bible accessible to readers who did not speak Hebrew.
41. Compared with the Latin art, the Hebrew grammar starts from the root rather than the word, counts three parts of speech rather than eight, and describes possession by the construct state rather than by a case.
42. Like the Latin art, however, it served the reading of a classical text, and its precepts were drawn from that text and tested against it.

## XI.5 The Chinese Study of the Character (`xi.chinese`)

1. The Chinese tradition called the studies that must come before the reading of the classics xiǎoxué (“the lesser learning”).
2. It had three branches: the study of the written character, the study of its sound, and the study of its meaning in the old texts.
3. There was no list of parts of speech and no account of case or conjugation, because classical Chinese has no inflection: its words do not change their form.
4. So the Chinese tradition differs most from ours: our art begins with the voice and the letter, whereas the Chinese art begins with the written character, which is often a whole word, and studies its form, its sound, and its meaning.
5. Its aim, like ours, was to make the classical texts readable, but the language required a different art.
6. The basic unit of written Chinese is the character, a written sign that usually stands for a single syllable and a single word or meaningful part of a word.
7. So the Chinese “letter” already has a meaning, as many of the Sumerian signs did, and meaning can begin at a single written sign.
8. About AD 100 Xu Shen compiled the Shuowen jiezi, which explains about nine thousand characters, arranged under 540 components called radicals, according to the part of each character that indicates its meaning.
9. So the order of that dictionary is itself an analysis of the characters, and it remained the foundation of the study of the character for the rest of Chinese history.
10. Some Chinese characters are pictures of what they signify, but most are not, and Xu Shen describes six ways in which characters are formed, called the six writings (liùshū).
11. They are pictographs, which picture a thing; simple indicatives, which point to an idea by a mark; compound ideographs, which join two characters to make a meaning; phono-semantic compounds, which join a part for the meaning with a part for the sound; derivative cognates, a class whose meaning is disputed; and phonetic loans, characters borrowed for another word of similar sound.
12. Most Chinese characters are phono-semantic compounds.
13. So the six writings are the Chinese tradition’s account of the parts of the written word, as our figure and species are an account of how words are made.
14. A pictograph may seem to signify by nature, because it looks like what it signifies, but the character signifies the word, not only the shape, and the word itself was imposed by the speakers of the language.
15. The loan characters show this most clearly: a character made for one word is borrowed for another word that sounds alike, so that it signifies by convention alone.
16. By recognizing the phonetic loans and the phono-semantic compounds, the Chinese tradition recognized that the character signifies the spoken word, so its account of writing agrees with Aristotle’s, that written marks are signs of spoken words, though it gives the written sign a much larger place.
17. The oldest Chinese work on the words of old texts is the Erya, which explains old words by more familiar ones, arranged in groups by meaning.
18. This practice of explaining the words of an old text by those of the present was called xùngǔ (“glossing”).
19. Glossing was the Chinese counterpart of explaining (ēnarrātiō), the second task of the grammarian, and the classics were the texts on which it was exercised, as the poets were for the Greek and Latin grammarians.
20. So the Chinese tradition, like ours, joined the study of the language to the reading of a body of authors.
21. Because the character does not show its sound fully, the Chinese scholars developed the method called fǎnqiè, which spells the sound of a syllable by two other characters.
22. The first character gives the initial consonant, and the second gives the rest of the syllable, its vowel, its ending, and its tone.
23. The Qieyun (“Divided Rhymes”), completed in 601, arranged characters by their tone and rhyme and gave their sounds by fǎnqiè, and its successors became the standard references for pronunciation and for the rhymes of poetry.
24. The Latin syllable is divided by its vowel and consonants, whereas fǎnqiè divides the Chinese syllable into initial and remainder, a division suited to a language of single-syllable words with tones.
25. Chinese is a tonal language: the pitch of the voice on a syllable distinguishes words that are otherwise alike.
26. The Chinese of the Qieyun had four tone classes, level, rising, departing and entering (píng, shǎng, qù, rù), and the dictionary was arranged by them.
27. The rhyme dictionaries classified every character by its tone, and the tones governed the rules of classical poetry.
28. The Greek accent was also a matter of pitch and could distinguish words, but in Chinese pitch distinguishes far more words, because every syllable has a tone and many words are a single syllable.
29. So the Chinese study of sound gave tone the central place that the Latin study of sound gave to quantity.
30. The nearest the traditional Chinese scholars came to parts of speech was the distinction between full words (shízì), which name a thing or an action, and empty words (xūzì), which do not name but have a function in the sentence.
31. The distinction resembles the medieval division between categorematic and syncategorematic words more than Donatus’s eight parts.
32. The empty words do the work that Latin does by endings and by many of its conjunctions and particles: they mark the relations between the full words, ask a question, or close a sentence.
33. Treatises devoted to the empty words were among the most important works of Chinese grammatical study.
34. In a line of classical Chinese the construction is shown by the order of the characters and by the empty words, not by endings.
35. Our art would call some empty words pronouns and others conjunctions, but the Chinese tradition called all of them empty words and explained each by its use in the line, and the commentators glossed such words in their place.
36. Because classical Chinese has no inflection, its construction is shown almost entirely by the order of the words and by the empty words: the one who acts stands first, the action second, and the thing acted on last.
37. Time is shown, when it is shown, by a word of time, not by a form of the verb.
38. English, which has lost most of its endings, relies on order to show its construction, and Chinese is the extreme case: the relations that Latin shows by case and conjugation are shown by the place of each word.
39. The traditional Chinese marks of punctuation, which readers added to the classics, divided the text into its units (a completed stretch and a pause), as the marks of pause did in Latin.
40. The traditional Chinese scholars made no list of parts of speech; the first to make one was Ma Jianzhong, a scholar trained in European grammar, in 1898.
41. His grammar of classical Chinese divided Chinese words into nine parts of speech and described subjects, objects, and kinds of sentence, using the categories of Latin grammar adapted to Chinese.
42. Ma’s grammar was the beginning of modern Chinese grammatical study, but later Chinese grammarians criticized him for imposing Latin categories on Chinese, and they sought categories drawn from Chinese itself.
43. So it raised the question whether it is right to describe a language in categories made for another, that is, whether the parts of speech and the relations of construction are the same in all languages.
44. The Chinese tradition began from the written character, and its three branches studied the form, the sound, and the meaning of the character.
45. Because Chinese has no inflection, it had no study of the accidents of the word, and it showed construction by order and by the empty words.
46. The rhyme dictionaries fixed the correct sound of each character, and glossing was its ēnarrātiō, the explaining of the classics.
47. Its division of full and empty words resembles the medieval division between categorematic and syncategorematic words more than Donatus’s eight parts.
48. Each of the six traditions arose to keep and understand a classical language and its texts: Sumerian for the Babylonian scribes, Middle Egyptian for the Egyptian scribes, who copied it but left no treatise of grammar, the Veda for the Indian grammarians, the Qurʾān for the Arabic, the Bible for the Hebrew, and the classics for the Chinese.
49. Each began from what was most evident in its language and writing: the bilingual match, the sign and its determinative, the root and affix, the governor of the ending, the three-letter root, and the written character.
50. The traditions agree in some things: all distinguish words that name things from words that relate them; most distinguish the noun from the verb; and several connect the verb with becoming or action.
51. They differ in the number of the parts of speech, in where they place meaning, and in how they explain construction.
52. Whether these agreements show a grammar common to all languages, and whether the differences are only of accidents, as Roger Bacon held, is a question that we considered in Section VIII, with universal grammar and typology.
53. Since the late Middle Ages, language has been studied in three principal ways: the classical art, philology, and modern linguistics.
54. The twentieth century found things that the thirteenth did not: phonology, a clear account of the structure of words, the grouping of words into constituents, the comparison of hundreds of languages and the discovery of their tendencies, and a serious study of how children acquire a language.
55. Saussure’s description of a language as a system was a real discovery, and so was Greenberg’s method of comparison.
56. The thirteenth century had something that modern linguistics mostly set aside: the question why the categories of grammar are these categories, and the refusal to treat that question as unanswerable.
57. Grammar is the art of speaking and writing correctly and of understanding what others have spoken and written.
58. That grammar is in an important sense one in all languages is still a claim on which both medieval thinkers and many modern linguists agree, although it is not accepted by everyone, and modern linguistics has certainly both challenged this claim with new evidence and also given new evidence in favor of it.
59. The classical art as such does stand by its central insights: that words are signs of what the mind understands, and that the mind understands things.
60. Because these are true, it is possible to learn another language, to translate, and to read the authors of other ages, and these are the reasons that the tradition gave for studying grammar first among the liberal arts.
